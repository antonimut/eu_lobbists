The steel industry welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to secure the sustainable 
access to critical raw materials. This is necessary to support the EU ambition to be the first 
climate neutral continent, to lead the digital decade and to be sure that the future of industry 
is made in Europe. The steel industry, as a backbone of the EU economy, can also significantly 
contribute to meeting the challenging Green deal targets, while delivering technologies and  
solutions to transform EU into a competitive, low-carbon economy. In particular, the transition 
to a low carbon and circular economy will make secondary raw materials increas ingly 
important. We thus believe the time has come to include ferrous scrap, an essential secondary 
raw material, in the list of CRMs.  
Furthermore, the list of critical raw materials needs to be extended to additional primary raw 
materials in order to prevent most likely future shortages that can have significant impact on 
key EU industries and thus affect the EU economy and its priority sectors.   
Materials that should be added to the list of CRMs given their criticality to the European steel 
industry include ferrous scrap, nickel, manganese. 
 
Primary Raw Materials 
 
➢ Nickel 
 
Today, nickel demand is predominantly driven by the stainless-steel industry, which 
accounts for around 70% of total primary nickel consumption  and, as already 
acknowledged in previous CRM reports, the EU import reliance for refined nickel was 
around 80%. However, the dynamics of the nickel market are foreseen to rapidly change 
in the future as  nickel is a primary component of lithium -ion batteries, which have 
become the technology for core automotive and energy storage system applications. Only 
within the automotive sector, the nickel demand is expected to increase by 2.6 Mt Ni in 
2040, up from only 92kt Ni in 20201.

➢ Manganese 
 
Around 90% of the manganese ore mining worldwide goes to producing steel in the form 
of ferroalloys (Ferro -silico-manganese and Ferro -manganese). Manganese alloys are 
relevant for all steel production routes, but the production of these alloying elements 
mostly takes place outside the EU. It is ther efore of utmost importance to ensure their 
availability to the steel industry and this should be reflected in the revision of the CRM 
Act. In fact, as already noted in previous CRM reports, “there are currently no satisfactory 
substitutes for manganese in its major applications (i.e. iron and steel)”.3 
 
In 2021, more than 95% of manganese alloys production was concentrated outside the 
EU27; 60% of which is concentrated in China, 14% in India, 3% in Russia and 3% in Ukraine, 
the latter being the main EU import source of manganese alloys – 27% of the total - since 
2018. In this respect, it is worth noting that the company owning the two biggest ferro -
alloy producers in Ukraine announced the suspension of production as of November 2022 
because of the difficulties in securing energy supplies in the midst of the war. 
 
Manganese demand is expected to increase because of it is increasingly used in the 
battery sector. According to and S&P Global article, manganese demand is expected to 
rise from approximately 34,000 mt in 2020 to nearly 360,000 mt in 20304.

Ferrous scrap as an important CRM  
 
➢ The European Commission recognises the steel industry as a priority sector for 
transitioning to a circular and climate -neutral European economy: recycling of ferrous 
scrap in the EU plays and will play in the future a fundamental role not only in 
decarbonisation, but when recycled during the steel making it also serves as a supply of 
iron and  some CRM (e.g. vanadium, tungsten, yttrium, niobium) for numerous steel 
grades used in key industry sectors providing high tech products and emerging 
innovations. In order to support the recycling and reutilisation of the above -mentioned 
CRMs within the EU and fully exploit the circularity  of ferrous scrap as a  valuable 
secondary raw material, the European steel industry therefore recommends that ferrous 
scrap becomes a CRM as well. 
 
Using more ferrous scrap in steel production in integrated route up to 30% (Blast Furnace 
and Basic Oxygen Furnace, BF/BOF) saves another CRM - coking coal. This saving is even 
more significant in steel production through scrap-based route using electric arc furnace 
(EAF) that can utilise up to 100% of scrap.  
 
➢ Stainless steel scrap, a particular family of ferrous scrap, is a significant source of nickel 
and chromium when recycled during the stainless-steel production.  
Secondary sources of nickel , in particular,  are a key feedstock for the EU stainless steel 
industry and  play a key role in minimizing the demand from the st ainless sector  for 
primary nickel, thus freeing up primary nickel resources for other first-use sectors. 
 
Contribution to Green deal targets 
 
➢ The European Steel Industry is committed to reducing CO2 emissions by 2030 by 30% 
compared to 2018 (which equates to 55% compared to 1990) towards carbon neutrality 
by 2050, if the right framework conditions are put in place  at EU level. The recycling of 
ferrous scrap is one of the clearest examples of the possibility to combine the main goals 
that the EU has set for the coming decades: strategic autonomy, circular economy, 
decarbonisation, energy saving.  For every tonne of carbon steel scrap recycled, a saving 
of 1,5 tonnes of CO2 and of the consumption of 1,4 tonnes of iron ore, 740 kg of coal and 
120 kg of limestone is achieved. In case of stainless-steel scrap about 5 tonnes of CO2 per 
tonne stainless steel can be saved.  The amount of saved CRM depends on scrap 
quantitative but also qualitative parameters which are in hands of scrap treating industry 
preparing the scrap prior to recycling in steelmaking technology. Specific quality of scrap 
will be needed to utilize this secondary raw material in order to partially supplement CRM

Sustainability of ferrous scrap 
 
➢ Future ferrous scrap availability therefore plays a fundamental role in sustainable steel 
production in the EU. According to projection of scrap availability and scrap consumption 
in future based on technological transformation of the European steel industry (provided 
by both independent Mission Possible Partnership study  and EUROFER study based on 
own calculations and on Navigant 2019 assessment for low -CO2 steel roadmap) to meet 
challenging Green deal targets, it is visible, that there will not sufficient amount or ferrous 
scrap available to cover  the production needs.  We expect that demand for CRM will 
increase accordingly to transformation of our industry.

The installed capacity of electric arc furnace and the share of production through scrap -

scrap might exceed supply already before 2030. 
 
➢ The increasing strategic role of ferrous scrap is further confirmed at international level  
by the fact that a large number of non -EU countries have put in place export restrictions 
on scrap, such as, for example: total ban on exports (e.g. Kazakhstan, Argentina, UAE); 
licensing system (e.g. South Africa, Indonesia, Tajikistan); export taxes (e.g. Russia, China,

➢ On the contrary exports of EU scrap hav e been on the rise for several years, increasing 
by  113% in 2021 compared to 2015 levels leading to a worrying paradoxical situation: the 
EU is increasing exports of a secondary raw material which is key for decarbonisation and 
as valuable source of CRM, at a moment when EU scrap needs are also increasing precisely 
for meeting the EU climate targets. Instead of ensuring the EU’s strategic autonomy, we 
risk to have by 2030 a scrap shortage which would jeopardize our decarbonisation and 
eventually lead to a new/extended dependency on CRM from third countries. The EU 
can not afford to be caught unprepared, as unfortunately happened with other strategic 
supply of energy or materials. 
   
Conclusion 
 
The revision of CRM act is an unique opportunity for the Commission to reduce dependency on 
critical raw materials through the inclusion of additional key primary raw materials in the CRM 
list, such as nickel and manganese and also the recognition of the criticality of secondary raw 
materials such as ferrous scrap in the EU. The availability of ferrous scrap is a vital issue for our 
industry for the coming decades and fits very well with all the priorities that are currently high 
on of EU agenda: EU strategic autonomy and resilience, Green Deal, decarboni sation, energy 
saving and circular and sustainable use of resources.  As secondary raw materials can provide just

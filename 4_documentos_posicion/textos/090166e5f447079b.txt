ACEA VIEWS ON THE CRITICAL RAW 
MATERIALS ACT 
 
GENERAL REMARKS  
ACEA welcomes the announced initiative of the European Commission for a European Critical 
Raw Materials Act (CRMA).  
The COVID-19 pandemic, together with the semiconductor crisis and Russia’s invasion of 
Ukraine evidenced some inherent weaknesses of global supply chains and the EU’s extreme 
dependency on third countries for certain key materials.  
From ACEA’s perspective, the CRMA initiative is therefore timely and essential to enable the 
necessary conditions to ensure a smooth and resilient green and digital transition while at the 
same time strengthening the open strategic autonomy  of key EU industries, such as the 
automotive sector and its battery value supply chain.  
The European automobile industry is fully committed to  the goal of decarbonising road 
transport. With the ban o n sales of internal combustion engine for passenger cars and vans 
set for 2035 and new CO2 reduction targets for the heavy-duty vehicles due to be set in the 
near future, the need to ensure a smooth change towards the green transition has never been 
more evident. Currently, almost 40% of new car sales in the EU are  electrified1, and this is 
expected to increase further in the next years. In the electrified commercial vehicle segment, 
medium-duty electric trucks for city distribution and waste  collection, and electric buses for 
public transportation have been in serial production since 2019 . Likewise, 2022 saw the first 
series production of electric heavy-duty vehicles. The nature of the market in all segments will 
be completely transformed over the next decade.  
E-mobility is clearly the driving force of transport decarboni sation and, in particular, batteries 
will be the dominant technology to ach ieve this. Nevertheless, the key materials contained in 
the batteries for electrified vehicles are largely of non -EU origin, making domestic industry 
reliant on other countries and external factors for their sourcing. These supply chains are 
vulnerable to external shocks, to dominance by a very small number of third countries and to 
supply/demand imbalances that create significant price volatility. In order to reduce these risk 
dependencies and advance the transition to e-mobility, it is essential that a European battery 
value chain is developed. To achieve this, security of access to critical raw materials must be 
enhanced

It is equally important to stress that -in addition to e-mobility- raw materials have also important 
applications for other aspects in automobiles.  
ACEA calls the European Commission to center its strategy for raw materials  around the 
following principles:  
1. Enhance - through industrial policy  measures - domestic capacity to extract, refine 
and process critical raw material s as identified in the Commission’s critical raw 
materials list. 
2. Improve the security of supply and diversity of sources of raw materials through the 
legal frameworks of FTAs and raw material partnerships. 
3. Strengthen the role of circular economy to increase the availability of secondary raw 
materials.  
4. Ensure that access to critical raw materials -whether sourced domestically, externally 
or through recycling methods - respect international standards and adheres to 
sustainable practices that fully respect human rights and the environment. 
5. Identify strategic R&I projects in the field of recycling, recovery and substitution of 
critical raw materials  ensuring their  access to funding, while supporting the 
development and implementations of innovations throughout the supply chains. 
 
As underlined earlier , the CRMA initiative is of key importance in the context of the whole 
transformation pathway of the sector  and will impact the entire automotive value chain, 
covering the biggest OEMs to the smallest SMEs. In this regard, ACEA - together with other 
associations in the automotive sector - has already drawn up some recommendations2. 
 
SPECIFIC REMARKS 
Access to critical raw materials is essential for the  set-up and proper functioning of the  
European battery value chain and  grid management in the EU  (as well as the renewable 
energy use in the energy sector) – this is valid both to support the transition towards e-mobility, 
as well as for new or second/re -used batteries for the energy sector transformation. The EU 
should clearly focus on  ensuring resilience throughout the battery value chain, through 
diversifying domestic and international raw material supply sources and recycling (including 
supportive measures for investments  in domestic supply ). The fact that most of the raw 
materials are insufficiently available on the European market reinforces the need of a trade 
and industrial policy on the basis of both openness and strategic autonomy.

On the basis of a preliminary analysis, ACEA has identified - in a non-exhaustive manner- that 
the following critical raw materials are of u tmost importance to the green transition of our 
sector:    
• Lithium, nickel, manganese, cobalt and graphite, used in the production of lithium -ion 
batteries. 
• Iridium, platinum, tantalum, cobalt and nickel , for the production of hydrogen. Rare 
earths such as dysprosium, neodymium and praseodymium, which are essential to the 
production of permanent magnets in the traction motors for electric vehicles.  
Access to these materials will therefore have to be significantly enhanced in order to ensure 
the green transition of the automobile industry.  
 
1. Domestic capacity and industrial policy  
 
In order for the automobile sector to deliver the targets set under the EU’s climate policy with 
regards to the reduction of greenhouse emissions, the CRMA will have to strongly rely on the 
industrial policy of the EU in its implementation.  
The European Commission should make sure that the CRMA initiative is in line with key 
priorities of the EU  on strengthening its industrial base, ensuring its strategic auton omy, 
enhancing manufacturing investment and creating favourable framework conditions to support 
the global competitiveness of the European industry. However, the move towards strategic 
autonomy should also balance the needs of a European economy that remains open to trade. 
From that perspective, ACEA sees high value in having an open discussion with all relevant 
stakeholders on the raw material needs throughout the entire automotive value chain to identify 
existing gaps and analyse potential solutions.  
 
2. External dimension 
 
As there exists geological limitations to the availability of certain raw materials in the EU, 
imports will continue to play a key role in the future in the EU’s transition to a green and digital 
economy.  
In order to ensure security of supply, the EU must avoid  to e xcessively concentrate the 
sourcing of raw materials from  only a few third countries. The recent invasion of Ukraine by 
Russia exposed the  EU’s vulnerability in this respect . S everal industries across Europe -
including the automobile - were severely affected due to shortages in key components and 
critical raw materials for some months. 
The EU’s action in the trade policy field is therefore key and should complement the CRM A

• Ensuring that FTAs concluded with resource-rich countries  are ratified as soon as 
possible after negotiations are finalised. Currently, agreements with Mexico, Mercosur 
-which were concluded over three years ago- and Chile are still waiting to be submitted 
for ratification.  
• Finalising the FTA negotiations with Australia – one of the world’s largest producers of 
lithium and rare earths – and speeding up negotiations with Indonesia is also extremely 
important. 
• Exploring options on how to enhance the provisions of the energy and raw materials 
chapters already contained in EU FTAs to ensure that EU companies  are able to 
maximise their benefits.   
• Establishing strategic partnerships with resource-rich countries beyond the framework 
of FTAs to deepen cooperation in the field of critical raw materials  including R&D, 
mining and exploration, processing etc.  
• Ensuring coherence between the raw materials act and trade policy in relation to issues 
such as rules of origin for batteries and for recycled materials in the batteries used in 
EVs that would facilitate the export of those vehicles under EU FTAs.  
 
3. Circular economy and recycling  
 
Given the heavy dependency on imports, ensuring that critical raw materials are used to their 
full potential is essential. European companies across the automotive value chain are already 
applying the principles of circula rity in their business models. Vehicle manufacturers are 
already using recycled materials despite numerous technical limitations and considering that 
vehicles are very long-lasting products with an extremely complex supply chain. 
  
However recycled materia ls do not necessarily represent the best  option to reduce 
environmental impacts over the vehicle’s whole life cycle. For the reuse of critical raw materials 
to be effective on a large scale, a number of key preconditions have to be in place: 
• Recycled material quality has to be guaranteed. Recycled materials can only be used 
if there is guarantee that they have the same properties as the virgin material. 
• The availability of recycled material has to be ensured.  
 
While recycling and re -using critical raw materials are an attractive alternative to import 
dependency, some measures currently being discussed might result in problematic 
bottlenecks for the automotive transition towards cleaner mobility solutions. For example, the 
EU is trying to safeguard the reuse of recycled material through  provisions such as the ones 
contained in Article 8 of the Battery Regulation. However, setting mandatory recycled content 
targets should be done with guarantees on the quality , availability or cost of this recycled 
material in order to avoid disrupting the plans for the e-mobility transition.

The CRMA should therefore focus on: 
• Encouraging and strengthening the European recycling sector and promoting 
investment in technologies that will yield quality recycled material. 
• Monitoring the availability of the materials -including the recycled ones - taking into 
account the growing needs of many industrial sectors such as the automotive. 
The automotive industry already has systems in place today that may be used for the tracking 
of critical raw materials contained in vehicles. These systems, tools and processes are well 
established throughout our full global supply chain and may only require minor modifications 
and improvements. In addition, also other upcoming legislation such as the Battery Regulation 
is elaborating on tools to collect and provide the relevant information to and within the 
automotive value chain (e.g. Battery Passport). 
In order to avoid unnecessary administrative burden  further existing or planned regulations  
have to be taken into account as well. For example, waste policy should only be discussed in 
context with other policy areas such as climate and energy policy.  Moreover, any measure 
must be introduced in a harmonised –standardised– way across the EU. 
For this reason, ACEA welcomes the idea of looking into recycling raw materials but warns 
against a one-size-fits-all approach which may lead to more expensive and harder to produce 
batteries in the future.  
 
4. Standardisation, social, environmental and other issues  
 
There are number of issues that the CRMA has to tackle with respect to the standardisation5 
as well as voluntary standards /best practices promoting responsible behavior in mining, 
processing and use of critical raw materials. This is valid also for a number of social  and 
environmental items that are related to the extraction of the raw materials (not only the critical 
ones). In particular, ACEA calls on the European Commission to assess the suitability of: 
• IRMA (Home – IRMA – The Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance) for mining as 
the recommended/adopted industry standard to ensure a level-playing field. 
• RMI Environmental, Social & Governance (ESG) and/or RMI All Minerals (Responsible 
Minerals Initiative Releases ESG Standard for Mineral Supply Chains ) for processing 
companies as the recommended/adopted industry standard. 
• RBA ( https://www.responsiblebusiness.org/) for all companies as the 
recommended/adopted industry standard on Code of Conduct. 
Likewise, it is also important that decision makers promote access to raw materials with net -
zero carbon footprint  and promote  investment in strategic R&I projects focused on new 
materials and technologies to substitute critical raw materials while reducing the demand (e.g., 
increasing recycle and recovery rates of CRM)

NEXT STEPS AND RECOMMENDATIONS: 
 
ACEA and its members welcome the CRMA initiative and will provide full support to the 
European Commission in deeper and further work on this file. The automobile sector would 
like to suggest the following recommendations: 
1. The European Commission -in co -operation with industry stakeholders - should 
continue its work on the update of the critical raw materials list and  associated future 
policy measures.  The screening and updat ing of the list should be sector -based as 
different industrial sectors have different raw materials needs  and varying levels of 
urgency in terms of their green transition. This review process should be initiated as 
soon as possible to ensure that it feeds all the legislative work that will follow in the 
coming months.  
2. E-mobility is the driving force in the decarboni sation of the road transport. Therefore, 
all critical raw materials for the European battery value chain and EV application must 
be assessed and included in the list and reviewed periodically.  
3. A thorough impact assessment is critical ahead of the adoption of any policy measure 
taken and it must include all relevant parts of the e-mobility value chain.  
4. Industry asks for a concrete and rapid action plan to be associated with the proposal 
of the CRMA to provide clear guidance and predictability for the industry. 
5. With respect to the issue of stock piling, there are number of good practices across the 
globe that third parties are  currently performing and practicing with respect to the 
emergency reserves of raw materials. ACEA calls on the European Commission to 
balance advantages and disadvantages of these systems.

• 12.7 million Europeans work in the auto industry (directly and 
indirectly), accounting for 6.6% of all EU jobs

governments across key European markets

for the European Union

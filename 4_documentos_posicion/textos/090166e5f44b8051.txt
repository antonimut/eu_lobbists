Statement  
 
European Critical Raw Materials Act 
 
The EU has announced a Critical Raw Materials Act to identify strategic projects along the 
entire value chain and build strategic reserves where supply is at risk. The Critical Raw Mate-
rials Act provides for various complementary measures. 
 
Positions: 
• Establishing a list of critical raw materials is the right way to initiate or modify focused 
supply security measures. The list of critical raw materials must be reviewed and 
revised at regular intervals to take account of the current supply situation. 
• However, the list of critical raw materials must not lead to an exclusive focus on (pri-
mary) raw materials that are important for certain core applications of future technolo-
gies. Here, too, the entire value chains must be taken into account. Only through a 
sustainable steel industry can the important technologies of the future be put into prac-
tice in a climate-friendly way.  
• From the point of view of the steel industry, the following primary raw materials or their 
supply situation are currently classified as critical: Nickel, ferro- silicon-manganese, 
chromium and agglomerated iron ores.  
• For the transformation and for the expansion of the circular economy, the importance 
of secondary raw materials must also be taken into account. Steel scrap in particular 
is predestined for inclusion in the list of critical raw materials due to its properties 
as well as emerging scarcity trends.  
• The measures derived by the EU to establish or strengthen security of supply are lar-
gely to be endorsed.  
 
Background 
The European Critical Raw Materials Act is a planned component of the Green Deal; an action 
plan has already been presented in 2020. The green and digital transformation is only possible 
on the basis of a sustainable basic materials industry, which in turn is based on a secure supply 
of raw materials. This is particularly true of the European steel industry, which is not only very 
raw material-intensive but also heavily dependent on imports of raw materials. At the same 
time, the steel industry is already a key pillar of the circular economy. Recycling steel massively 
reduces production-related CO2 emissions and the use of primary raw materials, while at the 
same time steel scrap serves as an important source of othe r raw materials (e.g. nickel and 
chromium) that would otherwise be produced primarily.

Content of the Critical Raw Materials Act 
The Critical Raw Materials Act, which has been planned for some time, focuses on maintaining 
sustainable supply security. The pandemic has made it clear that the resilience of supply and 
value chains in Europe needs to be strengthened to a much greater extent than has been the 
case to date. As a result of the war of aggression on Ukraine, there is also a need to reduce 
dependence on raw materials and, in particular, fossil fuels of Russian origin. In the medium 
term, the high dependence on China, especially for rare earths, is seen as a high risk for resi-
lience and security in Europe.  
The EU has therefore announced a Critical Raw Materi als Act to identify strategic projects 
along the entire value chain and build up strategic reserves where supply is at risk. This inclu-
des the following measures:Re-establishing the list of critical raw materials that are of strategic 
importance to the European economy as a key element of the proposed legislation  
> Improving the monitoring of risk management  
> Strengthening the value chain of critical raw materials in the EU (mining, refining, pro-
cessing, recycling),  
> Expansion of the circular economy 
> Strengthening the EU's external trade 
> Acceleration of research and innovation as well as qualification of skilled workers  
Evaluation 
The path taken by the EU to increase the resilience of the European supply and value chain 
points in the right direction. In par ticular, the fact that the planned legislation contains a very 
broad package of measures which are mutually influential or as mutually supportive as pos-
sible is to be welcomed. The EU strategy is therefore welcomed by the steel industry, although 
clarification or correction of the planned measures is required on a number of points.  
Inclusion of all processing stages: 
WV Stahl welcomes the fact that the Commission's consultation on "Critical Raw Materials 
Projects" explicitly includes not only the explorati on and extraction stages but also refining, 
processing and recycling activities in the scope of the definition. The expansion of refining 
capacities is of particular importance, as China currently holds a dominant position in this sec-
tor. 
Orientation to scarcity criteria and relevance for the economy and transformation: 
With the list of critical raw materials, the EU chooses a risk -based approach. It thus focuses 
on a small number of raw materials. However, this also means that only a fraction of all steel-
relevant raw materials will be part of the list. The criticality of raw materials should be made on 
the basis of the following criteria: supply concentration, demand substitution and projected 
supply gaps, which in the case of exploration are based on geoscientific data. In the second 
step, the economic importance of these raw materials must be measured by whether and to 
what extent the downstream value chains are affected, thus posing a high risk of damage to 
the manufacturing sector as a whole. A further , additional criterion could be "transformation 
criticality", i.e. the extent to which raw material availability impairs completed transformation 
projects. Prices, on the other hand, should not be used as a criterion, as these tend to express 
the short- to medium-term market structure. It is also important that the list is reviewed at re-
gular intervals to reflect the current supply and demand situation.

Inclusion of steel-specific primary raw materials: 
In the selection of primary raw materials that should be part of the list of critical raw materials, 
the following raw materials should be considered from the current perspective of the steel in-
dustry. Nickel, ferro -silicon-manganese, chromium and agglomerated iron ores. On the one 
hand, these raw materials are characterized by scarcity tendencies (high country concentration 
of nickel, ferro -silicon-manganese and chromium); at the same time, they play an important 
role in decarbonization. In the case of agglomerated iron ores in particular, it is important to 
take into account not only the original raw material extraction but also the processing stages, 
as stipulated in the EU Critical Raw Materials Act. The extraction of iron ore reserves and 
production is sufficient in terms of capacity. For the transformation of the blast furnace-oxygen 
steel route (EU: 55%, D: 70%), the use of DRI will be necessary in the future; for this purpose, 
so-called agglomerated iron ore ("pellets") will be required; however, the plant capacities re-
quired for this are currently not available to a sufficient extent worldwide. 
 
Inclusion of steel-specific secondary raw materials: 
Since the expansion of the circular economy is also an important part of the package of mea-
sures of the EU Critical Raw Materials Act and this goes hand in hand with the efforts to protect 
the climate and transform the steel industry, logic dictates that secondary raw materials must 
also be included in the list of critical raw materials. Steel scrap is already one of the key raw 
materials for steel production. In the EU, 45 percent (D: 30%). of crude steel is produced exclu-
sively on the basis of steel scrap in electric arc furnaces. Steel scrap thus already makes an 
important contribution to decarbonization. However, the share of scrap-based steel production 
will increase in the EU as well as worldwide. At the same time, additional demand for steel 
scrap on the existing blast furnace oxygen steel route is also expected in a transition phase, 
as electric arc furnaces will also be used here. This could lead to steel scrap demand excee-
ding steel scrap supply. The existing scarcity trends as well as the decarbonization properties 
are thus clear criteria for the inclusion of steel scrap in the list of critical raw materials.   
 
Early risk identification: 
The expansion of a risk warning system in order to be able to identify bottlenecks in (critical) 
raw materials at an early stage is to be welcomed from the point of view of WV Stahl. In Ger-
many, DERA, which was established a few years ago, provides a wide range of data. DERA 
should therefore be expanded nationally in terms of its personnel and financial resources. In-
sofar as similar institutions exist in other EU countries, the exchange between these institutions 
should be intensified. Likewise, an EU -wide mandate of DERA, as a central authority for 
geoscientific exploration and consulting, would also be conceivable. 
 
Financial support for self-reliant diversification: 
The proposed law is based on a self-responsible approach; member states themselves do not 
take an active entrepreneurial role. The company-specific possibility of raw material diversifi-
cation is to be supported, and financial support measures are often the decisive lever here. 
Consequently, financial resources, national and European development banks or their pro-
grams, subsidies, etc., which serve to support critical raw material projects, should be expan-
ded. This will enable companies to participate directly in critical raw material projects. An im-
portant instrument in this context is the so -called untied financial loans. With the guarantees 
for untied financial loans (UFK guarantees), loans from banks based in Germany (and under 
certain conditions also from foreign banks) for raw material projects are secured against eco-
nomic and political default risks. 
 
 
Recycling economy must be expanded: 
WV Stahl welcomes the planned expansion of recycling and the circular economy as well as 
the creation of corresponding framework conditions, also in environmental and licensing law.

It is also important that both research and measures for professional qualification and further 
training are expanded in such a way that the necessary progress can be made, particularly in 
the field of processing and sorting methods. In this context, it must be criticized that the con-
sultation does not pay any attention to digitalization. Digitalization in particular is seen as a 
major driver of processing and sorting technology in the recycling sector. 
 
Trade instruments are an important pillar of raw materials policy: 
From the point of view of the steel industry, the accelerated conclusion of trade and raw ma-
terial agreements and partnerships in the area of raw material value chains with developing 
countries should be seen as a further important measure. An additional pillar of these agree-
ments can be the development of infrastructure and qualification measures on site. These 
agreements would also have positive effects on those raw materials that are not included in 
the list of critical raw materials. New trade agreements are therefore to be welcomed. These 
are all the more important because the number of trade barriers and export restrictions is in-
creasing worldwide. 
 
Strengthening decentralized, self-reliant stockpiling: 
The program also provides for the expansion or strengthening of stockholding. Since measures 
such as commodity agreements only take effect in the medium to long term, the targeted build-
up of reserves of critical raw materials is a sensible addition. However, a decentralized ap-
proach, i.e. company-specific warehousing, is also important here. In the case of stockpiling, 
the capital employed and tied up is regularly the decisive factor. As a result, government in-
centive instruments such as special write-offs on raw material reserves, government guaran-
tees for inventory loans or subsidies for purpose-driven joint ventures would be the appropriate 
instruments to strengthen an inventory. 
 
No redundant legislative provisions: 
However, the planned law should in no way be about harmonizing environmental, social and  
governance criteria and standards. This is regulated by the planned European supply chain 
law CSDD, and a duplication of regulations must be avoided. However, the drafts of the CSDD 
stand in the way of the desired resilience, as the corporate due diligence obligations are not 
designed pragmatically and the one- sided shifting of risks tends to lead to retreat ("cut and 
run") instead of empowerment ("stay and behave").

CRITICAL RAW MATERIALS ACT 
Companion paper to ECI Response to Open 
Public Consultation 
November 2022  
 
ECI welcomes the initiative of the EU Commission for a Critical Raw Materials package with 
legislative and non-legislative measures. This paper wants to summarise our views about which 
essential elements the package should address and to  give the rationale of and expand on our 
responses to the open public consultation questionnaire. 
In particular, ECI wants to stress the following crucial elements: 
▪ The package should be broad in scope and consider all the raw materials that are 
strategic for reaching the goal of the  transition to clean energy and climate neutrality in 
the EU, such as copper ; not just those classified as critical by the EU metho dology on 
criticality. 
▪ The package should foresee measures to grant easier and more predictable permitting 
procedures to those projects involving strategic raw materials in the different steps of the 
supply chain: mining, smelting and refining, and recycling. 
▪ The package should ensure the competitiveness of the EU industry  via the supply of 
green electricity and energy at competitive prices compared to other regions. 
▪ The EU legislative framework on waste should be adapted to ensure the availability and 
quality of secondary raw materials  for the European operators and should improve its 
legislative provisions to allow a wider collection of end-of-life products and complex waste 
streams and more efficient recovery of their content of strategic raw materials. 
▪ The new framework should guarantee a harmonised approach with existing and 
upcoming legislative files and that there are no contradicting provisions targeting strategic 
raw materials needed for the EU’s twin transition (e.g., ban of Lead). 
▪ Strategic raw material value -chains, including mining, refining and recycling, should be 
eligible in the context of sustainable finance, by development of ambitious but operational

1. Introduction 
The European Copper Institute (ECI) welcomes the intention of the European Commission to release a 
package on Raw Materials. The preservation, strengthening and possible extension of the different raw 
materials supply chains are an essential element for a resilient and sustainable EU economy and 
society. It is undoubtful that there will be a massive increase in the demand for raw materials, and, many 
of those materials will be essential for ensuring the essential  conditions – e.g., electrification, green 
technologies, and renewables – for reaching the targets fixed by the EU.  In particular, the package 
should foster a multiple set of elements, all of which are aimed at improving and reinforcing the open 
strategic autonomy of the European Economic Area ; for instance : EU availability of primary and 
secondary raw materials; improving quality of secondary raw materials; securing effective and 
sustainable international strategic partnership on raw materials; setting con ditioning for securing an 
industrial presence in the EU and fostering investments; introducing easier permitting process for mining 
and industrial sites producing and processing key raw materials. 
This paper has two objectives: (1) to present aspects relevant for the members of the ECI, which should 
be part of the package on Critical Raw Materials; (2) to complement and expand on ECI’s options 
selected in the questionnaire released for the open public consultation.  
2. Relevant elements of the package for the European Copper Institute 
Scope of the legislative Act on Critical Raw Materials 
During the last years, the EU and different stakeholders widely used to term ‘critical’ with different 
connotations. For some, the term critical means a material that needs to be substituted, while for others 
it means a material that is necessary for the EU economy because it’s part of many key supply chains. 
In addition, others understand the term as  a material that is or will be in short supply. For instance, the 
IEA Critical Minerals policy tracker1 stresses the importance of ensuring supply reliability and resiliency, 
irrespectively of the fact that different regions or countries can classify the m inerals as critical or 
strategic. It is not easy to have a clear definition, but it is also understandable that policymakers – EU 
and Member States – want to have a definition to have a clear scope of the future legislative act.  
However, the definition actually available at the EU level and given by the methodology to assess the 
criticality of the raw materials developed by the Joint Research Centre is not fit for this package and  
should not be used for its foundation. 
This EU criticality methodology: 
▪ utilises past data to derive an assessment of how much a raw material is critical, and 
▪ does not consider future needs of investments  in the supply chains that will experience the 
largest increments of raw materials demand. 
Moreover, the past revisions of the EU list of critical raw materials were carried out more like a screening 
exercise rather than a tool to help the strategic planning of the EU  open and strategic autonomy . For 
instance, coking coal and rubber are still on the list, but it is questionable how much they are linked to 
future investments in green technologies. Finally, it is important to consider that although the EU 
methodology on criticality does not flag a mineral, this does not mean that the material is not strateg ic 
for the EU and that its availability and quality cannot be subjected to bottlenecks or disruption in the 
short and medium term. Thus, ECI wants to propose to define the scope of the legislative  action based 
on the raw materials that will be necessary for the supply and value chains, relevant to the digital and

The relevance of copper by-products and recycling 
Copper mining , processing, and recycling produce multiple benefits beyond making refined copper 
available for the EU market.  The production of copper , from primary ores,  also generates other non -
ferrous metals that, depending on the ore type and deposit characteristics, can be gold, silver, zinc, lead 
and molybdenum, among others. 
Moreover, copper can be produced via a secondary production route that mainly uses secondary  raw 
materials, such as Waste Electric and Electronic Equipment (WEEE), Printed Computer Boards (PCBs), 
industrial residues containing copper and Incinerated Bottom Ashes (IBAs) containing copper. The 
peculiarity of copper metallurgy is to allow the recove ry and then recycling of other non -ferrous metals 
that are components of the waste stream. Figure 1  shows the industrial eco -system of non -ferrous 
metals, where the ability to recover base and technology metals from natural resources, residues and 
end-of-life products is visualised . In particular, copper metallurgy can recover a wide range of non -
ferrous metals , most of which are classified as critical by the EU and  send them to other industrial 
sectors.

Therefore, the  legislative act on critical raw materials should also contain provisions to strengthen 
industrial symbiosis and recycling opportunities , because they will be an essential ingredient to secure 
the EU’s open strategic autonomy on raw materials. Moreover, setting an effective legislative framework 
on by-products and recycling now will allow recovering and recycling of materials contained by the first 
generation of clean energy technologies that will reach their end-of-life in meaningful volumes  in the

Therefore, the release of the  new critical raw materials act must  prioritise changes in the EU product 
and waste legislative acquis to unleash its full potential in delivering recycling and industrial symbiosis 
opportunities.

Copper’s role in the green transition and future trends 
The recently published KU Leuven study “ Metals for Clean Energy: Pathways to solving Europe’s raw 
materials challenge ”5 showed the essentiality of metals to ensure the tr ansition towards EU’s clean 
energy and climate objectives. The study also demonstrates the essential role of copper  that is  
ubiquitously present in all the value chains that are key for the energy and digital transition: Figure 2.  It 
is also relevant to stress that metals essential for the ‘clean energy transition’ will have in the next decade 
a much stronger demand at global and regional level. For instance, according to several studies and 
reports6,7,8, the demand for copper is expected to massively increase both at the global level and at the 
EU regional level; for instance, in Figure 3, the forecast of the total global demand for copper and the 
additional copper demand in the EU linked to energy transition is shown. 
Although every forecast and every study have its own degree of uncertainty and should not be taken for 
granted in exact quantitative terms, it is also evident that the trend is clear and will develop in such a 
direction, making copper an essential element for reaching the EU goals about the energy transition. 
Therefore, the new legislative act on critical raw materials must be a pivotal instrument to put projects 
on raw materials forward and to ensure certainty and predictability through the following actio ns: 
(a) the identification of strategic projects to secure raw materials supply to the copper industry;  
(b) the definition of a clear and easier framework for permitting and financing the new projects;  
(c) the preservation of the competitiveness of the copper industry operating in the EU within the future

Therefore, the EU Critical Raw Material Acts should contain provisions t o materials the following 
concrete actions. 
▪ Projects belonging to the entire copper value chain should be considered as potential 
strategic projects – mining, smelting and refining, and recycling – in the EU and abroad.  
▪ Easier permitting procedures and financing eligibility should be granted when the project 
will fulfil responsible production via assurance frameworks and sustainability principles; 
for instance, projects aiming at being EU Taxonomy aligned and thus aiming at superior 
performance should have a fast-track procedure. 
▪ EU and Member States’ finance tools should be accessible to projects on strategic raw 
materials. Their importance should be placed on equal footing with projects on electricity 
grids that profited from flexible State Aid instruments. Moreo ver, European banks on 
Investments and  Reconstruction and Developments should financially support de -
risking strategic projects. 
▪ The development of new projects along the copper supply chain needs long -term 
certainty, to ensure their competitiveness during  quite long business cycles. In such a 
context, copper production in the EU needs a secure supply of fossil -free energy at 
competitive prices, compared to other regions , as this is a crucial pre -requisite for 
decarbonising copper production . In the current  situation of sustained, high electricity 
prices, European producers are put to a competitive disadvantage vis-à-vis producers in 
other regions where electricity prices remain lower and the unpredictability of prices is 
impacting on the ability to plan investments. Structural changes are needed to electricity 
market design to address the high energy costs and to bring more certainty.

3. Comments and extensive rationale of the ECI to the questionnaire 
This part of the document wants to complement and explain, where necessary, the options selected by 
ECI in the questionnaire of the open public consultation. 
General Challenges 
The options proposed by the questionnaire have been selected assuming in the response that the term 
critical raw material is assumed to be equivalent to strategic raw material . More precisely, the scope of 
the new act should allow potential investments into the supply chains of the raw mater ials that are 
enabler of the green and digital transition. The term strategic here is used not to create a new classes 
of materials, different from the critical ones, but to enlarge the scope enough to consider also raw 
materials that will be, for instance, in strong demand in this and in the next decade, element that the EU 
concept of criticality does not consider. Therefore, the options were chosen without any reference to the 
EU methodology on criticality or to the EU list of critical raw materials. 
Current EU Policies 
Option (a). The list of critical raw materials is not the correct instrument to be used to secure all the 
strategic materials that are and will be relevant for the digital and green energy transition. Other tools 
linked to the future needs of the EU or the reference to international reports seem more adapted to the 
scope. 
Options (c) and (d). The open strategic autonomy of the EU on raw materials cannot be achieved only 
via domestic supply. Although the domestic supply of primary and secondary resources is of paramount 
importance, this is also true for supply from third countries. 
Supply Chain Vulnerability 
- Monitoring 
Options (a), (b), and (c). The neutral option was selected because only a company can give a specific 
reply to the proposed challenges.

- EU value chain 
Options (a) and (b). the option ‘agree’ was chosen for both challenges linked to access to funds, not 
because of large and systemic past or present difficulties in financing projects. The option was selected 
from the perspective that future larger demand of metals will increase the need for rolling out more 
strategic projects. Therefore, the actual instruments seem not adequate anymor e and a different 
approach should be taken by policymakers, member states and banking authority.  
- Circularity 
Option (a). The use of secondary raw materials in products depends on the material properties and 
product type. Therefore, the option ‘neutral’ was selected. For instance, incentivising the use of 
secondary raw materials for plastic production makes sense. Plastic re cycling performance was 
extremely low, and its properties are not fit for high -quality recycling (i.e., preserving the material's 
inherent properties); therefore, its secondary raw materials market needs legislative intervention. On the 
contrary, using secondary raw materials by the copper industry does not need any intervention because 
the copper input is maximised in each production step in the supply chain. Moreover, certain routes, 
such as secondary copper production, seek additional secondary input mat erials to sustain their 
business models and increase their output. 
Option (b). The use of waste legislation, and in particular the implementation of the Waste Framework 
Directive, showed substantial flaws in how the recycling targets in the member states w ere assessed. 
For instance, large flexibility was given in selecting the calculation point for recycled waste. Thus many 
EU member states assumed the collection point as the point where to calculate the tonnage of recycled 
waste without taking into account the yields and losses due to sorting, treatment and final transformation 
into new materials or products. The Waste Framework Directive's revised text should be implemented 
so that the reported recycled tonnages will be referred to the final recycling, whe re the material 
recovered from waste will enter a recycling facility, such as a copper production installation.  
Option (c). It is essential to reach a common and harmonised understanding of classifying waste for 
internal EU shipment , to avoid unnecessary b ureaucratic burden for companies and to prevent 
shipments from being classified as illegal just for different views between the dispatch and consignee 
country.  
Option (d). Export of waste should occur only when makes sense in sustainability terms. For instance, 
the export of waste like WEEE from the EU should occur only when the environmental and safety 
standard at the country of destinations are equivalent to or better than the ones in the EU. Waste export 
should be an instrument, following the principles of the Basel Convention, for which the best possible 
fate of a waste stream should be found in environmental and safety terms. Therefore, the EU should 
never impose an export ban, but should be vigilant in controlling export flows. 
Possible measures to ensure a secure and sustainable supply of critical raw materials 
- Strengthening the monitoring, risk management and governance of critical raw materials supply 
chain in Europe 
Option (b). The role of raw materials in the future of the EU will continuously increase . Thus, it would 
be a strategic decision to create an EU agency or body that will supervise the EU -wide governance 
capacity tasked with monitoring actions and strengthening the EU value chain. 
Option (f). The option ‘neutral’ was selected about the possibility of having provisions to increase the 
stockpiling of the raw materials because the proposed measure equally refers to the EU, national or 
industry level. However, the proposed alternatives are not equ ivalent, and the burden can differ. For 
instance, imposing stockpiling at the company level can have huge economic impacts because it can 
immobilise large capitals that cannot be used for companies’ operations. Therefore, the measure can

- Strengthening the critical raw materials supply chain in Europe 
o supply chain (To what extent do you agree that the following policy options could help 
strengthen the EU critical raw materials value chain?) 
ECI supported all the policy options proposed for strengthening the EU supply chains of critical raw 
materials. In particular, the option (c), (e), (f), and (g) are extremely important for making the EU critical 
raw materials act an effective and efficient instrument towards securing the supply of raw materials EU 
will need. The options (a), (b), and (d) are supported as well, but however , it is essential to do not to 
restrict the list to very few raw materials, to avoid the use of EU criticality methodolog y and to consider 
a specific list of raw materials able to cover the key supply chains of the green energy transition.  
- Sustainability 
o Substitution 
Substitution cannot be forced by the member states or imposed by legislation because, in most cases, 
it could happen to fall into ‘regrettable substitution’ cases. Market players are best placed to decide on 
the instances where substitution is technically , economically viable, and desirable from different 
sustainability considerations. Therefore, no one of the op tions presented in the questionnaire, such as 
strengthened cooperation across Member States, innovation funds and research on substitution, and 
public-private cooperation. These are not worth options because the market should be flexible in 
selecting the m ost sustainable and economically viable substitutions, and the research should not be 
forced to look for substitutions. 
- Circularity 
Options (a) and (b).  Waste operators collect, sort, and treat end -of-life products and large waste 
streams to prepare them for being shipped to the final processors or recyclers. For instance, waste 
operators collect end-of-life products, like old computers, and recover circuit boards that will be sent to, 
e.g. secondary copper production installations. Therefore, it is essential to impose on them recovery 
targets that should not refer to specific materials but to the waste streams and end -of-life products that 
usually contain these critical raw materials. 
Options (e) and (f). The use of the minimum recycled content for critical raw materials is not a measure 
that can be equally applied to all materials, irrespective of their specific properties, fundamentals of the 
market for secondary raw materials, and the environmental benefits associated with the recycling. In 
addition, design requirements for having products lasting longer are already under consideration in all 
different strands of the EU products policy. Design requiremen ts for durability, re -usability and 
remanufacturing are under development in the delegated acts on Sustainable Products Regulations. 
Then, the two options cannot be supported.

About the European Copper Institute  
Based in Brussels, the European Copper Institute (ECI) is the leading advocate for the copper industry in Europe and is 
the European arm of the International Copper Association (ICA). Through a team of policy, industry and scientific experts, 
ECI acts to support copper’s role in achieving the EU’s policy goals. Our members mine, smelt, refine and recycle copper 
for use across the economy, in the electricity system, buildings, transport and industry.

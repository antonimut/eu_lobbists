The EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act  
 
SUMMARY  
  
Ragn-Sells welcomes the Commission's timely initiative of a European Critical Raw Materials 
Act and agrees with the objectives stated in its call for evidence , to support the green and 
digital transition and strengthen EU resilience by securing a sustainable supply of critical raw 
materials. We especially agree with the identified need for a definition of priorities and 
objectives for EU actions (objective numbe r one) and strengthening the EU’s critical raw 
materials value chain (objective number three).  
 
It is positive that the Commission highlights the need for future EU legislation on the recovery 
of critical raw materials from waste. We do, however, consider the focus of materials that are 
essential for “components of green technologies, used in digital application, and are crucial to 
the defence, aerospace, and health industries”, too limited. If the Commission is serious about 
putting forward new legislation on critical raw materials, we find it important that it takes all the 
benefits that come with extracting resources from waste into consideration. 
 
The current fertiliser supply crisis is a result of the shortage of raw materials , such as 
phosphorus, potassium, and nitrogen. We are facing a shortage in nitrogen fertilisers due 
to the current energy crisis (natural gas price and supply crisis), as well as phosphate and 
potassium due to disruptions in international supply chains that impact the EU’s access. This 
leads to high food prices and reduces the union’s food security.  By including the food and 
fertiliser sector, as well as adding nitrogen and potassium to the list of critical raw 
materials, the EU could strengthen its value chains of key fertilisers. 
 
In addition to important actions to reduce the overall demand for critical raw materials, there 
are two ways to reduce  third-party dependence on critical raw materials. Either by increased 
mining and recycling within the EU, or reduced demand by substitution, i.e., changing design 
and processes. In our opinion , it is important to address both supply and demand of critical 
raw materials . The demand side could be addressed through research and development  
efforts, aiming to find new substitutes or new ways of producing goods and services without 
the use of  critical raw materials . One key challenge to succeeding with new recycling 
processes is reaching an economy of scale. One way to address this challenge is to use  
strategic stockpiling of waste containing critical raw materials. Over time a manmade deposit 
of critical raw materials of sufficient scale would enable the investments needed for a viable 
recovery of  critical raw materials . This opportunity is lost under the current EU waste  
framework, where low-value use of waste such as aggregates (currently not considered a 
critical raw material  in the EU) in road construction is preferred to storage (I.e., controlled 
landfill) enabling future extraction of critical raw materials. 
 
We request a recycling -first approach. Ragn-Sells technology could produce 30% of the 
EU’s annual targeted production of magnesium, minimising the need for new mining of critical 
raw materials and strengthening the EU’s autonomy. Recycling raw materials from waste 
streams are, however, not sufficie ntly facilitated or incentivised in current EU legislation. 
Materials originating from waste are met with market barriers or other administrative obstacles. 
This results in a lack of investments at a time when these investments are more crucial than 
ever. 
 
We strongly believe that our innovative technology could help the EU strengthen its supply of 
critical raw materials and, by doing so, ease the dependency on raw materials from third 
countries. Future actions in the critical raw material action plan shoul d therefore aim at the 
recovery and recycling of nutrients with high-quality and efficient recovery processes,

ABOUT RAGN-SELLS AND OUR SOLUTIONS 
The environmental company Ragn-Sells converts waste into raw materials that can be used 
over and over again. Ragn-Sells drives the transition to a circular economy through solutions 
that reduce its own and other actors' environmental and climate impact. Ragn -Sells wants to 
be living proof that caring for the earth and business go hand in hand. 
 
EasyMining, part of the Ragn -Sells group, is passionate about inventing new technologies 
that use intelligent chemical solutions to close nutrient cycles and detoxify waste flows. Our 
objective is to improve existing or even to create new circular material flows efficiently and 
commercially viable. EasyMining has developed and patented several chemical processes and 
holds great know-how in chemistry, resource efficiency, circular business models and industrial 
symbiosis. EasyMining provides three solutions on how to make use of resources from waste 
and wastewater. Our technologies (Ash2Phos, Ash2Salt and Aqua2N) produce clean 
commercial fertilisers in a circular economy.  
 
The RS-OSA Service OÜ  is a Ragn-Sells subsidiary in Estonia, focusing on oil shale ash 
valorisation (the OSA project). The project is a collaboration between Ragn-Sells and scientists 
in Estonia, which has led to an innovative and patented technology to produce precipitated 
calcium carbonate (PCC) from oil shale ash. There are currently 600 million tons of deposited 
oil shale ash in Eastern Estonia, ready to be recycled. Beyond the benefits of climate-positive 
production, the technology makes the extraction of additional critical materials from the oil 
shale ash possible, such as magnesium, silica, and aluminium. These materials could then 
be used in fertilisers or as raw materials for everyday products. 
 
PHOSPHORUS 
The transition to a circular economy is crucial in building a sustainable society. Today, almost 
half of our climate impact and 90% of water scarcity issues are linked to the way we extract 
resources and produce goods and food. In this context, phosphorus (P) is key. Without it, 
global agricultural output would be cut in half.  The EU’s fertiliser and animal food industries, 
as well as other industry sectors using phosphorus compounds, are highly reliant on imports 
of phosphate rock (or materials derived from it, e.g. phosphoric acid), making geopolitical 
instability a real threa t to the EU’s supply of phosphate. Today, the EU is largely (92%) 
dependent on imports as most mines are located outside Europe (incl. Russia). Because of 
the limited availability of this scarce resource, the large ecological footprint from mining, 
geopolitical instability in the source countries, long transport and health issues from cadmium 
contamination, safe recycling of P needs to be a top priority with clear goals and actions.  
 
Wastewater from households and industries contains massive amounts of P b ut is today 
considered a problem rather than an asset. Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) put great 
efforts into discarding sludge even though it is rich in P.  P can be recovered from sludge and 
brought back into the loop, securing an endless supply. This means enormous opportunities 
for Europe. A changed EU approach, and more efficient use of resources, is key to increasing 
Europe´s self-sufficiency in phosphate. The WWTPs of today will be the resource plants of 
tomorrow. This scenario is well within reach. However, it requires key alterations in legislation. 
 
POTASSIUM 
Potassium (or potash) is currently under consideration for possible inclusion in the EU’s list 
of critical raw materials. Potassium, together with phosphate rock (which is classified as a 
critical raw material) are both critical for agriculture and food security. We do, howeve r, note

NITROGEN 
Wastewater from households and industries also contains massive amounts of nitrogen (N). 
But today, this is a problem rather than an asset. WWTPs put great efforts into discarding 
sludge even though it is rich in both P and N. N is then released from the p lants as N 20, a 
greenhouse gas with a huge climate impact. N could, however, be captured from wastewater 
streams and used to produce fertiliser, replacing today’s greenhouse gas -heavy production, 
which is dependent on natural gas supply.  
  
The Commission is developing an Integrated Nutrient Management Action Plan (INMAP), to 
meet the Green Deal target to reduce nutrient losses by 50%. The proposed revision of the EU 
Wastewater Treatment Directive presented on the 26 th of October also aims to red uce 
phosphorus and nitrogen losses and increase recycling. Nitrogen emissions to air (N2O, NOx, 
ammonia) are critical greenhouse and air quality challenges (National Emissions Ceilings 
Directive). We underline the  strong synergy between EU energy, food and  Critical Raw 
Material resilience and reducing environmental and greenhouse impacts,  possible 
through improving nutrient use efficiency and increasing nutrient recycling. 
 
MAGNESIUM 
Ragn-Sells technology makes it possible to extract materials such as  magnesium, which is 
defined as a critical raw material in the European Union, from oil shale ash. The EU uses huge 
amounts of magnesium every year (155 000 tonnes per year) and 93% of the union’s supply 
is imported from China. The EU aims to increase domest ic annual production to 100  000 
tonnes by 2030. Ragn -Sells OSA-project has the potential to account for 30 % of the EU’s 
annual targeted production, minimising the need for new mining of critical raw materials and 
strengthening the EU’s autonomy. 
 
PRECIPITATED CALCIUM CARBONATE 
Ragn-Sells and scientists in Estonia have also developed an innovative and patented 
technology to produce precipitated calcium carbonate (PCC)  from oil shale ash and CO2 
– two large waste streams. PCC made from oil shale ash can then be used to produce new 
products (flooring, paints, and other construction materials), reducing the demand for mining 
several virgin materials.  The technology itself is carbon negative since the CO2 used in the 
process will be captured instead of r eleased into the atmosphere, thus making the end -
product climate positive. Ragn-Sells planned plant in Estonia will be able to valorise up to one 
million tons of oil shale ash and 250 000 tonnes of CO2 in a year, resulting in around 500 000 
tonnes of ultra-pure calcium carbonate.  
 
We urge the Commission to, in its upcoming Critical Raw Materials Act, facilitate the 
recycling of raw materials from waste streams and reduce market barriers for materials 
originating from waste.

PROPOSALS  
Ragn-Sells notes that the Commission’s call for evidence on critical raw materials already 
underlines the need to improve resource efficiency and to support circularity, by facilitating 
regulation and investment in recycling. Ragn-Sells suggests that the Critical Raw Materials Act 
should include these needs, and further: 
• Introduce recycling obligations, as proposed in the call for evidence, applicable both 
within the EU and internationally. The Commission should add potassium (or potash) 
and nitrogen, as well as other nutrients, to the list of critical raw materials and introduce 
fixed recycling targets. Such obligations are necessary to ensure a level playing field. 
• Address regulatory barriers to recycling nutrients derived from wastes and 
animal by-products, whilst ensuring safety: in particular, the use of Cat.1 ABP ash 
in fertilisers, clarification of legislation concerning recycling to animal feed, the waste 
status of algae, End-of-Waste for materials recovered from wastewater, integration of 
recycled nutrients into standardisation. The CEN SABE identified need1 for standards 
to support phosphorus recycling from wastewater should be implemented  and 
extended to the recycling of phosphorus and other nutrients from other secondary 
sources.  
• Facilitate permitting of recycling facilities  for critical raw materials, focusing on 
modification of permits of existing factories to allow the intake of waste materials (for 
recycling) and not only virgin materials. Permitting should be carried out by national 
authorities, but EU-level coordination and circulation o f information on best practices 
and examples could facilitate the process. 
• Include fiscal and market tools to monetise environmental and social impacts of 
the consumption of critical raw materials and support recovery and recycling , 
including e.g. via the EU Taxonomy for green investment funding, modulated VAT, 
ecotaxes, and support for recycled critical raw material products. If imported products 
containing critical raw materials are not subject to the same fiscality, then this must be 
compensated by import taxation or border compensation mechanisms.   
• Develop a flow of critical raw material data  collections, with regular update 
mechanisms, to support the identification of key flows offering the potential for 
improving use efficiency and for recycling, and make available to regulators, 
stakeholders, and industry. Customs codes and statistics definitions  should be 
adapted to ensure the identification of materials and products containing critical raw 
materials in a relevant form.  
• Support critical raw materials recycling and demonstration projects or first-mover 
full-scale installations. 
• Ensure that contaminants susceptible to inhibiting critical raw materials recycling are 
addressed in EU chemicals and pharmaceuticals policies, in particular by urgently and 
effectively restricting problematic industrial and consumer chemicals (e.g. 
PFAS).  
• Waste, or secondary raw material, is a key raw material for circular economies 
and needs to be treated as such by the waste and transport legislation.  When 
promoting a transition to a more circular economy, we need to ensure that secondary 
raw material, like ash from incinerated sewage sludge, containing a critical raw material 
can be sent across borders.  
• Focus on the quality of the raw material, rather than its origin. We need to ensure 
that the quality of the material always is the key regulatory factor in defining its use and 
tradability, rather than its origin. This is particularly relevant for trade, not least because, 
in many cases, the sourcing of waste needs to be do ne from many countries to make 
it economically viable compared to primary mining. Similarly, the trade of materials is 
essential for research and development purposes.

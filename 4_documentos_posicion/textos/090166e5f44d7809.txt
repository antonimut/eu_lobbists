Position Paper for the Critical Raw Materials Act Recycled CO2 as a climate-positive raw material November 24, 2022   Paebbl is a young, pan-European SME, whose aim is to develop and commercialise scalable mineralisation technology that can help the EU in its transition to climate neutrality. We are delighted to be given the opportunity to contribute to the work on the upcoming Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) and convey our message that recycling captured CO2 into climate-positive raw materials should be a core pillar of it.    Our operations are based in Sweden, Finland, and the Netherlands, with a diverse founder base with a strong track record in science, technology, engineering, successfully scaling up start-ups, and venture capital. Our vested interest is in the field of turning captured carbon dioxide (CO2) into industrial raw materials through a process known as mineralisation. The output products of the mineralisation process include carbonates, silica, nickel, and chromium. We see this vested interest as part of a broader climate-positive technological and industrial agenda, one that has a strong scientific backing and needs to be considered at a higher political and legislative level.  We recognise that the EU is facing a significant challenge of securing critical raw materials for the transition to climate neutrality. The supply of these raw materials is not only a matter of sovereignty, but also regional security. Simultaneously, the production or extraction of most, if not all, raw materials traditionally comes at an environmental and societal opportunity cost, and hence the EU’s mission to reach climate neutrality. It is therefore crucial to consider essential sustainability questions when provisioning the CRMA. When CO2 is mineralised, it is permanently sequestered, thereby contributing as a negative emissions technology (NET) to the EU’s race to CO2 neutrality. However, even more importantly, the mineralisation process also produces valuable metals, which can be recovered (such as Nickel and Chromium). Our US counterparts are already investing considerably into novel technologies to recover essential metals in sustainable ways.  The EU’s pursuit of a CRMA is a prime opportunity to rebuild the EU’s supply chain of raw materials with a sustainability-first approach, whilst ensuring a stronger supply base of metals required for the energy transition. By ensuring industrial and mining waste products such as steel slags and CO2 are seen as valuable raw materials in a broad range of value chains, the EU would not only ensure a more diversified supply of raw materials, but also have a much better chance of reaching its climate targets. We argue in particular that capturing CO2 and turning it to carbonates through a scientifically proven technological process of mineralisation can replace CO2-intense raw materials such as limestone in a number of industrial value chains. Further, through mineralisation of CO2 using silicate minerals, critical raw materials such as Nickel and Chromium can be extracted for the energy transition.

A growing number of stakeholders see the need to repurpose CO2 into industrial raw materials and chemicals, and are building innovative technologies to this end. The field of CCUS (carbon capture, utilisation, and storage) is booming with innovation both from SMEs and large corporates, where the former are usually starting from scratch, the latter are protecting vested interests. Value chains such as the paper industry also recognise the need to innovate and already see CO2 as a valuable raw material. NGOs such as CO2Value present a great example of a growing community of stakeholders interested in seeing CO2 as a raw material, with both SMEs and large corporations partaking in the debate.   We urge the European Commission to consider the possibility of recycling captured CO2 into climate-positive raw materials as a core pillar of the provision of raw materials. Not only should the EU see capturing of CO2 from the atmosphere and industrial flue gases as a worthwhile climate mitigation investment, but also a significant economic opportunity for the region as a whole. Captured CO2 can be turned into carbonates and silica through mineralisation, which can replace mining of the respective virgin materials. Trace metals such as Nickel and Chromium can in turn be extracted from the process, further supporting the EU’s dual raw materials supply and climate goals.   Paebbl fully supports the EU’s objective of reaching climate neutrality by 2050, and negative emissions thereafter. In fact, Paebbl was founded on the premise that more credible technological shots on goal are needed to enable the transition to net zero by 2050, and that the technologies that we commercialise must have a sound scientific foundation. The technology that we are building is based on a scientifically-backed study of mineralisation, with over 20 years of academic research from around the world working on this topic. One of the leading experts in the field of mineralisation – Mr. Pol Knops – is one of our co-founders and CTO. It is on the basis of Mr. Knops’ research that the Founding team has come together to form a business with commercial interests that are aligned with that of the planet.  We remain available to provide any additional information on our activities that can be useful in such a crucial debate over critical raw materials for the EU economy.       Paebbl represents the interests of its shareholders and stakeholders. Our shareholders are our founders, evergreen investment firms with a focus on climate-technology, and other climate-committed private investors, with aligned planetary and capital interests, and focused on long term goals. Our stakeholders include both public and private institutions involved in the novel field of Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) as well as buyers of novel materials made out of carbonates that are produced when CO2 is mineralised. Through mineralisation two goals are simultaneously achieved: CO2 removal and the production of new materials and metal extractions (such as Nickel and Chromium).

LKAB’s views on the Critical Raw Materials Act 
• LKAB welcomes the Commission’s initiative to address the obvious and urgent need to secure supply 
of raw materials required to develop digitalization and advanced technologies, to reach the 
objectives of the Green Deal. The need for metals and minerals also includes demands to accomplish 
a transformation of both the transport sector and of the energy sector, to maintain a strong defense 
system as well as to secure resilient value chains for European industries and for the future growth, 
security, and prosperity of the European society. The harsh reality is that none of this can be realized 
without a large influx of raw materials into the system, and especially of metals and minerals. Such 
metals and minerals are of critical and of strategical importance for the EU. The need to ensure the 
availability of metals and minerals is therefore paramount and cannot rely on import, trade and 
stockpiling but must also be secured by domestic production.  
 
• LKAB produces approximately 80% of the iron ore in Europe and has the potential to become the 
leading producer also of REE and phosphorus in the EU. Thus, being a leading mining company in 
Europe and the leading iron ore producer, LKAB contributes to this consultation with deep 
knowledge and experiences regarding what is needed to strengthen the EU domestic supply, all 
guided by sustainability principles.  
 
As an example, LKAB and the Swedish mining industry has committed to a biodiversity strategy 
implying that by 2030, LKAB and the Swedish mining and minerals industry will contribute to a 
biodiversity net gain in all regions where mining and minerals operations and exploration take place. 
 
• The potentially impacts on downstream users from supply deficits are exponential. A concrete 
example is the European car industry. To meet the policy decision that requires that all new vehicles 
sold from 2035 be zero emissions, the fleet of new vehicles will generally have to be equipped with 
electrical motors. Such motors will necessarily consist of some kilograms of REEs and with a 
noticeable higher amount of copper and other metals than in fossil fueled cars. Each kilogram of REE 
may not have such a high economic value, but in relation to the price of the cars that cannot be 
manufactured if there is a deficit of REEs the impact will be massive for both the European car 
industry and its subcontractors. The demand of metals globally will exceed the supply. We cannot 
rely only on trade agreements and there is an overwhelming risk that the European car production 
will have to move closer to where these raw materials are produced, outside the EU. And if the

LKAB’s most urgent requests for the Critical Raw Materials Act 
Supply of metals and minerals all starts in the mine. And mining starts with exploration. 
Changes are necessary. As part of securing supply of raw materials, the EU should set up objectives regarding 
EU domestic production of both critical and strategical metals and minerals. Without changes in the current 
legal and policy framework that acknowledge the need for more mining in Europe and that makes increased 
mining possible, we are convinced that the EU will not be able to meet the demand needed for the green 
transition.  
We ask for the following: 
1. Support and enhance the possibilities to undertake exploration work 
Exploration is the instrument to gain knowledge and collect geological data, to make it possible to find new 
mineralizations and to single out the ones that have the greatest geological potential. 
2.  Improved permitting conditions  
Think “raw materials first” in legislative and policy context, to introduce high awareness of the importance to 
secure raw materials supply from domestic production. 
At EU level, we see the need for:  
1. An emergency regulation to establish fast-track permitting. 
2. Possibility to balance opposing interests against each other by introducing mining projects as 
Important Reason of Overriding Public Interest (IROPI). 
3. Guidance on how the EU legislation should be interpreted with the aim of boosting permitting for 
projects with proven geological and technical feasibility but where conflicting interests related to land 
use/access to land are blocking projects. 
LKAB welcomes the proposal of an emergency regulation to establish fast-track permitting within RePower 
EU and pleads that the Commission will take a similar approach into account in the coming Raw Materials Act. 
Fast-track permitting should apply to specific projects that are identified as of strategic importa nce for the 
EU’s raw material supply. 
By providing a legal instrument regarding raw materials supply and mining, the EU can introduce a transparent 
way of balancing of opposing interests regarding access to land and land use by recognizing mining projects 
as IROPI, thus mak ing it possible  that in specific cases mining projects can be given precedence over  
competing legal requirements in water and nature protection legislation.  
In the same manner, it would be useful with more guidance on how the EU legislation should be interpreted  
and how derogations from water and nature protection objectives could be applied in specific cases to enable 
 
 
  
 
land use for mining projects. This could be proposed to be combined with requirements on offsetting and of 
creating biodiversity net gain. Currently, it is recognized that non-deterioration principles unconditionally can 
block important projects also when relatively small impact will occur, thus hindering potentially large gains on 
other climate and environmental aspects . A more holistic view at all levels (EU, national ly, regionally, and 
locally) on what sustainable land use actually means is necessary to really achieve sustainable development 
and clear guidance on EU level is necessary.  
In summary, introduction of a legal instrument such as a Raw Materials Act has the potential to substantially 
improve the landscape of permitting requirements for mining, by establishing legally binding ways to balance 
between opposing interests. The lack of efficient and transparent ways of doing this is what presently causes 
the greatest unpredictability and time-consuming parts of mining permitting processes.  
3. Both critical and strategic metals and minerals must be acknowledged in the Critical Raw Materials Act 
Experience shows that the understanding and development of which materials that should be seen as 
critical have resulted in a constantly growing list. Based on the forecasts on how the metals demand will 
increase vastly over the coming years and what we can assume regarding the supply, the number of raw 
materials on the list can be expected to increase even further, not least to the fact that other world regions 
and economies are strategically stepping up efforts to secure their own supply or to further increase their 
market-dominant position. In addition, we have learned that the global supply can take rapid and 
unexpected turns which both the last year’s pandemic and the Russian invasion of Ukraine are soundly 
reminders of. Therefore, it is crucial to not only focus on the now critical materials, but also on strategically 
important materials.  
Besides the predicted growing number of critical raw materials (CRMs) and intensified global competition, 
several of the raw materials on the critical list are geologically associated with other more common metals 
and minerals. This means that most CRMs are often linked to carrier metals such as iron, copper, zinc, or 
other commonly mined metals. Restricting any regulatory measures to only CRMs will clearly risk being 
counterproductive for the supply potential of them as both the geological and economic feasibility in most 
cases relies on extraction of a primary ore. 
To secure new and increased production of CRMs, the design of the CRM Act must build on the fact that the 
economic prerequisites often lie in the extraction of a primary ore and that CRMs typically will constitute by-
products. This is in fact an advantage both from a resource efficiency perspective and from a supply-security 
perspective since the extracting of CRMs as by-products thus will become be less sensitive to the volatile 
markets often associated with CRMs and less sensitive to market manipulation.  
  
 
 
  
 
4. Improved policy and regulatory coherences 
Considering the high ambitions of the EU to reach climate neutrality it is necessary to make sure that 
different overriding objectives in steering policies and regulations are aligned and not counterproductive to, 
or conflicting with, each other. As for the equation of the Green Deal, metals and minerals are proven 
essential to succeed. Or, as Ms. van der Leyen concluded in her 2022 State of the union speech: “whether we 
talk about chips for virtual reality or cells for solar panels, the twin transition will be fueled by raw materials. 
Lithium and rare earth are already replacing gas and oil at the heart of our economy”.  
Mineralizations cannot be relocated. They inevitably must be mined where they are located, if at all. This 
means that mining concessions and environmental permits for mining almost always ends up in conflicts 
with other land use or nature protection interests of which the latter often are possessing protection based 
on EU legislation.  
A Raw Materials Act can enable improved policy and regulatory coherence by raising the awareness of the 
importance of increased raw materials supply, and by establishing legally support for enhanced production 
of raw materials. 
LKAB trusts that the coming Raw Materials Act will introduce mining and production of raw materials as 
important common interests with a corresponding legal recognition. If the Raw Materials Act will not provide 
possibilities to legally recognize mining and production of critical and strategical raw materials as equally 
important as other interests with legally binding objectives on EU level, and with the potential in particular 
cases to override other such interests, LKAB see no other alternative than that the EU immediately will have 
to open up several other legislative acts for amendments in order to prioritize mining and raw materials 
production within the EU to fulfill the Green Deal. 
 
  
 
 
  
 
More about LKAB and how we work with critical raw materials 
Luossavaara-Kiirunavaara AB (LKAB) is an international high-tech mining and mineral group, with iron ore 
production in Sweden for the global steel market. More than 80% of the iron ore produced in Europe comes 
from LKAB’s three mines in the north of Sweden. Most of our mining takes place several hundred metres 
underground at the same locations where it all started for more than one hundred years ago. The mines in 
Kiirunavaara (Kiruna) and Malmberget (Gällivare) are the world’s two largest underground iron ore mines. 
LKAB, fully owned by the Swedish state, started its mining operations in 1890’s and with recent exploration 
results, we now plan for continued operation in our present mines beyond 2060.  
 
LKAB has a vision to lead the global transformation of the iron and steel industry to reduce the carbon 
footprint, and to set a new world standard for mining, by producing carbon-free sponge iron and by 
extracting critical minerals from the mine waste. LKAB’s fossil-free sponge iron produced from hydrogen 
with the HYBRIT technology will cut 40-50 million tons of CO2 emissions annually in Europe’s iron and steel 
value chain in the process. This is equal to Sweden’s entire annual CO2 emissions. To realize this strategy 
and transformation, LKAB plans to invest approximately 1-2 billion euros each year for a 20-year period.  
 
When it comes to make a difference, we talk from experience. Since the 1960s, LKAB has reduced its carbon 
footprint by 84%. Now, the time has come for the next shift. With clear environmental and climate 
commitments, LKAB applies a holistic corporate strategy towards zero CO2 emissions in all its processes 
and operations by 2045, at the latest.  
 
In the same spirit, LKAB aims to bring a similar shift to Europe’s strategic autonomy of CRM through its 
ReeMAP project, which will enable circular solutions for EU sourcing of phosphorus and rare earth elements 
(REEs). The ReeMAP project has by recent exploration results shown the potential to replace the Russian 
export of phosphor to Europe. It is the most promising project in terms of scale and time for creating a 
European production of REEs needed for the electrification and transformation away from a fossil society.   
 
  
 
 
  
 
LKAB’s ReeMAP project  
The functionality of the CRM Act demands acknowledgement of both critical and strategical raw materials  
It all starts in the mine. And mining  always starts with exploration. Without the current iron ore production, 
the ReeMAP project would not be economically feasible. Even though the large volumes of the project, t he 
content of phosphorus and rare earth metals in our ores would not justify primary mining of these. However, 
thanks to our strong business model we can create further utilization of large volumes of mined material. This 
clearly shows that LKAB’s iron ore production is strategically needed to enable production of phosphorus and 
REEs. Such a  combined production  also creates a less sensitive  business case since the CRM production 
becomes less vulnerable to the volatile markets often connected with CRMs, and to market manipulation. 
Thus, the coming CRM Act needs to acknowledge also strategic materials and make sure that the regulatory 
measures that are created are not restricted to only CRMs but also covers the strategically important metals 
and minerals and enables for combined production. 
The potential of LKAB’s ReeMAP project 
CRMs are crucial for our daily lives , from securing Europe’s food security to being vital components for the 
energy transition. Alarmingly, several CRMs are mainly imported from countries such as Russia or China. To 
reduce this dependency, i t is crucial that Europe promptly takes responsibility for the raw materials supply 
needed for the twin transition. Fortunately, Europe has significant domestic geological potential to source 
several of the CRMs and other raw materials needed.  
In the case of LKAB’s ReeMAP project, this at current estimates has the potential to  produce the following 
elements mentioned on the EU’s 2020 CRM list: 
• Phosphorus corresponding to 5 times Sweden’s demand (base case) 
• Rare Earth Elements  (REEs) initially corresponding to 30% of today’s imports of REE into the EU and 
important as the first and only sourcing in the EU. B ut still, it is just a small part of what is needed to 
produce permanent magnets to electrify the European automotive industry by 2035.   
• Fluorine products  (the gaseous form of fluorite/fluorspar) for the chemicals industry and medical 
applications  
 
As a bonus, the project will also generate Gypsum to meet demand from all building construction in Sweden.  
It is important to stress that the figures above are based on current estimates: They exclude the expansion 
potential shown in exploration results of the Per Geijer ore body, please see below. 
 
 
 
  
 
In the North of Sweden, the recently explored Per Geijer deposit close to the existing Kiruna mine contains 
up to eight times as much phosphorus compared to the orebodies currently mined by LKAB. With circular 
extraction of phosphorus and REEs as by-products from iron ore mining, LKAB can potentially replace all 
import of phosphorus to Europe from Russia.1 This will also entail expansion large opportunities regarding 
increased production of REEs. 
Mining the mine waste in a circular way  
Mining always brings other elements than the mined ore up to the surface, often ending up as waste that 
needs to be deposited. LKAB will make today’s waste to become tomorrow’s resources by to some extent, 
preventing useful and even critical materials from becoming waste. Finding ways to make products from 
waste streams from the iron ore production  and create byproducts instead is part of LKAB’s strategy from 
both a sustainability and profitability perspective. In the ReeMAP case, these byproducts are valuable and 
critical minerals such as phosphorus, REEs and fluorine.  
Unlocking ReeMAP’s potential, with a production of CRMs to start by 2027  from LKAB’s current mines , is 
based on the ambitious assumption that all permitting procedures will succeed without interference, which is 
unusual.  
ReeMAP has a true potential and with the right conditions the project can contribute to make the CRM Act a 
game changer for the European supply of certain raw materials. But to unlock the full potential to increase the 
CRM supply, it needs to be facilitated by stable and foreseeable conditions for both primary production and 
permitting of all stages. We are not asking to m ake the environmental standards lower, but to make the 
permitting processes more predictable.

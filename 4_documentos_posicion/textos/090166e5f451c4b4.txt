European Critical Raw Materials Act: An Opportunity for More Resilience and 
Circularity 
November 2022 
 
Technology Industries of Finland (TIF) warmly welcomes the European Commission’s 
planned adoption of a European Critical Raw Materials Act (henceforth ECRMA).  
 
Raw materials are indispensable to ensuring that the transition towards a green and digital 
European economy is successful. At the same time, they are also commodities that are at 
high risk of supply disruption. Unless the European Union can reduce demand for and, 
where indispensable, secure a reliable supply of raw materials in the long-term, the 
transformation of the European economy cannot and will not happen. In turn, consolidating 
the EU’s responsible use of and access to these materials will greatly strengthen the EU’s 
economic, environmental, and geopolitical resilience.  
 
In TIF’s view, the ECRMA should prioritise the following four policy ambitions: 
 
1. Adopt a Long-Term Perspective to Critical Raw Materials 
 
For the European Critical Raw Materials Act to succeed, it needs to be based on a robust 
foundation: an evidence-based and holistic EU list of Critical Raw Materials. TIF looks 
forward to the re-evaluation of the existing CRM list as part of the ECRMA initiative. It is 
vital for the Commission to re-examine and, where necessary, adjust the list.  
 
Crucially, however, any such revision must reflect both short-term supply risks and 
long-term supply needs facing the Union. In its present form, the CRM list fulfils the 
former function but not the latter. Therefore, TIF calls on the Commission to complement it 
by introducing complementary criteria for the identification of “Strategic Raw 
Materials”: these are substances that are to be considered essential to achieving the EU’s 
long-term goals under the digital and green transition. Metrics for identifying a “Strategic 
Raw Material” should include factors such as: 
 
➢ Strategic use 
➢ Economic importance 
➢ Long-term demand development 
➢ Global availability  
➢ Recyclability 
➢ Substitutability 
 
Considering criteria for “Strategic Raw Materials” in the compilation of the CRM list 
evaluation system would raise further awareness on the fundamental needs of European 
industry in the future and facilitate the adoption of policy action destined to secure access to 
these materials.

In a recent survey conducted among eighty-three (83) of its member companies, TIF 
identified a number of raw materials that may be considered to be “strategic” in line with 
the criteria cited above: antimony, borates, cobalt, germanium, lithium, Light Rare Earth 
Elements (LREEs), silicon metal, and tungsten (see Figure 1).1 
 
 
2. Improve Material Efficiency & Savings Across the EU 
 
To resolve the challenge posed by current and prospective shortages, it is first and foremost 
vital to lower the demand for all primary materials and especially Critical Raw 
Materials wherever possible. The EU should therefore incentivise the uptake of material-
efficient solutions at all stages of the value chain that reduce the reliance of industry on 
CRMs in larger quantities. It should be borne in mind that material efficiency consists of 
multiple sets of action: to begin with, the reduction of the amount of virgin natural 
resources required for producing a certain level of output and the recycling of post-
consumption waste material to allow it to be fed into the manufacturing process again (see 
Section 4). The reuse of wear parts and components is also part of material efficiency, as is

the extension of the lifetime of machinery, components, and spare parts through

Concretely, therefore, the EU should promote research into new technologies that could 
enable companies to use reduced amounts or lower grades of raw materials at the design 
and production stage – in other words, to do more with less. Financial support for such 
efforts ought to be provided via EU research programmes such as Horizon Europe. 
 
Likewise, the Commission should incentivise secondary material efficiency at the user stage 
by supporting measures to prolong the longevity of products and thereby reduce the need 
for new resources. To this end, it should be promoting the expansion of Operation and 
Maintenance (O&M), as well as repair and renovation services. This could be, among others, 
addressed in the forthcoming “Right to Repair” initiative.2 
 
TIF would also like to highlight that maintenance and services related to any given asset 
provide a great opportunity to improve the energy and environment performance. 
Prolonging the lifetime of assets, with optimised energy and material efficiency 
performance, is a key lever to reduce global GHG emissions. One of the most urgent 
supporting measures in this respect is to include all maintenance and services activities 
(including predictive maintenance) that are required to support enabling technologies in the 
EU taxonomy for sustainable activities3.    
 
3. Diversify Access to Primary Critical Raw Materials 
 
A large share of the European Union’s primary raw materials needs are covered by imports 
from outside the EU. As an organisation that endorses market-based solutions and is 
committed to free trade, TIF strongly welcomes the fruitful and mutually beneficial 
commercial relations in raw materials with third countries. However, recent geopolitical 
developments such as the Russian invasion of Ukraine have underlined that the EU must 
avoid unilateral dependencies when it comes to raw materials supply, especially on 
authoritarian states or countries without an effective regulatory system for the adequate 
management of environmental and social impacts. While satisfying supply needs in the 
short-term, such imbalances expose the Union to the risks of sudden disruptions and may 
limit its scope for political maneuver in crisis situations. As such, they pose a long-term 
economic threat to EU industry. 
 
In the spirit of the “open strategic autonomy”, TIF therefore calls on the EU institutions  to 
minimise the risk of such dependencies by two means: first, the EU should promote the 
domestic sourcing of primary raw materials and secondly, the EU must strengthen 
and launch new strategic trading partnerships with third countries on Critical Raw 
Materials.

➢ Faster Permitting: While sufficient financial means are available for primary CRM

➢ New IPCEIs: Classifying relevant mining, processing, and refining initiatives as

At the same time, certain measures such as mandatory objectives for the sourcing of 
Critical Raw Materials are not likely to be effective and should thus be avoided. Likewise, the 
EU will not be able to exclusively or even predominantly rely on domestic Critical Raw 
Material capacities. Self-sufficiency is not a feasible ambition. It is therefore crucial that the 
EU strikes commercial pacts with other countries to gain a reliable access to raw materials. 
Partnerships with like-minded economies should be prioritised in this context: the EU should 
therefore rapidly proceed with the negotiation, ratification and implementation of a number 
of Free Trade Agreements (i.e., Australia, Chile, India, Mercosur, and New Zealand) and 
strategic international partnerships that could boost the EU’s access to Critical Raw 
Materials. On the other hand, exposures to authoritarian regimes (e.g., China) must be 
avoided and rolled back as much as is feasible. This is pivotal to ensure the long-term 
success of European business and the green transition, as well as the integrity of the rules-
based international order. 
 
4. Promote a Truly Circular Economy for Critical Raw Materials 
 
Primary Critical Raw Materials are finite resources. Alone, they cannot cover the demand for 
raw materials within the EU in the coming decades. More action must be taken, therefore, 
to improve the access of European industry to recycled, secondary raw materials and 
technologies. By increasing their extraction from household and industrial waste streams, 
the EU can make a significant contribution towards reducing its dependency on primary raw 
materials. This is an area with great potential for improvement: when polled, less than 20%

of Finnish technology companies stated that they presently resort to recycling, substituting 
or using fewer primary raw materials to minimise supply risks (see Figure 3).12 This is not 
least concerning considering that many of the technologies to enable the uptake of 
secondary raw materials are already mature; they are presently simply not deployed at a 
sufficient scale to ensure a adequate supply to companies. One example for a material 
whose potential is currently underappreciated is ferrous scrap which, when recycled in the 
steel production process, can serve as a source for a wide variety of Critical Raw Materials, 
including tungsten, niobium, and vanadium. Other presently underused options to recover 
Critical Raw Materials are the use of industrial waste and side streams, as well as urban 
mining: it has been estimated that the European manufacturing industry could save up to 
630 billion euros per year if its resource use was more efficient.13 One challenge when it 
comes to industrial side stream utilisation – and waste utilisation in general – is that the 
definition of “waste” is very rigid and narrow. On the other hand, there are far too few EU-
wide “End-of-Waste” criteria available. Over-regulating waste through inflexible definitions 
endangers the ability of industry to take up potential resources from reused and recycled 
materials. To ensure that the potential offered by secondary raw materials is maximised, it 
is also vital to, among others, study the effects of circular economy principles on business 
ecosystems, value chains, and business models, as well as current political attitudes 
towards a circular economy and circular materials. 
To increase the availability and use of secondary raw materials in the short- and medium-
term, it is pivotal to facilitate the rapid roll-out of infrastructure for the recovery of 
and adoption of reliable standards on the quality of secondary raw materials. 
Collection, sorting, and recycling projects should thus benefit from similar support and 
inventivisation measures as mining, processing, and refining facilities:

➢ Faster Permitting: Recycling plants face similarly complex and cumbersome 
permitting procedures as projects for the extraction of primary raw materials. They 
should therefore likewise be considered as “overriding public interest”.  
 
➢ IPCEI Status: The EU should consider granting recycling projects for secondary raw 
materials IPCEI status in order to facilitate their uptake.  
 
Beyond this action, TIF supports efforts to set binding EU-wide technical norms for 
recyclates to ensure predictability for companies using them in their production processes. 
Furthermore, synergies between the ECRMA and other EU legislation ought to be identified: 
the Commission should thus explore how new instruments such as the Digital Product 
Passport could help in boosting the uptake of secondary raw materials by improving the 
traceability of products, including their components. Furthermore, it should consider the 
possibility of harnessing the potential of recyclates through a reform of the Waste Electrical 
and Electronic Equipment14 (WEEE) and Waste Framework15 directives. If correspondingly 
revised, both proposals could help to improve the collection, sorting, and recycling of high-
quality secondary raw materials by setting targets for collection, expanding the “End -of-
Waste” criteria, and establishing higher standards for the handling of waste resources. At 
the same time, the EU should be careful to ensure that the revision of relevant regulation of 
chemicals – most notably the REACH Regulation16 and the RoHS Directive17 – do not 
inadvertently create obstacles to recycling efforts. 
 
Beyond relying on and expanding the use of existing raw material recycling solutions, it is 
vital to support efforts to discover new recyclates and recycling technologies. The 
EU should, therefore, ensure that it allocates sufficient financial resources to research, 
development, and innovation (RDI) projects in this field through programmes such as 
Horizon Europe.

Technology Industries of Finland (TIF) represents Finnish technology industries and counts 
over 1,800 member companies, ranging from SMEs and start-ups to world leading MNEs. 
The technology industry is comprised of five subsectors: electronics and the electrotechnical 
industry, mechanical engineering, the metals industry, consulting engineering, and 
information technology. Technology industry is the most important export industry of 
Finland, with operations constituting over 50 % of all Finnish exports and accounting for 70 
% of all private investments.

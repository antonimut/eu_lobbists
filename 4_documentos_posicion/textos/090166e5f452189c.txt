European Critical Raw Materials Act Consultation response 
25 November 2022 
Anglo American welcomes the European Commission’s work on critical raw materials and its focus on 
forward-looking sectors, such as clean energy technologies (photovoltaic, wind, storage), electric mobility 
and digital technologies.  Metals and minerals are the essential ingredients in such technologies, helping  
move the world towards a cleaner, greener and more connected future – a more sustainable future.   At 
Anglo American, we are working to make this future a reality, to better connect precious resources to all of 
us who need and value them. 
 
Food security 
 
However, until the recent food security crisis caused by the war in Ukraine, the critical role of minerals in 
food production, and potassium specifically, has often been overlooked. As pointed out in the March 2022 
Communication on safeguarding food security, Europe is in the fortunate position that access to food for 
the disadvantaged, rather than widespread food insecurity, is our primary problem.  Yet Europe plays a 
pivotal role in growing and processing food for consumers around the world. Therefore, we should not be 
complacent about the importance of growing abundant and high-quality crops in Europe. Plant nutrients 
are essential for this, as was recognised in the 9 November 2022 Communication on ensuring availability 
and affordability of fertilisers. In this context, and with the rationale outlined below, we urge the 
Commission to include potassium in its next list of critical raw materials (bearing in mind that 
there are other forms besides potash to take into account as described below).  
 
The role of metals and minerals in the food chain 
 
Potassium (K) (often referred to as potash, K2O) is an essential nutrient for all living organisms. It is the 
third most important plant nutrient (by amount required) and has a vital role in plant metabolism, growth 
and adaptation to stress. Including potassium in fertiliser blends increases crop yield, health and uptake 
of nitrogen and potassium, which is an important tool in reducing nutrient pollution - the core aim of the 
Commission’s action plan for better nutrient management. 
In this context, we encourage the work on critical raw materials to include an emphasis on the food 
chain alongside the other sectors you list. The sectors you currently focus on are strategically 
important over the mid-term; disturbances in the food chain can trigger immediate crises, and these c an 
develop very quickly, as we have learned this year. 
Looking at the food chain alongside the digital and energy transitions is especially important since there 
will be competition for some of these raw materials among these sectors, and we need to unde rstand 
these dynamics. For example, copper is an essential plant micronutrient, a common crop fungicide, and 
an important component for electronics and renewable energy technologies. Phosphate is one of the 
major plant nutrients and a component in next-generation batteries.

Potassium security of supply 
 
In the 2020 “Study on the EU's list of Critical Raw Materials” prepared for the Commission during the 
revision of the EU’s list of critical raw materials, potassium had an equivalent economic importance as 
phosphate, but was just below the cut-off point for “supply risk” (see Figure A from that report, reproduced 
below).  However, this year’s events suggest that the security of potassium supplies should be re -
evaluated. To avoid disruptions to food supplies, sanctions packages have allowed some Russian and 
Belarussian potash to continue to be sold on international markets, but prices are at historically high 
levels. Furthermore, the global dependency on potash from these two countries means that the supply of 
potash could potentially be weaponised as the conflict continues. Together these two countries account 
for about 35% of potash product, and the Joint Research Centre’s briefing on potash in the context of the 
war in Ukraine notes that more than half of total EU imports of all forms of potash were sourced from 
Russia and Belarus in 2020. 
 
We therefore strongly endorse the third objective listed in the call for evidence to “Strengthen the 
EU’s critical raw materials value chain in a global context, specifically, the last point: “Identifying 
strategic projects outside the EU which could benefit from better access to funding, for instance 
based on their environmental and social performance.”  
However, funding it is not the only issue: the EU’s own policy coherence can either be an enabler or an 
obstacle to friend-sourced sources of critical raw materials. A case in point is the polyhalite mineral 
deposit located in the north of England that is currently being developed by Anglo American and one 
other company. Our 10 million tonne per year low-impact Woodsmith Mine will become one of Europe’s 
and the world’s largest single sources of crop nutrients when it reaches full production in the coming 
years. The mine’s European location and stable jurisdiction means EU farmers will benefit from security of 
supply and responsiveness to changing customer requirements.

The polyhalite extracted from this mine is a natural mineral containing potassium, sulphur, magnesium 
and calcium plus numerous micronutrients, and is more environmentally friendly than most other 
potassium fertiliser sources. Because it is not chemically processed, polyhalite has an ultra-low carbon 
footprint, is naturally low in chloride, and is suitable for organic farming. However, polyhalite does not fit 
well within the EU Fertilising Products Regulation (FPR) [Reg (EU) 2019/1009] because the tolerances 
defined in the FPR assume that the content of each nutrient can be controlled within narrow bands .  This 
is only true if chemical processing occurs and if the manufacturer can modify the content of each nutrient 
independently; neither of these assumptions applies in the case of a naturally occurring multi-nutrient 
product that is not chemically processed. We have submitted these observations to the EU Survey that 
DG GROW ran in September on further improvements of the Fertilising Products Regulation  (FPR).

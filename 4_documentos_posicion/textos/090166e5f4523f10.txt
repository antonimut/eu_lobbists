Summary 
Background 
To accelerate the clean energy transition in Europe and limit global warming to 1.5°C, EU countries 
have to rapidly scale up renewable energy and electr o-mobility. This requires increased amounts 
Critical raw materials are key to renewable energy technologies, electronic and electrical equipment and 
batteries for electric vehicles, amongst other applications. Growing global demand and increasing 
geopolitical instability creates the need to strengthen supply chains in Europe. 
ECOS welcomes the initiative to establish a  European Critical Raw Materials  (CRMs) Act. However, this 
must be based on circular principles and promote both sufficiency and resource -efficiency. This can be 
achieved by adopting the following circular strategies:  
• Rethink and reduce : demand-side reduction through systemic change, promote access over 
ownership, and substitute and optimise CRM use, 
• Circular design, repair and reuse: extend product life to keep CRMs longer in use, at the highest 
level of utility as possible; 
• Recycle: invest in high-quality recycling to bring CRMs back into circulation. 
Regulatory and non-regulatory actions should focus on the reuse and recovery of secondary raw materials 
from products and components, while CRM value chains have to meet strict  environmental and social 
requirements. To safeguard from supply disruptions, a mandatory, legal requirement to reuse and recycle 
CRMs in selected e-waste categories detailed in this paper is needed. This way, the energy transition will 
not only be climate-proof, but also material-efficient and socially just.

Currently, the mining and processing of these minerals is generally happening outside  of Europe 
and concentrated in a few countries (e.g., China 1). This strengthens  EU’s dependency on global 
supply chains. Global supply chains have faced (and will continue to face) serious disruptions, e.g., 
due to the COVID -19 pandemic or geopolitical tensions – increasing the need for supply chain 
resilience and stock management in the EU. 
The supply of critical raw materials (such as for  batteries: lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese and 
graphite) will become problematic in the near future as demand for these raw materials is expected 
to increase significantly in the coming years. The EU will need about 60 times more lithium and 15 
times more cobalt for EV batteries and energy storage by 2050, analysts estimate. 
The biggest challenge for the EU is to achieve a sustainable and stable balance between demand 
and supply of CRMs. Circular economy strategies play a critical role to reduce the demand for virgin 
raw materials2 and increase EU supply security and resilience. 
With its Critical Raw Materials Act initiative, announced by European Commission President 
Ursula von der Leyen in her speech on the State of the Union, the Commission wants to strengthen 
the EU CRM value chain , both domestically and externally. While we welcome this initiative , we 
call for the European Commission to base the CRM Act on  circular principles that promote both 
sufficiency and resource -efficiency. This can be achieved by adopting the following circular 
strategies:  
• Rethink and reduce : demand-side reduction through systemic change, promote access over 
ownership, and substitution of CRM use; 
• Circular design and reuse: extend product life to keep CRMs longer in use, at the highest level 
of utility as possible; 
• Recycle: invest in high-quality recycling to bring CRMs back into circulation. 
This paper will present each of this steps that should be underpin ned by a  transparent supply 
chain and sustainable mining and extraction practices. 
Rethink and Reduce 
To strengthen the resilience of CRM applications in Europe, the EU should not only look at 
managing the supply side but also the demand -side by implementing policies that centre around 
demand reduction (sufficiency). This will make the EU less dependent on imports of large volumes 
of primary raw materials.  
First of all, an EU-wide material footprint reduction target  (incl. CRMs) is necessary to r educe 
overall demand across the economy. Demand -side reduction can then be achieved through 
systemic change of critical infrastructure, utility delivery, design of business models and supply 
chains, ownership models and product use to make the product redu ndant or use it more

Another important strategy is substitution of CRMs  in manufacturing processes. For ins tance,

on the production of renewable energies, sustainable forms of mobility and technologies that help

Circular Design and Reuse 
Circular design requirements for products and infrastructure are crucial. As input f or production, 
secondary CR Ms should be preferred over primary raw materials. To achieve this, minimum 
recycled content targets of CRMs in key products and components are necessary. This requires a 
swift development of the methodology for the calculation o f recycled content of all types of CRM 
equipment. The success of this measure will highly depend on whether the volumes of CRMs 
placed on the market remain stable. 
Strong circular design requirements  should ensure good performance and durability, reusabili ty 
and repairability of CRM equipment and components, thus extending product lifetime and keeping 
CRMs longer in use. These requirements have to be supported by appropriate technical standards 
(e.g. standards that assess the performance and durability of C RM products, components and 
equipment, but also technical specifications to facilitate repair, reuse and recycling of CRM 
products, components and equipment). 
Despite the immense potential for reuse of EV batteries, this is not happening at scale at the 
moment because of various obstacles, e.g. design, costs, lack of incentives, lack of guidance and 
standards, low availability of batteries (which will change in the near future), legal ambiguities etc. 
The following recommendations stimulate circular design and reuse of CRM equipment and 
components: 
• Prioritise reuse over repurposing  and remanufacturing over recycling  – through mandatory 
testing of suitability for reuse, repurposing and remanufacturing prior to recycling;  
• Phase-out single-use products containing CRMs (e.g. portable batteries); 
• Introduce mandatory second life assessment  requirements for CRM equipment and 
components. Mandatory tests to determine whether it is technically possible and economically 
reasonable (including if there is a market) for CR M equipment  and components to be 
repurposed for second life; 
• Devise circular design requirements  so that components containing CRMs can be easily 
accessed/disassembled for reuse or recycling;

Recycle 
Various equipment contain CRMs in concentrations high enough to enable recycling. This applies 
to printed circuit boards, batteries fr om WEEE and en d of life vehicles, neodymium iron boron 
magnets from hard-disc drives, electrical engines of e-bikes, scooters and end-of-life vehicles, and 
fluorescent powders from cathode ray tubes and fluorescent lamps ( CEWASTE, 2020). Recovery 
technologies and processes are well established for some CRMs, such as palladium from printed 
circuit boards or cobalt from lithium -ion batteries. For other CRMs, ongoing recycling technology 
development will soon make industrial scale operations possible.  
However, in the absence of clear requirements to recycle CRMs, the generic, weight -based 
collection and recycling targets for WEEE in the EU lead producers and member states to focus on 
overall tonnages rather than the quality recycling of small amounts of CRMs. In general, low 
collection rates of WEEE prevent the ir sound treatment, while insufficient separation and sorting 
of key CRM equipment (KCE) from other WEEE during collection leads to a situation in which there 
is insufficient concentrated volumes to recycle CRMs. 
Furthermore, lack of financing prevents the recycling of CRMs even in cases where this would be 
technically feasible with acceptable a dditional effort at a reasonable cost -benefit balance. 
Currently, recycling of CRMs is not economically attractive for CRMs other than palladium and, to 
a certain degree, cobalt and antimony under the present economic conditions (CEWASTE, 2020). 
As a result, recycling most of the products rich in CRMs is still not commercially viable, with low 
and volatile CRM price s undermining efforts to improve European CRM recycling rates, which 
today are close to zero in most cases. That is why the CRM Act should establish binding targets 
for the final recycling of specific CRMs from different (post-consumer) waste streams.  
End-of-life products and infrastructure containing CRMs (e.g. e -waste and renewable energy 
infrastructure), manufacturing scrap5 and materials (e.g. black mass from batteries) should be kept 
in the EU for high -quality recycling 6. The collection and separation of CRM products  and 
components respectively should also be improved to concentrate fractions of CRM -rich products 
into larger quant ities to enable the economic viability of recycling. The collection of CRM -rich 
equipment could be stimulated through deposit -return systems or similar financial incentives. 
Furthermore, cost-effective methods  to disassemble WEEE , isolate CRM bearing components, 
and upgrade/concentrate CRMs should be scaled up for industrial adoption.  
For instance, a collection target of 85% should be set fo r portable batteries from 2030 and 90% 
for light means of transport from 2025, complemented by a material recovery  rate of 95% for 
cobalt, nickel and copper in 2025 and 98% in 2030; 70% of lithium in 2025 and 90% in 2030 7. 
To achieve these targets, market incentives  should be created to spur the economic viability of 
recovering CRMs and to stimulate the use of recovered CRMs in new products. Platforms have to

Sustainable Mining and Sourcing 
Domestic extraction and refinery capacities for CRMs in Europe are low and projected to stay low 
in the near future. At present, there is  minimal lithium mining and ref ining in the EU (only in 
Portugal). For cobalt, EU supplies 10% of demand from domestic mining, while no new mining 
projects or expansion to refining (70% of demand) are planned. 
The problems with mining are of course the far -reaching environmental and social impacts9. This 
is why there is often significant public opposition by local communities and civil society against 
mining operations. Therefore, the extraction of primary raw materials should be avoided as much 
as possible. Existing (urban) mining waste should be mapped10 and recovered as a priority ahead 
of new mines. The EU must also establish “no -go” zones for exploration and extractive activities 
e.g. protected areas, Natura2000 sites, the Arctic, the deep sea.  
If secondary raw materials cannot mee t the demand, strategic projects for the mining of CRMs in 
Europe have to meet strict environmental regulations on environmental impacts and waste 
(tailings management 11). Low -impact, sustainable mining of course has the preference over 
destructive practices 12. This has to be ensured through ambitious legislation 13 and international 
standards, closely involving civil society. Failure to manage environmental and social impacts from 
minerals development will slow clean energy transitions 14 (IEA, 2021). Sustainable low -impact

When secondary raw materials and domestic extraction are not sufficient and CRMs have to be 
imported, sustainable sourcing - respecting human rights and environmental  due diligence - is a 
must15. Global imports of CRMs should be underpinned by strong standards such as IRMA to 
guarantee responsible supply. Both the secondary and primary raw materials that are stockpiled 
must comply with the highest possible ecological, human rights and labour standards.  
Lastly, the pursuit of supply diversification through Free Trade Agreements and new partnerships 
to advance the EU’s vital interests and values should not undermine the interests and development 
of countries in the Global South.  
Transparency throughout the value chain 
Transparency and data -sharing on CRMs and environmental impacts along the value chain is 
crucial. Access to information on CRM -rich compo nents should be improved through the 
development of solid criteria to assess CRM presence in products and components. As a minimum, 
the marking of key CRM components and their chemical composition is necessary to differentiate 
them from other components (i n particular relevant for NdFeB - magnets containing CRMs). 
Furthermore, monitoring and reporting requirements should be based on a reliable methodology 
to be used in a standardised way by all producers. Blockchain could be used to track CRM presence 
in all materials in a standardised and protected way.  
The Critical Raw Materials Act should require all suppliers to measure and report their carbon 
emissions16, which will reward better extraction technology, as well as recyclers. Furthermore, 
companies in the  upstream and downstream supply chains must monitor and report their 
performance related to other environmental and social impacts.

Impact assessment feedback  
 
EU businesses are progressively witnessing a significant increase in demand of strategic materials 
and components that is not being met by existing production capabilities. At the same time, the EU is 
facing threats of losing production due to decreasing global competitiveness, especially in the area of 
energy intensive basic products.  
This leads to shortages in downstream product supply, some of them with strategic importance (i.e. for 
those products linked to the implementation of the Twin Transition).  
Such a context, in conjunction with an increasing weaponization of strategic resources, urgently calls 
for dedicated actions to: 
1) Increase supply availability and resilience via an ambitious global strategy to secure 
responsible and diversified imports, avoiding an overdependence on unsustainable single 
suppliers – Policy option 1, 2 and 3; 
2) Foster a circular economy for secondary raw materials via improved regulatory integration 
within the Single Market, and via horizontal waste collection and reuse activities across the 
Member States – Policy option 4; 
3) Provide a predictable legislative framework so to foster growth, financing and investments in 
the sector (e.g. on processing and reformulation, R&D, etc.) – Additional policy 
recommendation.  
 
1) Supply availability 
 
The upcoming CRM Act should foster a proactive and market-oriented trade policy, including via new 
Free Trade Agreements with dedicated raw material chapters, as well as sectorial partnerships with 
likeminded partners (e.g. EU-Canada Strategic Partnership on Critical Raw Materials).  
Priority should be given to the strategic partnership negotiations with resource-rich countries/regions, 
such as Latin America (for copper/lithium), and Indonesia (for nickel, cobalt).  
 
Supply chain resilience should be further supported by a coherent and EU-level coordinated impact 
assessment mechanism, including via regular stakeholder consultations, to prevent and promptly 
respond to possible future shortages (i.e. via voluntary stockpiling activities). 
 
Moreover, the current methodology used to define a “Critical Raw Material” should be regularly 
amended (e.g. each 2 years) to reflect current and expected dependencies, future demand 
increases, supply risks, and investment needs, bringing together in one strategic list:  
I. A list based on current market demands/availabilities, thus focusing on the scarcely -available 
resources that are needed for certain strategic sectors (e.g. e-mobility, electrification);  
II. Other materials, defined strategic by the relevant EU stakeholders, considered more exposed 
to supply shocks in the future, but not yet “critical” (reflecting future demand evolution and 
potential supply challenges). 
 
This new approach should be further strengthened by the creation of an EU-level system to identify 
priority projects, in line with European ESG standards, aimed at improving security of supply to EU 
industries (e.g. on the model of Important Projects of Common European Interest). These projects 
should then qualify for faster permitting at national level, for easier financing from EU and national 
tools, and for public funding opportunities, including via debt/equity investments in foreign mining 
projects with offtake of European industry players (e.g. Korea/Japan model).  
 
2) Circularity  
 
The Act should provide dedicated measures to ensure barriers-free alignment among Member States 
in integrating waste and circularity frameworks, so to promote the material-efficient recycling of critical 
raw materials across key product groups (e.g. e-batteries, vehicles, windfarms etc.) within the Single 
Market. 
 
Particularly relevant amendments could target, for instance, prevention of the leakage outside of the 
EU of valuable intermediate waste streams such as black mass (which contains valuable metals such 
as lithium, cobalt and nickel, derived either from end-of-life batteries or battery production waste).

The upcoming CRM communication should further highlight additional priority actions to be carried 
forward across waste legislation reviews, such as: 
• Call for achievable end-of-life recycling targets, and propose EU-wide improved collection and 
sorting system requirements; 
• Enforce transparency across the EU’s most strategic value chains (e.g. batteries) and 
introduce incentives for the consumer to choose products containing raw materials that are 
sourced based on European ESG standards; 
• Ensure that European ESG standards are a given for imports across critical materials’ value 
chain in Europe. 
 
3) EU Legislative framework  
 
The upcoming CRM Act should present measures aimed at providing long-term certainty for 
competitive operating conditions, so to encourage growth, innovation and stable sectorial 
investments.  
In this regard, securing a competitive access to energy, which is a key aspect for energy intensive 
business activities such as metals’ processing, will be fundamental. The EU Critical Raw Materials Act 
should make the link with EU energy market discussions, aiming at fostering creation of a fully 
integrated Single Market for energy, and thus at securing the circulation of affordable, and increasingly 
sustainable energy within the EU.  
 
The investments’ certainty for critical raw materials’ projects is also severely hindered by the current 
EU chemical legislative framework, which makes it even more fundamental for future amendments 
(i.e. REACH revision) to address these existing barriers.  
Several strategic metals, such as lithium and cobalt, indeed have to comply to oftentimes 
unpredictable changes in classification and/or risk management procedures – which finally hamper 
investments’ flows into the sector.  
The CRM Act should thus include measures to ensure a more coherent and predictable chemicals’ 
regulatory framework. Such measures would further support the Union’ s chemical industry, which is a 
fundamental component of CRMs’ processing and recycling (i.e. via the production of intermediate 
chemicals). 
 
Furthermore, increasing the resilience of our value chains will necessarily require a broader impact 
assessments mechanism for any potentially impactful legislative initiative (e.g. implementation of 
CBAM; ETS phase-out). Such a thorough and horizontal approach will support the restoration of 
missing value chains, whilst safeguarding the competitiveness of existing ones.

Positioning on a European Strategic Raw Materials Act 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Summary  
   
Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine, which violates international 
law, has highlighted Europe's dependence and vulnerability on Russian 
energy sources. But not only here: In the case of strategic  minerals  such as 
rare earths, the dependence, especially on China, is already much greater.  
 
Raw materials are being used as a weapon by Russia in particular. Europe 
can be blackmailed. Unlike for oil and gas, there are no strategic reserves 
of strategic  minerals foreseen. A supply stop would affect E uropean 
industry immediately and far -reaching.  
 
Without raw materials there will be no energy transition, no e -mobility, no 
digitalization, no industry 4.0 - but also no infrastructure expansion , no 
secured food supply chains  and no powerful defence industr y. 
 
The race for strategically important raw materials is in full swing. Europe 
risks losing important sources of raw materials in competition with other 
countries. The consequence: dependencies and the risk of supply shortages 
are increasing.  
 
To actively and ambitiously deal with these challenges, the supply of 
strategic  raw materials must be defined as part of Europe’s security.  
 
The Federation of German Industries (BDI) shares the European 
Commission’s general analysis and goal to secure strategically important raw 
materials. In order to reduce its strategic dependencies, the EU and its 
member states need to pursue all three pillars of a sustainable supply of raw 
materials: first, strengthening domestic extraction of raw materials; second, 
securing fair access to raw materials from abroad; third, expanding recycling 
and the circular economy.   
 
For BDI this means:   
 
− Reducing geopolitical supply risks through a n integrated value 
chain and three -pillar strategy.  Europe should diversify and beco me 
more independent. The goal is integrated value chains for strategic areas 
in Europe and with reliable partners. Europe's security of supply can only 
be guaranteed by domestic raw materials (pillar 1) , imported raw 
materials (pillar 2) and recycled raw materials (pillar 3) together. 
 
− Countering the NIMBY mentality : Promote domestic mining and 
refining and make it socially acceptable.  Every ton of domestic raw 
material extracted  or refined  reduces dependence on imports and 
strengthens Europe's resilience and security of supply. Mining  and 
refining must be realised through accelerated planning and permitting 
procedures. The mining and processing industry needs competitive 
energy prices and s ufficient renewable electricity as well as support for 
 
 
www.bdi.eu 
the conversion to hydrogen . Education and awareness -raising initiatives 
on social acceptance must be expanded , as well as skills development . 
Promoting international raw materials cooperation and the  
extraction of critical raw materials abroad . By public de-risking and 
risk capital instruments , long -term purchase agreements should be 
supported in a targeted manner. Cooperation with resource -rich 
countries with high standards must be intensified , and strategic raw 
materials must be included as a component in trade, investment and 
development agreements  to promote more sustainable local exploration 
and refining.  
 
− Leveraging innovations for the circular economy and avoid 
inconsistencies. Appropriate framework conditions are needed for a 
circular economy along the entire value chain, which encourage the 
comprehensive collection of end -of-life products, the application of 
high-quality recycling processes, and the use of recycled materials and 
industrial by -products - as well as  related private investment. Further 
innovation in manufacturing, processing and recycling processes should 
be supported through research funding. It shou ld be avoided that supply 
chain due diligence legislation, EU chemical legislation and taxonomy 
rules jeopardize the necessary diversification of raw material supply.  
 
With this package of primarily market -based measures, European 
enterprises should be su pported and strategically incentivized to 
diversify, to monitor and identify strategic raw materials, to expand 
storage and to increase resource efficiency as well as circular economy  
approaches. 
 
Finally, the BDI wants to stress that the challenge goes beyond raw 
material policy and shows the importance to design policy measures in 
the context of a n updated  strategic (internal and external) European 
industry policy – as e.g. the US show with its Inflation Reduction Act .

Introduction 
 
Raw materials are at the very beginning of the value chain of innovative 
technologies, industrial processes and applications.  Without raw materials 
there can be no digitali zation or Industry 4.0, no energy transition or e -
mobility, no green deal or compliance with the Paris climate targets. German 
and European industry depends upon a secure and sustainable supply of raw 
materials.  
 
The Federation of German Industries shares the analysis of the European 
Commission that in view of the growing global demand for raw materials for 
the energy transition, strategic technologies as well as increasing geopolitical 
challenges urgent efforts in the area of the EU’s raw material policy are 
needed to secure the supply of strategic  raw materials for Europe’s industry.  
 
Free and fair access to raw materials is often obstructed through state 
measures that distort trade. Compounding the secure access to raw materials 
further is the high concentration of supply in one or a few countries , as well 
as the current rise in geopolitical tensions.  
 
As the EU is heavily dependent on concentrated supply sources from a few 
countries and from China in particular for a number of strategic raw 
materials, i t is urgent time to re-evaluate and establish the tools needed to 
minimize and manage the projected structural supply risks that will most 
likely affect the EU’s ability to achieve the green and digital transitions.

1. Preliminary remarks on the scope and name of the Act  
 
We wel come that the Commission , when defining “critical raw materials” 
states that they can also include other strategic raw materials (e.g. copper).  
We would thus suggest speaking of “strategic raw materials” and re-name the 
legislative proposal into “European Strategic Raw Materials Act”.  
 
In any case, “strategic” would need to be properly defined to really focus and 
prioritise. What should be avoided is that any policy measure and public 
investment based on that list fizzles out. While “criticality” provides one set 
of criteria, technology and industry policy related considerations could add 
more.  
 
Furthermore, while energy resources are excluded from the scope , it is key 
to mention that the acces s to competitive energy  and sufficient renewable 
electricity in Europe  is paramount when designing effective and efficient 
strategic raw material policy measures.  
 
2. Challenges 
 
As the Commission asks in its questionnaire, w e need to identify the reasons 
why the EU’s  strategic dependencies have developed in order to counter 
them: Alongside the globally increasing demand for strategic raw materials 
it was mainly China’s targeted pricing and industry policy that made mining 
and further processing in other countries economically unattractive. 
Consequently, the geographical concentration of reserves, extraction and 
processing facilities increased.  
 
Other factors include consolidation processes in the European mining sector, 
the lack of a global level playing field, the increase of state intervention and 
low investment in new exploration projects. Additional challenges in the 
mining industry include the long and high-risk timelines of projects, possible 
changes in demand and disruption caused by technological innovations, of 
the lock-in to deposits, high entry barriers and pricing monopolies.  
 
Finally, there is a lack of social awareness and acceptance for the domestic 
extraction of raw materials.   
 
3. Solutions 
 
In order to reduce its strategic dependencies, the EU and its member states 
need to pursue all three pillars of a sustainable supply of raw materials : 
first, strengthening domestic extraction of raw materials; second, securing 
fair access to raw materials from abroad; third, expanding recycling and the 
circular economy. 
 
Strengthening domestic mining and refining (pillar 1) 
 
Every ton of domestic raw material  extracted under high European mining 
standards reduces the EU’s dependence on imports , supports climate 
protection by reducing CO2 intense transport  and thus strengthens Europe's 
 
 
www.bdi.eu 
resilience and security of supply. The European mining sector is committed 
to the EU Principles for Sustainable Raw Materials.  
 
What many are not aware of: Today, Europe is almost self -sufficient in 
producing many industrial minerals and aggregates. But t here are also 
deposits of most of the required strategic raw materials within the EU. Mining 
in Europe takes place according to high est transparency and sustainability 
criteria. The last reports by the multi -stakeholder Extractive Industry 
Transparency Initiative (EITI)  on the raw materials sectors in Germany and 
the Netherlands once again impressively confirmed this.  
 
If we want that, all stakeholders – politicians, industry, trade unions and 
NGOs – have to be more willing to compromise and act pragmatically  to 
strengthen and secure domestic raw materials supply . This includes, for 
example, the new mining of strategically important lithium within the EU but 
also well-established mining of raw materials like potash . 
 
What we see as one of the main preconditions for domestic mining and 
refining though is countering the societal NIMBY mentality when it comes 
to concrete projects on the ground. In our view, the EU and its member states 
thus need to promote domestic mining and refining  and contribute to 
making them socially acceptable. Education and awareness -raising 
initiatives on social acceptance must be expanded , as well as skills 
development. 
 
Similarly, important is that mining and refining must be realised through 
foreseeable, streamlined, simplified and accelerated planning and 
permitting procedures . In operational practice, approval procedures have 
meanwhile reached a scope and complexity that is becoming increasingly 
difficult for companies to handle and makes it almost impossible to act 
flexibly in the face of rapidly changing market situations.  
 
While the governance and regulation should maintain national or local close 
to the communities the EU could play a role in defining domestic mining 
and refining as strategic for the EU , by comparing legal, administrative 
and participatory procedures to provide for best practices and by providing 
for seed money for exploration projects .  
 
Europe has the highest environmental, water and nature protection standards 
in the field of raw materi al extraction in the world. In some areas, however, 
the high standards no longer lead to significantly higher environmental 
protection but tend to become a hurdle for new raw material projects and for 
the continuation of previous raw material activities. T he European 
Commission should therefore examine the areas in which simplifications are 
possible and acceptable in order to ensure a competitive supply of raw 
materials. 
 
The goal of the Raw Materials Act is to be welcomed. At the same time, the 
EU continue s to enact legislation, such as the revision of the EU Industrial 
Emissions Directive, future soil protection legislation, higher CO2 pricing, 
etc., which significantly burden and complicate domestic raw material 
 
 
www.bdi.eu 
extraction. These run counter to the EU's goal of improving the supply of raw 
materials. There is therefore an urgent need for a " Raw Materials Check " 
for future legislation to ensure that new regulations do not impair domestic 
raw material extraction. 
 
But all efforts to build an independent value  chain will fail if t he location 
conditions for the raw material -extracting and refining  industry are not 
improved, especially  with competitive energy prices and sufficient 
electricity. A lot of smelters in Europe had to downgrade or even shut down 
their production. De -Industrialization and thus an increase in dependencies 
on imports is a reality already and need to be stopped.  This goes beyond raw 
material policy and shows the importance to design policy measures in the 
context of a strategic (internal and external) European industry policy . 
 
 
Securing access to raw materials from abroad (pillar 2) 
 
Complete self-sufficiency is wishful thinking and strategically not desirable 
in a networked world. Under the new geopolitical conditions, Europe’s 
dependencies and networking risks must be managed as effectively as 
possible and control over strategic nodes in this network must be 
strengthened.  
 
In order to secure access to strategically important raw materials in highly 
concentrated markets and to a void the risk of bottlenecks, even delivery 
stops, both international cooperation and public de-risking is crucial in order 
to expand production capacities and thus supply. 
 
Instruments like the German Untied Loan Guarantees (UFK) that since 1961 
facilitates the access to raw materials abroad encourage long-term purchase 
agreements, so-called off-take agreements, and could be complemented by a 
similar EU de-risking instrument for access to strategic raw materials that 
are of interest for the European industry as a whole . Also, we recommend to 
establish a dedicated EU venture capital instrument , e.g. an European 
strategic raw materials investment fund . 
 
Finally, there is still too little strategic international cooperation on raw 
materials. The cooperation within the framework of the Minerals Security 
Partnership, in the Trade and Technology Council with the USA and with the 
EU’s strategic raw materials partners is a good start. 
 
What is missing are more extensive links between foreign policy, trade 
policy and development policy  as well as with the Global Gateway 
Initiative. Next to the obvious resource-rich countries Australia and Canada, 
for example, Brazil is extremely relevant for the strategic raw material 
niobium, South Africa for the platinum group metals and Turkey for boron 
minerals. Strategic raw materials should be included as a component of trade, 
investment and development policy agreements to encourage more 
sustainable local exploration and refining with credit s, loans, 
investments and technical assistance as well as access to raw materials in 
exchange for investments in infrastructure and technology transfer .

Together with industry it should be elaborated where there is real potential 
and interest in scalable exploration  both abroad and in Europe. 
 
 
Expanding recycling and the circular economy (pillar 3)  
 
BDI sees the great potential of innovation and the circular economy. Metallic 
raw materials in particular are not “consumed” but “used” and can be made 
available again at the end of the product lifespan in which they are used. 
While the recycling quotas in the EU are already among the highest in the 
world on paper, the physical reality in closing the materials loops still looks 
different in many cases, especially for consumer goods such as electronic 
devices, cars and batteries. E.g. in the field of metals, significant amounts of 
these are lost due to insufficient collection of end-of-life-products, illegal and 
dubious exports and an unlevelled playing field from low standard recycling 
processes. These significant deficits cause substantial losses of raw materials 
that are urgently needed for future technologies . Thus, collection rates must 
be increased. 
 
In order to be able to make further progress in closing material cycles, 
recyclability needs to be integrated much more than before  already in 
the product design, at the very beginning of the value chain. Innovations in 
the area of strategic raw materials with a view to materials as well as 
manufacturing, processing and recycling processes  should be promoted . 
Recycling stands and falls with the input. For this reason, more measures are 
needed to strengthen inputs. 
 
BDI calls for a regulatory framework that supports companies on their way 
to a climate -neutral circular economy. This requires additional instruments 
for physically closing material and product cycles, planning and investment 
security for companies, a coherent, innovation -friendly and secure legal 
framework, and initiatives to ensure competitiveness. In addition, industry 
must face a setting which enables the calcula tion of competitive energy and 
raw material prices. This urgently requires a funding policy that supports the 
transformation towards the circular economy as well as resource efficiency , 
and also takes into account the interests of small and medium -sized 
enterprises (SMEs).  
 
As an example, recycling processes for lithium -ion-based traction batteries 
are becoming increasingly established but are not yet sufficiently scaled and 
require some further technological optimisation. Technological development 
can therefore continue to be supported by specific research and development 
programs as well as investment funding programs of the European Union. 
The same also applies to the development of high -quality, resource -saving 
and recyclable materials and material design s, the closing of material cycles 
in production, and the digitalization and automation of end -of-life vehicle 
dismantling and recycling processes.  
 
More concretely, BDI advocates the product -specific consideration of 
"design for circularity" criteria such as durability, reusability, repairability, 
recyclability, etc. within the framework of the EU's sustainable product and 
 
 
www.bdi.eu 
materials policy. Possible conflicts of objectives arising in this context, e.g. 
with the "design for performance" of products, must be  resolved in the sense 
of a holistic "design for sustainability" in a circular economy. Only through 
uniform rules in the EU internal market and uniform enforcement and market 
surveillance, such criteria may become effective for numerous products.  
 
In addit ion, it must be ensured that imports from third countries can only 
enter the EU internal market if the requirements are met, to avoid competitive 
disadvantages for European companies  and sustainability leakage . For those 
product groups that do not fall within the scope of the Ecodesign Directive in 
the future, specific regulations must be used or created with regard to their 
sustainable product design. Besides others, these include the regulations for 
packaging w ithin the meaning of the EU Packaging Directive, for 
accumulators and batteries within the meaning of the EU Battery Regulation 
or for products for which environmental product declarations are available in 
accordance with EN 15804. 
 
BDI calls for a swift, EU-wide ban on the landfilling of untreated municipal 
waste, legislative support and incentives for high Europe-wide collection 
rates and support for EU -companies for the control of the recycling circuit  
and the sole application of high-quality recycling processes . The illegal 
outflow of secondary raw materials  must be prevented. As an example, the 
European customs authorities should be able to stop the export of potentially 
environmentally hazardous used cars. To this end, the authorities should have 
a catalogue of criteria to hand to aid in decision-making, which takes account 
of the interests of the exporting and the importing countries, too. The Swiss 
system could serve as a model.  
 
German industry advocates for manageable and unambiguous regulat ions on 
GPP in the context of the Green Deal that comply with public procurement 
law and encourage the tendering of climate -friendly circular economy 
products and services. To this end, it will also be necessary to ensure that the 
products and services to be procured are also identifiable as such for the 
procuring entities. 
 
BDI calls to ensure that the interfaces between product, substance and waste 
law are coordinated in such a way that companies gain legal certainty in 
closing material cycles and that th ere are no contradictory regulations. In 
addition, new ordinances, guidelines and regulations for the end of waste 
status and for by -products should also be considered. Scientific risk 
assessment must be retained as a core element of chemicals management.  
 
There must be a focus on coherence between the ambitions to further develop 
the Circular Economy and the Sustainable Chemicals Strategy. Digital 
information systems, such as the announced digital product passport of the 
EU Commission, which are to provide the necessary data for closed product 
and material cycles, are to be developed together with the circles involved. 
This is particularly important against the background of preventing future 
initiatives that are not target -oriented and cost -intensive, such as the 
implementation of the so-called SCIP database (pursuant to Article 9(1)(i) of 
the Waste Framework Directive).

4. Further measures / remarks 
 
Avoid inconsistencies 
 
The EU has to be careful not to set the wrong framework conditions. 
Supply chain due diligence laws, for example, rightly emphasize the 
increased importance of risk management and prevention in the supply 
chain. In the currently envisaged form, however, there is a risk  that 
companies will avoid certain countries and suppliers as a precautionary 
measure only because of incalculable liability risks, or that they will be 
faced with insolvable conflicts in countries on which they are heavily 
dependent. This makes diversific ation difficult. What is needed is thus the 
right sense of proportion and the targeted support of transformation paths.  
The EU risks to do the second step before providing for enough own 
capacities and resilience. 
 
Also, if three lithium salts were classif ied by the European Chemicals 
Agency ECHA as “hazardous” and mining activities were declared 
“unsustainable” within the EU taxonomy, the goal of integrated value 
chains in Europe would probably not be achievable.  
 
Weighing pros and cons of instruments 
 
If the EU and its member states are serious about a strategic raw material (and 
industry) policy, they also need to provide for sufficient support instruments. 
Apart from the instruments already mentioned above (de -risking, investment 
fund(s) etc.), the pros and cons of several instruments that the Commission 
proposes in its questionnaire should be carefully analyzed and open questions 
of different options for potential implementation discussed with the industry.  
 
As an example, BDI is in favo ur of tax incentives for storage at company 
level or of changing existing tax law and thus accounting rules that 
discriminate storage at company level, respectively . When incentivising, it 
should be noted  that not all strategic raw materials and their processed 
products can be stored  due to their chemical characteristics. If a strategic 
storage in the defence sector is considered, it should  include questions of 
logistics for both strategic raw materials a nd components.

About BDI 
 
The Federation of German Industries  (BDI) communicates German 
industries’ interests to the political authorities  concerned. She offers strong 
support for companies in global competition. The BDI has access to a wide -
spread network both within Ge rmany and Europe, to all the important 
markets and to international organizations. The BDI accompanies the 
capturing of international markets politically. Also, she offers information 
and politico-economic guidance on all issues relevant to industries. The BDI 
is the leading organization of German industries  and related service 
providers. She represents 40 inter-trade organizations and more than 100.000 
companies with their approximately 8 million employees. Membership is 
optional. 15 federal representations are advocating industries’  interests on a 
regional level.

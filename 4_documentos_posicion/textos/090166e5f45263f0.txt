ETRMAs response to the Call for Evidence on the Critical Raw 
Materials Act 
 
 
Introduction 
 
The European Tyre and Rubber Industry welcomes the Roadmap published by the European 
Commission on a European Raw Materials’ Act. 
 
Our industry and its value chain are dependent on critical raw materials like natural rubber: 73% of 
the world’s natural rubber consumption is used in tyre production, representing between 20 and 
40% of the weight of a tyre. With Natural Rubber included in the EU critical raw materials’ (CRM) list 
since 2017, ETRMA and its members have been active in the context of sustainability and resilience 
of the natural rubber supply chain as well as the wider initiatives under the European Innovation 
Partnership on raw materials.  
 
In that context, our industry engaged in several activities to tackle the criticalities in the tyre and 
rubber supply chain. These included the expansion of the cultivation of natural rubber to further 
geographies beyond South East Asia and investments in the development of European-grown 
alternatives to hevea rubber.  
 
EU’s efforts to support the further development and uptake of EU-sourced bio-based alternatives, 
such as dandelions and guayule to produce natural rubber, and bio-based synthetic polymers are 
highly welcomed and expected to provide positive impetus to the resilience of the EU supply chain. 
 
Recent geopolitical turbulent events in the EU’s immediate neighbourhood have evidenced further 
criticalities in our supply chain. Particularly, this concerned carbon black and synthetic rubber – for 
which Russia was one of the main suppliers of the EU. For these and other strategic raw materials 
used in the tyre and rubber industry, we fully support efforts to increase and diversify the supply 
chain, particularly by enhancing and incentivising the recovery of materials from waste, such as 
recycled carbon black. ETRMA considers the concept of circular economy as a necessary approach to 
manage future scarcity of raw materials and to promote the efficient use of resources. 
 
This paper presents measures already implemented by our sector as well as areas where a Critical 
Raw Materials Act could help realise the untapped potential that could help meet the EU’s policy 
objectives and ultimately contribute to a resource-efficient and competitive Europe.

In the context of the pillars proposed in the Roadmap, the tyre and rubber industry supports the 
following actions 
 
1. Determining strategic critical raw materials must be based on pre -set criteria (including -but 
not limited to - economic importance, supply concentration, demand substitution, strategic 
applications and forecasted supply gaps). These criteria should be complemented by the need 
to increase the resilience and regional diversification of the supply chain. Furthermore, when 
considering the criticality of a raw material  it is important to take into account  the 
downstream supply chain in addition to the upstream perspective.  
These criteria should also include the support to secondary raw materials developments and 
use, in order to sustain circularity in key strategic applications 
 
2. Improving the EU’s monitoring, risk management and governance in the field of CRM : this 
action supported through the work of a dedicated network should go beyond the selected list 
of identified critical raw materials in order to anticipate criticalities not yet identified. This 
should be done through the transition pathway, in our case, for the automotive sector.  
 
3. Build the necessary regulatory and financing framework  to strengthen the EU’s critical and 
strategic raw materials value chain. 
 
ETRMA calls for actions towards a resilient, sustainable, and innovative European tyre & rubber 
industry. To this end, there is the urgent need  to remove obstacles and barriers , which stand in the 
way of reinforcing, in the mid - to long -term, an increased independence in the EU for key raw 
materials in the tyre and rubber production.   
 
In particular, it is key to facilitate the use of recycled carbon black (rCB) in new tyres. Companies are 
already working on specifications / minimum requirements for rCB to be used in new products. ETRMA 
suggests complementing the above actions with the following concrete measures. 
 
To this end, ETRMA suggests the following actions: 
1. Ensuring the availability of relevant and accurate harmonised technical standards: accelerate 
the industry’s efforts towards the development of minimum requirements for recovered 
carbon black from ELTs 
2. Recognise chemical recycling in EU regulatory framework as a recyc ling operation based on 
quality output material. 
3. Identify, prioritise, fund and facilitate the development of strategic projects in the EU that 
have the potential to help secure the EU’s diversified access to critical raw materials and have 
strong environmental and social performance. 
4. Set EU-harmonised End of Waste criteria for end-of-life tyre-derived secondary raw materials. 
This would result in  their inclusion in the scope of the  REACH regulation, leading to a level-
playing field , both in terms of harmonisation of rules across the EU and enabling fair 
competition with virgin raw materials . Circular economy would thus be at the service of 
material criticality.

These measures should be underpinned by the following principles: 
1. Ensure consistency, ambition and coherence across EU legislation, as well as avoid duplication 
of regulatory requirements . Objectives concerning the resilience of supply chains, 
competitiveness of the EU industry and  environmental, social and governance  (ESG) should 
be considered in a holistic and coordinated manner.  
2. Enhance cooperation with third countries both with regards  to ensuring the supply of the 
necessary raw materials, but also to ensure that these are obtained in line with the EU 
ambitions with regards to ESG objectives. 
3. Enabling the development of the needed skills: this is an overarching objective for the tyre 
and rubber sector. Given the mix of skills in the sector (mechanical, chemical, electronic and 
digital) this need will become more acute with the development of new/alternative supply 
chains.   
 
 
Actions in the wider EU remit
Ensure consistency, ambition, and coherence in the regulatory action  
 
The EU is taking leading role in a number of global challenges, in particular, with regard to 
Environment, Social and Governance issues. This is coherent with the direction that our industry has 
taken both as sector level – particularly by setting up the Global Partnership additional inputs to the 
Sustainable Natural Rubber – and at company level, with actions listed in the Roadmap, that 
encompass the entire value chain.  
With a growing number of EU Regulations in this field, it is becoming increasingly necessary to 
ensure their coherence, both in terms of objectives – to avoid duplication – and level of ambition, 
particularly when it comes to impacting raw materials produced outside the EU.  
 
Enhance cooperation with third countries both with regards to ensuring the supply of the 
necessary raw materials, and to ensure that these are obtained in line with the EU ambitions with 
regards to ESG objectives. 
When it comes to materials that are sourced outside the EU, the international context should be 
used in order to secure both security of supply, but also to ensure that the sourcing is done in 
coherence with EU ESG objectives. To this end, the new FTAs offer an important tool and the 
sourcing of key raw materials should be included in these agreements.

ANNEX – Additional inputs to the Roadmap’ actions 
 
 
 
Determining strategic critical raw materials based on pre-set criteria 
The criteria developed to select critical raw materials are a good starting point, but they are not 
sufficient to effectively foster the resilience of the tyre and rubber supply chain. The strategic role of 
certain raw materials for an entire ecosystem from the point of view of the downstream users 
should also be considered. 
To this end, further qualitative criteria should be added to the analysis.  
 
With the production of some of the raw materials necessary to our industry in only a few regions 
for reasons that are different and belong to each material, there is the necessity to mitigate current 
supply challenges and prevent future ones, by breaking this strategic geographic dependency. The 
increasingly fragile geopolitical situation as well as the recent trade disruptions also connected to 
the “shipping-container crisis” make this approach all the more urgent.  
 
These criteria should be complemented by measures enhancing the development and use of 
secondary raw materials, in order to sustain circularity in key strategic applications. 
 
Carbon Black is one example of a material currently explored in our industry for recovering/recycling 
in order to reduce dependency on virgin raw materials. This example offers a clear illustration of 
how the regulatory framework should be more supportive of secondary materials.  
Recovered Carbon Black (rCB) will potentially partially substitute virgin Carbon Black (vCB). A one on 
one substitution is not possible with the current technological status. As recovered Carbon Black 
must comply with all current regulations applicable to vCB in each country to allow its use in 
different applications, there is the need to set EU-harmonised End of Waste criteria for end-of-life 
tyre-derived secondary raw materials. This would result in their inclusion in the scope of the REACH 
regulation, leading to a level-playing field, both in terms of harmonisation of rules across the EU and 
enabling fair competition with virgin raw materials 
Furthermore, clearly recognising chemical recycling in EU regulatory framework as a recycling 
operation based on quality output material would help investments and access to permits for this 
technology. Eventually, this will lead to a higher quality of secondary raw materials and increased 
market uptake. 
 
In the mid- to long-term, these actions could create a new domestic alternative supply chain, 
leading to higher EU autonomy on key raw materials in the tyre and rubber production.  
 
Improving the EU’s monitoring, risk management and governance in the field of CRM

critical raw materials selected through the process above. However, the new geopolitical situation

Build the necessary regulatory and financing framework to strengthen the EU’s critical and 
strategic raw materials value chain 
Natural rubber is widely used in the rubber industry and is listed as a Critical Raw Material for the 
EU.  Equally indispensable materials for the production of rubber goods and tyres are e.g. synthetic 
rubber and carbon black. The current geopolitical situation has revealed vulnerabilities regarding the 
availability of these materials.  This demonstrated the need for Member States to become more 
resource independent and increase the recoverability and use of recycled materials from tyres. 
Before the recent war against Ukraine, Russia represented a significant supply source for carbon 
black and synthetic rubber. Pre-war, Russia supplied over 500,000 tonnes of carbon black and about 
50,000 tonnes come from Ukraine each year, totalling more than 50% of the total European 
demand. A diversification of supply is needed.  
We recommend the upcoming EU Critical Raw Materials Act to take into account these resources.  
Tyre Derived Oil, which can partially substitute the fossil-based feedstock that is normally used to 
produce polymers or Carbon Black, and recycled Carbon Black can be obtained through chemical 
recycling of End of Life Tyres. This could be a viable recycling option in the mid-to long-term for 
alternative European supply of critical raw materials, and our sector is exploring feasibilities.  
 Defining priorities and objectives for EU actions and identifying strategic projects for funding 
and permitting in the EU that have the potential to help secure the EU’s diversified access to 
critical raw materials and have strong environmental and social performance. 
 Develop End -of-Waste (EoW) criteria for end -of-life tyres -derived material.   This could 
enhance reuse and recycling of material derived from end-of-life tyres, which might well lead 
in the mid- to long-term to more domestic alternative supply chain, bringing about more EU 
autonomy on key raw materials in tyre and rubber production.   
 Recognizing chemical recycling as a clear recycling operation based on quality output 
material. This will ensure legal certainty for investments in research and development in 
recycling technologies and business cases. The waste hierarchy places more preferred options 
above less preferred options in terms of treatment. For the development and investment 
opportunity it is important that those pyrolysis processes that can meet the criteria for the 
output material flows and be deemed recycling are categorized as a recycling operation.  
 Ensure a sustainable level playing  field across the Single Marke t. An EU-wide EoW-status 
addresses currently diverging national regulations on waste streams. For rubber products and 
tyres a well-performing recycling market exists already. Harmonization of EU rubber and End-
of-Life-Tyres (ELT) markets -in terms of EoW  status- would support innovative recycling 
technologies and higher degrees of circularity in these sectors, by rectifying and preventing 
market imperfections and creating a single recycling market. Therefore, it is a strong 
contribution to the overarching object ives set in The European Green Deal to speed up the 
transition towards a circular economy and achieve climate neutrality by 2050, while 
supporting the release of the pressure on critical raw materials like natural rubber. High levels

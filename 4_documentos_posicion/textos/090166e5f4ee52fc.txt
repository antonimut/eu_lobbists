Introduction 
The Proposal by the European Commission for a new directive on liability for defective products 
aims to regain legal clarity through adapting civil  liability rules to the new digital reality . 
SMEunited fully supports the proposal and its aim to increase product safety.  
However, an excessive easing of the burden of proof for the consumer as it is foreseen will lead 
to a significant increase in claims for damages against manufacturers and distributors. This 
will even more so affect SMEs, who do not have in-house legal service or capital reserves. 
 
Key messages  
• SMEunited welcomes the proposal as updated liability rules are necessary to 
account for the necessities of a digitalised world and a circular economy. 
• The proposal must take into account that an increased liability comes together 
with higher prices for European products. 
• Entrepreneurs require clear rules in order to make their business decisions. The 
proposal lacks clarity regarding some notions such as on the definition of 
defectiveness. Fair rules are important for SMEs  because they do not have the 
capacities or the capital reserves to deal  with a sharp increase in the number of 
claims. 
• This will come true without a 500 Euro threshold for damage claims and a n 
extensive definition of defective products. 
• Finally, it is not legitimate to make every economic actor liable for a product, even 
if they - by nature of their operation – have no influence on the defectiveness of 
the product.

SMEunited welcomes clarifications on software and 
repairs 
The proposal by the Commission includes liability also for digital manufacturing files and 
software. This extension will significantly change the scope of the directive. We therefore urge 
the Commission to investigate the effects of this extension , e.g., whether or in which cases 
knowledge content (geographical maps in a navigation service in an autonomous vehicle or via 
a mobile phone app) is to be considered software or a component of software.  
Still, we welcome the fact that there will be clarity on how software is to be treated following the 
implementation of this directive. Treating the developer or producer of software, including AI 
system providers as a manufacturer, irrespective of whether the software is purchased as a 
standalone product or already stored on a device, seems reasonable.  
The liability rules set out in the proposal are fair to repairers. Simple or conventional repairs are 
not considered a substantial change. This is vital for the repairers as they are mainly SMEs, but 
also for progress towards a circular economy. It is also only reasonable that a substantial 
modification to a product comes along with liability for the product.  
 
1. On damages (Article 4.6) 
The proposal opens claims  for small amounts,  contrary to the existing Directive from 1985 
which has a threshold of 500 Euros. As a consequence, the number of s uch actions for small 
amounts will increase significantly. This can very quickly lead to insolvency for SMEs.  
Therefore, SMEunited insists a threshold must be maintained. If one takes inflation since 1985 
as a basis, this would correspond to the current equivalent of more than 1,000 Euros, i.e. more 
than double. Thus, maintaining the existing limit of 500 euros is already a strong reduction 
compared to the original value. 
 
2. On defectiveness (Article 6)  
Article 6 introduces several new circumstances to the central concept of defectiveness. These 
new circumstances however do not contribute to defining defectiveness at all. 
For considering a product defective, Article 6 (e) asks to take into account “the moment in time 
when the product was placed on the market or put into service or, where the manufacturer 
retains control over the product after that moment, the moment in time when the product left 
the control of the manufacturer”. This part does not clearly determine whether a product is 
defective or not.

Furthermore, measuring defectiveness against the “specific expectations of the end -users” 
(Article 6 (h) ) works completely against the objective of more clarity on rules.  Why should 
specific expectations, i.e. a subjective factual criterion that does not even have to be agreed, be 
significant in the case of strict liability? 
Overall, introducing new circumstances without being specific on  what happens in what 
case, creates legal uncertainty for all market participants. 
 
3. On economic operators’ liability for defective products (Article 7) 
Regarding the liability of economic operators , the proposal include s all companies that are 
involved with the product, even for a short period of time, for the benefit of the consumer.  
SMEunited strongly disagree s with the proposal  in this regard . For example, "fulfilment 
service providers" as defined in Art 4 (14), unlike producers, do not manufacture the product or 
have an actual opportunity to have an influence on the defectiveness of the product. Fulfilment 
service providers do not even consciously select the product, such as traders. They only provide 
services for any products. 
By the nature of their economic operation, not every economic actor can be held liable in the 
same way. This is disproportionate and not legitimate. Thus Article 7 needs to be revised.  
Additionally, in Article 7 (5), the proposal illustrates a case where neither the manufacturer, nor 
the importer are established within the EU , but fails to explain what happens when  “(a) the 
claimant requests that distributor to identify the economic operator or the person who supplied 
the distributor with the product” and the distributor complies.    
This adds legal uncertainty rather than reducing it when products turn out defective.  As the 
extension of liability only applies to European operators, consumers might face higher prices 
from European manufacturers and distributors.  
 
4. On the burden of proof (Article 9) 
The shift of the burden of proof is not acceptable. Since the proposal introduces a strict liability 
irrespective of who is at fault, there is no need to shift the burden of proof at all. 
In most cases, none of the market participants can prove the opposite, since they can no longer 
dispose of the product . In other cases, m any market participants will not have the necessary 
information or knowledge to prove a fault.

On top of being unnecessary, shifting the burden of proof is also not feasible for most 
businesses. Presuming a causal link will only lead to an increase in unreasonable claims for 
damages against manufacturers and distributors. 
Ultimately, the wording of the provisions on this point is very vague (‘it is likely...’, ‘can 
reasonably be expected…’) and inconsistent with the principle of legal certainty. 
 
5. Exemption from liability (Article 10) 
The current proposal allows o nly manufacturers to invoke the exemption from liability based 
on an  objective state of scientific and technical knowledge. This restriction to the producer 
discriminates against all those other economic operators who can also be held liable and is 
therefore unobjective and discriminatory. Instead of “manufacturer”, the term “economic 
operators” should be used. 
 
6. Limitation periods (Article 14) 
Article 14 of the proposal introduces a limitation period of 10 years. SMEun ited worries that 
such a long period jeopardises l egal certainty for compan ies. Considering the capacities of 
SMEs and in comparison to other limitation periods ,10 years is already long and should, as 
such, include prolonged illness. 
 
7. Transition and implementation period (Article 18 and 19)  
The directive's 12-month implementation period appears unrealistic. SMEunited suggests a 24 
months implementation period, which would also be in line with the other package directive (AI 
Liability Directive). 
For businesses to be able to adapt,  the directive should foresee a transition period of at least 
one year.

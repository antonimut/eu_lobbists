Introduction 
Digitalisation makes it necessary to take appropriate measures to contain cybersecurity 
incidents. Business interruptions caused by  cyberattacks lead to detrimental damage for the 
affected companies.

Key messages  
• We welcome the proposal on horizontal cybersecurity requirements as it includes several 
of SMEunited’s preliminary requests. Indeed, a patchwork of rules would hamper the 
ability of manufacturers of digital products , especially of small and medium size,  to 
operate and scale up across European markets. 
• The provision of simplified assessment requirements for non -critical products and the 
taking into account of enterprise size concerning compliance assessment fees are 
welcomed.  Given the cross -cutting nature of the regulation, compliance burdens  
however may be particularly onerous for small operators. 
• The obligations of retailers appear very stringent and, in particular, we negatively evaluate 
some of the obligations of distributors and importers as burdensome and incoherent with 
their technical and technological skills.  
• Mandatory requirements represent an  additional major burden, especially for SMEs. 
Requirements must be proportionate.  
• Awareness raising also among the public will be necessary to justify the additional costs.  
• To ensure legal certainty, the requirements must also be defined precisely.

Micro and small enterprises, in fact, often lack the necessary technical skills to evaluate

1. Scope 
The proposal on horizontal cybersecurity requirements  (CRA) should clearly define 
cybersecurity. The definition “Products with digital elements shall be designed, developed and 
produced in such a way that they ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity based on the 
risks” is too general and fail to bring clarity on what is required.  
Cybersecurity is considered a dynamic environment where attack methods, threats, and 
functionality of products and services evolve over ti me. Therefore, a risk classification from 
lower to higher risk  in addition to the “ default” self -assessment by the manufacturer, and 
where appropriate a third -party assessment according to an evaluation methodology, has 
been rightly introduced. 
The type of products falling under its scope since the market access and market surveillance 
rules are closely linked to liability rules and will directly affect manufacturers entering or staying 
in the EU market. It must be ensured that no further obligations are created in the supply chain, 
to the effect that downstream users are directly or indirectly obliged to stop using certain 
products. 
 
2. Consistency with other legislations in the digital 
field 
Consistency with other legislations in the digital field is paramount, in particular with the Data 
Act. It will also be important to avoid overlaps with other existing regulatory provisions. 
 The aspirations of the CRA must be compatible with the data access requirements of the 
“Access by d esign” concept introduced in the Data Act as a  mandatory standard for all 
products and must not be at the expense of sharing data of IoT products. Actual or pretended

fears of security, liability or cost of compliance could indeed lead manufacturers to design their 
connected products intentionally in a way to make only a minimum amount of data available.  
“Security by design“ and “access by design” rules cannot become two competing principles. 
This could significantly contradict the goal of fair data sharing. 
 
3. Obligations of importers and distributors 
A uniform set of requirements is in the interest of importers and distributors of digital 
products, since this would allow a reduced and simplified process of verifying a product’s 
cybersecurity.  
The Regulation would however also set new obligations for importers and distributors. As most 
importers and especially distributors are SMEs, these obligations should be proportionate 
and take into account their capacities to assess whether a product and its manufacturer 
live up to the essential cybersecurity requirements . The liability of distributors to ensure 
cybersecurity verifications must be limited. 
SMEunited also believe s some of the additional obligations brought by the proposal can be 
simplified. Regarding article 14.2.b, which requires distributors to verify that the manufacturer 
and the importer have complied with the obligations set out in Articles 10 (10 ), 10(11) and 
13(4), this could be simplified by requiring producers and importers to provide a declaration 
demonstrating their compliance.  
Article 14.4 requires actions from distributors who have reasons to believe that a product (…) is 
not in conformity with essential requirements of the Regulation. The same applies to importers 
in article 13.  
The notion of ‘having reason to believe’ misses a common understanding of the required extent 
of reason and potentially proof thereof . It should be clarified that this does not include the 
obligation to proactively carry out research , and is limited to the technological means and 
expertise of the entrepreneur . Indeed, there is no assurance that importers and distributors 
have sufficient access to information related to the action taken to ensure the cybersecurity of 
the product.  
The same reflection applies concerning article 14.6 which states that ‘when the distributor of a 
product with digital elements becomes aware that the manufacturer of that product ceases is 
operations and (…) is not able to comply with the obligations’. Retailers have little to no means 
to know whether a manufacturer can update its systems or not if the latter does not provide 
them with the information. This obligation should apply to manufacturers only.

Lastly, the Regulation should include compensation mechanisms if distributors or importers 
are forbidden to place products on the market or forced to recall products that are no longer 
compliant due to a cyber-security vulnerability for which the manufacturer is responsible.  
 
4. Obligations of manufacturers  
Although only a limited number of digital products would need to undergo a more rigorous 
third-party conformity assessment, it can be foreseen that insufficient assessment resources in 
notified bodies can become a bottleneck in some Member States, hamperin g the access to 
markets. The Commission and Member States should proactively promote and support 
conformity assessment bodies and ensure manufacturers can have their legal obligations 
fulfilled without undue delay.  
The obligations the Regulation would imp ose on manufacturers of digital products are not 
insignificant and are likely to burden them with new compliance -related costs. As the burden 
is relatively larger for small and medium-sized manufacturers (included start-ups, who play 
a key role in this market field), the EU and national authorities should make every effort to 
alleviate it by ensuring the costs of third -party compliance assessments remain SME -
friendly and by providing SME-focused guidance. 
A risk-based approach to conformity assessment proce dures is a guarantee that digital 
products with more critical security implications should undergo stricter procedures. However, 
a strict classification with a list of high-risk products runs the risk of omitting a number of 
critical solutions from the out set and of being constantly outdated in terms of technology 
and its critical use cases.  
Therefore, as an alternative, a use -case-based approach , similar to the proposed AI 
regulation, would be better suited for categorising high-risk. Products whose intended use 
would fall within these parameters would need to be more closely scrutini sed in terms of 
conformity with the essential cybersecurity requirements. 
 
5. CE Marking 
After having conducted the necessary compliance assessment procedur es, the manufacturer 
would be required to draw up an EU declaration of conformity of products with digital elements 
with the essential requirements of the Regulation and to follow up by affixing a visible CE 
marking to products in question. This is only reasonable, given the purpose of the Regulation, 
but does raise some concerns.  
 
 5 of 5 
Many digital products already bear CE markings based on various rules, standards, and 
specifications unrelated to cybersecurity. This can make the CE regime hard to understand for 
end-users, causing unnecessary uncertainty also in terms of the EU -wide cybersecurity 
marking.  
We therefore underline the need to seek ways to improve the identification of the 
cybersecurity-specific CE label.  
 
6. Sanctions 
Penalties of up to EUR 15,000,000 against SMEs are a threat to their existence . Although 
necessary, sanctions should never jeopardise the survival of companies. The Regulation must 
clearly indicate that sanctions are proportionate and include thresholds for SMEs.

Position paper on the initiative for a 
Single Market Emergency Instrument  
COM(2022) 459 final 
 
Introduction 
During the corona crisis, the internal market became fragmented due to uncoordinated travel 
and import restrictions, the lack of predictability and harmonisation of national crisis measures 
and the subsequent disruptions of supply chains. This made it difficult, if not impossible, for 
entrepreneurs to supply their customers with essential goods and services. 
Key messages 
• SMEunited supports t he basic idea of moving away from ad -hoc measures by 
individual governments in crisis situations. 
• However, the draft of the Single Market Emergency Instrument (SMEI) still leaves 
many questions open  and contains obligations for companies that require further 
discussion and change.  
• SME representatives’ participation to the Advisory Group should not be limited to 
only gathering information. 
• The criteria according to which the various crisis modes can be activated are 
formulated far too vaguely. Instead of providing much-needed legal certainty, this 
could have exactly the opposite effect. 
• The necessity and proportionality of certain obligations are insufficiently 
substantiated to restrict the freedom of enterprise to this extent. 
• The instrument should not only be about sanctions, but bring forward incentives 
for companies. For instance, the strategic reserves measure could be complemented 
with a mention of fiscal or financial incentives.  
• The SMEI should focus more on joint EU public procurement of crisis -relevant 
goods and services as the way out of any crisis 
• The mechanism of the  SMEI must remain of temporary application  and cannot 
become the rule.

The Single Market is Europe's strongest asset , and the outbreak of the COVID pandemic had 
very suddenly highlighted the structural deficiencies and existing obstacles. It is therefore vital 
to protect the European market in times of crisis , and this is why SMEunited support s the 
general idea of a coordination tool at the European  level to ensure the economic resilience 
and the integrity of the freedoms of movements  of the Single Market  in an emergency 
scenario.  
The SMEI is intended to be suitable not only for further pandemics, but for crises of all kinds 
that could restrict the functioning of the internal market.  However, in order to do  so, the 
proposal is broadly and partly vaguely formulated, which could lead to misunderstandings, 
especially in times of crisis  and emergencies . The necessity and proportionality of the 
obligations are insufficiently substantiated, causing legal uncertainty for companies and supply 
chain managers.  
Lastly, in its current form, the proposal lacks an adequate democratic control of the power given 
to the Commission. 
 
1. On the consistency with other instruments 
Mechanisms to be used in time of crisis must be clear and unambiguous. Thus, the SMEI should 
ensure coordination with already existing EU policies.  It is crucial that the SMEI is coordinated 
closely with the EU trade policy in order to maintain supply chains that avoid additional cost, 
notably due to tariffs, for the European enter prises. Moreover, the SMEI must be coordinated 
with the Green Deal and potential bottlenecks the subsequent legislative initiatives could bring 
with them. In particular, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, the proposed Sustainable 
Products Regulation as well as the planned European Critical Raw materials Act have to be 
taken into account.  
Based on the legal principle " lex specialis derogat generalibus ", legal acts comprising a 
sectoral framework for crisis response and preparedness take precedence over the SMEI, but 
this general principle does not provide sufficient legal certainty. 
Lastly, to ensure the effectiveness of the instrument, the relationship with other consultation 
mechanisms such as the Political Crisis Response Mechanism (IPCR) and the Single Market 
Enforcement Taskforce (SMET) needs to be clarified.

2. On the definitions  
An instrument such as the SMEI must c ome together with clear definitions. In SMEunited ’s 
views, the definitions in Article 3 should include the definitions of economic operators and 
institutions that are in the scope of this Regulation.  
Furthermore, article 3.3 defines a "Single Market emergency” as ‘a wide-ranging impact of a 
crisis on the Single Market that severely disrupts the free movement on the Single Market or the 
functioning of the supply chains that are indispensable in the maintenance of vital  societal or 
economic activities in the Single Market.’ 
The definition does not clearly state whether these impacts must be cumulative, or whether 
it is already sufficient that only one of th e "wide-ranging impacts" enumerated exists. Also, 
nothing is mentioned on how many Member States should be impacted for the emergency to 
be declared. 
Regarding the definition of goods and services and of crisis -relevant goods and services of 
articles 3.5 and 3.6, SMEunited believes they are defined in a very general way. This might be 
justified as the SMEI should cover different types of crisis, but a clear definition of the term 
"crisis-relevant goods and services" is essential in order to clarify which goods and services 
are included, notably when it comes to certain raw materials. Indeed based upon the criteria 
that emerge , companies can evaluate which systemic value chains (upstream and 
downstream) are affected and necessary to be activated.  
 
3. On the advisory group  
An advisory group consisting of the Commission and Member States will be set up. Among 
other things, the advisory group will monitor the internal market and recommend the most 
appropriate response measures.  
Important stakeholders, such as the economic interest groups , participate in the advisory 
group at best as observers and are thus only insufficiently involved in the processes. Especially 
with such far -reaching interventions in the economy, the representatives should be given a 
greater say. SME representatives have an important role to play in time of crisis an d cannot 
only be consulted to gather information. Indeed public -private partnerships are essential to 
strengthen supply chains in the event of a crisis.   
The advisory group must be more consulted in the decision -making process. The Commission 
often appears as the sole decision -maker under the mechanisms of the SMEI. Involving the 
advisory group in more steps of the decision -making process will ensure more informed, fair 
and effective decisions.

4.  On economic operators’ obligations 
• The monitoring obligations and strategic reserves 
The vigilance mode will require businesses to monitor supply chains of goods and services of 
strategic importance. Reporting obligations and approval procedures must ensure they do not 
bring additional unnecessary burden. The standardised means to collect information must be 
ready when the Regulation enters into force, must be user friendly, and not require 
entrepreneurs to use resources that are useful elsewhere. In addition, the Commission must 
ensure it applies the Only Once principle.  
Moreover, regarding the strategic reserves, the proposal does not answer the question of the 
financial responsibility coming with the stockpiling obligations , nor  how stocks will be  
distributed in the event of a crisis. Moreover, mandatory stockpiling may lead to higher prices 
and market disruption . The use of public procurement is not mentioned, although this is a 
possible approach. The OECD's advice on a voluntary contribution by companies in 
stockpiling is furthermore ignored.1 
Additionally, Member States will be required to identify the 'most relevant economic operators' 
within the relevant strategic supply chains . Such a list of most relevant market participants 
raises fundamental questions in terms of signals to the market and the impact on companies 
from a competition perspective.  
• The priority rated orders   
During the Covid crisis, companies voluntarily started to manufacture masks  and  to provide 
strategic goods as much as they could. The SMEI would introduce a legal framew ork to these 
voluntary solidarity actions and this must be realistic regarding what it brings in the market. 
Such obligation could be counterproductive and miss the goal of protecting the single 
market.  
When prioritising orders, companies will often have to break already existing contracts, and 
some companies will be deprived of products they already paid for, or that they strongly need 
to survive. 
Additionally, economic operators being freed from contractual obligations to comply with the 
priority rated orders would only apply to obligations under European Law. Companies with 
obligations in third countries could therefore find themselves in the position of having to pay

damages under foreign law . This signal sen t to trading partners cannot underpin European 
businesses attractivity and competitiveness.  
In case the Commission requires companies to accept priority orders  of crisis-relevant goods 
and products, it is essential to have clear criteria that justify such an interference with economic 
freedom.  
• Ramping up production 
The Commission and Member States would also be entitled under the SMEI to require a 
company to ramp up its production . The Regulation must take into account that for some 
companies, ramping up production means using resources they don’t have, and thus would 
lead to insolvency and market disruption.  
 
5. On the national crisis measures 
SMEunited believes i t is positive that the proposal contains a framework that national crisis 
measures should comply with. However, in the current proposal, measures that restrict the free 
movement seem to be allowed unless they do not meet the conditions of the framework. This 
positive formulation seems to conflict with European law because restrictions to free movement 
are in principle not allowed under the Treaties unless justified by a public interest.  For this 
reason, the framework should be amended so that restrictions on free movement are in 
principle explicitly prohibited unless the restrictions comply with the framework for national 
crisis measures (negative formulation).  
Regarding the measures categorically banned, SMEunited welcome s the list which include s 
measures such as intra-EU export bans, measures of eq uivalent effect and measures that 
discriminate between member states or citizens. We believe this list protects the integrity of 
the internal market during a crisis. A supplementary reference in the regulation to the need to 
take into account relevant upst ream and downstream goods and services when drawing up 
the lists would be desirable. 
We also welcome the fact that Member States must notify proposals for national crisis 
measures to the Commission. It is also necessary that stakeholders, including businesses and 
consumers, are informed about national crisis measures in a timely manner and that 
stakeholders can react to the national measures.

6. On the sanctions  
It is deplorable that a policy is based on threat of fines to force compliance, without offering 
any incentives. If a crisis occurs, the impact on companies will be huge, and the SMEI does 
not take this aspect into account at all. Companies will already be struggling, and the foreseen 
fines notably for incomplete information are counterproductive and disproportionate.  
The last crisis have indeed shown that without unnecessary administrative burden, companies 
are really fast to provide the necessary help. However under the SMEI, t he fines foreseen will 
be very high for economic operators that do not comply with their information obligations . It 
must be remembered that these new costs would be passed on to consumers in order to ensure 
economic survival.  
Moreover, based on the proposal, it is unclear when fines can be imposed . For instance, it i s 
ambiguous whether fines can only be imposed when companies accept requests to share 
information and prioritise supplies or whether fines can also be imposed if they refuse a request 
by the Commission to provide information or prioritise orders. 
Ultimately, the concept of 'gross negligence' (Article 28) should be better specified. Its content 
should be defined under the present proposal and related to the size of the economic operator. 
 
7. On the overall power of the Commission  
The proposal gives th e Commission a 'carte blanche' to secure the supply of strategic goods 
and services through extraordinary powers and by means of delegated and implementing acts..  
Regarding the significant power given to the Commission under the SMEI, it would only be fair 
that the SMEI foresees a liability mechanism, under which the Commission should go in front 
of the European Parliament to justify the activation of the different modes and the result of 
the actions taken . Setting up a monitoring ad -hoc Committee within t he Parliament would 
guarantee an independent oversight.

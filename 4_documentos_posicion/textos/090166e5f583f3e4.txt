Position on the evaluation of the 2013 ADR Directive 
Introduction: Amazon aims to be Earth’s most customer -centric company. Amazon’s complaint handling processes 
are built to be easy to access (including for users with disabilities), user-friendly, non-discriminatory and to enable and 
facilitate the submission of precise and substantiated complaints.  Complaints are handled in a timely, diligent and 
objective manner and, where possible, within ten days starting on the date on which Amazon received the complaint.  
An appeal path against an initial decision is always available to the customer and an appealed decision will involve 
human interaction. The customer can choose to be called back (for free) or to be put in touch via real time electronic 
chat with an agent . Amazon’s chat channel is asynchronous, meaning that customers can leave the app for a few 
hours and come back to pick  up the conversation at any moment. Amazon’s agents are always available and have 
been given the training, tools and support they need to answer all types of contacts.   Amazon does not put time 
pressure on interactions with customers, giving each agent the  opportunity to spend as much time as needed with 
every customer to resolve their support request. Our customer service agents can escalate a complaint to an 
escalation team who are experienced in complex disputes. Throughout the process Amazon remains cus tomer 
obsessed and will, if in doubt, err on the side of the customer.  
It is against that background that Amazon sets out its position in relation to the Commission’s consultation on the 
evaluation of the Directive on alternative dispute resolution (“ADR”) for consumers disputes (Directive 2013/11/EU). 
Our position in a few sentences:  
A trader’s own processes are the first access point for consumers, and in the vast majority of cases are the mechanism 
by which issues will be resolved. These internal processes ensure a swift resolution of most customer issues and 
thereby serve as a filtering tool so that only genuine disputes end up in the ADR service.   
Amazon is fully supportive of consumers having access to simple, efficient, fast and low -cost ADR services after they 
have extinguished the available internal dispute resolution processes and appeal paths of the trader. However, 
without the initial filtering process , there is a very real risk that ADR bodies across the EU would not be equipped to 
handle a high volume of non-dispute related inquiries that traders receive and quickly resolve for the benefit of their 
customers.  In addition, traders are increasingly facing  fraudulent claims. The updated framework should  include 
safeguards protecting traders from being required to engage in ADR with consumers who (i) have repeatedly violated 
the trader’s terms and conditions, (ii) are avoiding the trader’s internal complaint handling processes, (iii) are bringing 
repeated, frivolous or vexatious claims, or (iv) are attempting to escalate a dispute that is not appropriate for 
settlement, for example, because it involves fraud.   
Why do we suggest this approach:  
The EU Commission’s assessment of the Directive dated 25 September 2019 noted that EU consumers have access to 
high-quality ADR procedures across the Union and in virtually all retail sectors, regardless of whether the dispute is 
domestic or cross -border and whether the  purchase was made online or offline. However, it identified  three main 
challenges as limiting the framework’s full effectiveness: 
- ADR awareness and perceptions 
- Navigating the national ADR landscapes 
- Traders’ participation in ADR procedures 
The first two should be addressed through consumer awareness campaigns, improvements to the ODR process and 
the simplification of the national ADR landscapes and their accessibility.  As for the latter,  mandatory ADR would not 
be appropriate in every case and may severely disrupt the trader’s internal processes by unintentionally encouraging

consumers to bypass the trader’s customer services, resulting in a worse service (and outcome) for consumers. ADR 
bodies should keep a focus on disputes rather than general inquiries.  
As set out in Article 23 of the Directive, the Directive should only apply after the consumer-complaint handling systems 
of the trader. It should remain the last resort for genuine consumer complaints before court -based proceedings are 
commenced. 
What should a Trader’s customer services / complaint handling process include? 
A trader’s customer services /complaint handling system should: 
- First, allow customers to solve their issues independently, via clear pathways and menus, designed to 
effectively assist customers in finding the solution to their query.  
- Keep the framework proportional, traders should be allowed to innovate in how cus tomers can reach a 
representative where the automated pathways are exhausted. In order to ensure such requirements do not 
become a barrier to various business models, the framework should not be prescriptive.  
- Provide the option for customers to escalate t he matter to specially trained customer service experts who 
are able to handle more complex cases, in the rare case of a dispute.  
- Deliver additional solutions to customers of intermediaries who have purchased from third party sellers, 
which means that if customers do not receive a reply from third party sellers within a certain time frame (e.g. 
48 hours), the customer is reimbursed by the intermediary, with the intermediary then taking on the 
responsibility for recovery from the third-party seller.  
- Allow an appeal route for customers against decisions taken by the trader. 
ADR service criteria 
In the rare case that a resolution is not possible after the customer has been through the trader’s complaint handling 
process, Amazon recognizes that there can be value in engaging in ADR  but ADR should remain a voluntary 
mechanism. The vast majority of complaints will not be actual disputes and can be solved by traders directly with 
customers.   
To ensure the system’s cost effectiveness is improved, there  should be criteria to determine which complaints are 
suitable for the ADR service.  Unless the trader consents to the ADR request, the customer should be required to 
demonstrate to the ADR service that  they/he/she has engaged with or has attempted to engage with th e trader’s 
internal complaints handling processes including its appeal route before the case can be accepted to the ADR service.  
This would ensure that customers are not bypassing the trader’s complaint handling processes or bringing frivolous 
or vexatious claims to the ADR service.  
When ADR is not appropriate 
Safeguards should be included in the following instances:  
- Not engaging with the trader: Where a claimant has refused to engage with the trader or has bypassed part 
or all of the trader’s complain t handling processes (given that such processes ought to resolve most issues 
and would avoid frivolous complaints or basic information requests inundating the ADR service).   
 
- Repeat ADR requests:  Where a claimant has lodged repeated unsuccessful requests to the ADR service on

Other considerations 
Costs of the ADR service 
It is noted that Article 41 of the Directive states that ADR procedures should preferably be free of charge for the 
consumer but that if costs are to be applied, the ADR procedure should be accessible, attractive and inexpensive for 
consumers (and should not exceed a nominal fee).   
Amazon recognises the importance of access to justice. Clearly the costs of any ADR service should not be prohibitive 
for consumers. However, making the trader bear the costs for ADR regardless of the outcome of the dispute coul d 
invite frivolous and vexatious claims given the lack of cost consequence for consumers.  There is a careful balance to 
be struck as, in the majority of cases, it is going to be entirely appropriate for a consumer to not face any costs risks 
of ADR where there is a genuine dispute. However, maintaining the potential risk of costs (for both parties) can serve 
as a very helpful, natural barrier to ensure that consumers do not bring frivolous and vexatious complaints to ADR.  In 
all cases, the ADR costs themselves should be affordable for consumers.   
The criteria for ADR entities 
We would suggest that any natural persons appointed by the ADR service to resolve consumer disputes should meet 
the following criteria: (a) they are impartial and independent; (b) th eir mediation services are affordable for 
consumers; (c) they are capable of providing their mediation services in English as well as the EU language of the 
consumer; (d) they are easily accessible either physically in the place of establishment or residen ce of the consumer, 
or online; (e) they are capable of providing their mediation services without undue delay, i.e. to scale; and (f) they 
have a sufficient understanding of EU consumer law, and the law of the relevant Member States (where applicable).  
Confidentiality and privacy 
As is noted in Article 29 of the Directive confidentiality and consumer privacy remain important considerations which 
need to be protected in any ADR procedure.  ADR bodies should be required to demonstrate that they have adequate 
processes in place to protect consumer information if there is to be a significant uptake in their services.

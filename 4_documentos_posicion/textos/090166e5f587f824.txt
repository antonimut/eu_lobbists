 
 
Brussels, 21 December 2022 
 
EASA’s answer to the call for evidence  
“Consumer rights – adapting out-of-court dispute resolution to digital markets” 
 
 
The European Advertising Standards Alliance (EASA) welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on 
the call for evidence “Consumer rights – adapting out-of-court dispute resolution to digital markets”.  
About the European Advertising Standards Alliance (EASA) 
 
Advertising self-regulatory organisations (SRO) throughout Europe enforce codes of conducts to ensure 
responsible advertising. Collective advertising self-regulatory systems are effective, independent, and 
contribute to the protection of consumers . Thanks to its high flexibility, quick response to consumer 
complaints and adaptability to changing market realities, self -regulation is a suitable tool which 
supplements regulatory instruments ensuring consumer protection . SROs are constantly addressing  
evolving issues and emerging digital marketing techniques, such as influencer marketing.  Collective 
advertising self-regulatory systems help to ensure that traders are aware of their responsibilities, thus 
contributing to higher compliance with the rules regarding  unfair commercial practices. Advertising 
self-regulatory codes are based on the principles enshrined in  the Advertising and Marketing 
Communications Code (ICC Code), complemented by ICC guidance documents called ICC Frameworks. 
The latest revision of the ICC Code made clear that its scope extends to new digital market issues such 
as influencers, bloggers, or vloggers. 
The complaint -handling system operated by EASA’s national SRO members ensures a high level of 
consumer protection to EU citizens.  It covers 96% of the E uropean Union’s population, enabling EU 
consumers to complain to the national SRO in their country and in their own language. On average over 
the past 10 years, the European SROs handled 60’000 complaints per year, and almost 62’000 in 2021. 
The complaint procedures are free of charge to consumers, consumers’ associations and public 
administrations, and provide investigations quickly, with the vast majority of cases being resolved 
within one month1. Whenever a complaint is upheld by the independent and impartial jury responsible 
for interpreting the SRO’s code, the jury decides on sanctions, which may include the obligation to 
amend or withdraw the ad, the publication of decisions that generates adverse publicity for the 
advertiser, compulsory pre-clearance for advertisers who frequently breach the rules, expulsion from 
trade organisations, or in extreme cases referral to the relevant authorities. Principles to ensure sound 
complaint adjudication and handling of complaints by EASA’s SRO members are laid down in EASA’s 
Charter2. 
Cross-border complaints are coordinated by EASA, according to the country-of-origin principle. The 
system, initially set up in 1992 in response to the forthcoming European Single Market, enables the 
smooth provision of answers to all consumers across Europe. 
Finally, EASA’s network also conducts preventive ex -ante actions, such as traini ng, copy -advice and 
monitoring, which aim to tackle the issue of harmful ad content at its root.

The Alternative Dispute Resolution status under Directive 2013/11 
 
The scope of the ADR Directive, as currently established under its Article 2, is limited to domestic and 
cross-border disputes “concerning contractual obligations stemming from sales contracts or service 
contracts”. This scope relying on the notion of contract represents a considerable hurdle for advertising 
self-regulatory organisations taking an interest in the ADR status. In contrast, opening the possibility for 
advertising SROs to apply for ADR status would benefit both consumers, since SROs enable access to 
quick and user -friendly complaint procedure against an ad , and Consumer Protection Authorities . 
However, we would like to stress that this possibility to obtain ADR status should remain optional, and 
that the Directive should not be amended in a way that would make ADR status mandatory in any way. 
In the case where an SRO would acquire ADR status, the status should in any case allow the SRO to 
preserve its self -regulatory nature and not force it into a co -regulatory type of relationship with 
authorities. Moreover, EASA’s present answer should not be interpreted as suggesting that EASA’s SRO 
members commit to seek ADR status in the future , or, for that matter, that EASA would seek to make 
any qualitative distinction within its varied SRO membership based upon having obtained such a status 
or not.   
In order to make it possible for advertising SROs so inclined to apply for ADR status, EASA recommends 
widening the scope of the Directive so as to encompass obligations stemming from sources other than 
contracts, such as codes of conducts in the field of advertising. Some ADR eligibility conditions, which 
only make sense in the case of a contractual relation, should not apply, or be adapted to, non-
contractual cases. For instance, this is the case of the obligation to enable the exchange of information 
between the parties (see Art. 5(2)(d) and Art. 9(1)(a)), or the excessively restrictive time limit in Art. 5(4)(e). 
In principle, EASA welcomes the fact that the Directive lays down  certain requirements ensuring the 
expertise, independence, impartiality, transparency, effectiveness, and fairness of ADR entities. EASA 
itself has established its own guidance for SROs to maintain high quality standards in their operations 
for the benefit of consumers and to promote industry best practice.  
Therefore, EASA consider that  the requirements under the Directive should set minimum standards , 
while stopping short of any detailed organisational prescriptive conditions, which would represent 
excessive burden liable to deter  some organisations that may have an interest in the status,  from 
applying for it.

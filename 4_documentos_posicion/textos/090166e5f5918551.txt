DIGITALEUROPE Response to the  
Commission Proposal on Media Freedom 
in Europe 
 
DIGITALEUROPE welcomes the Commission's Proposal on 
Media Freedom (thereafter ‘Proposal’) and its intention to 
strengthen the EU internal market while guaranteeing 
media freedom across Europe.  
 Key messages 
 DIGITALEUROPE supports the Commission’s efforts to ensure the 
harmonized application of media law across the EU, especially 
regarding provisions on prominence in Member States. 
 DIGITALEUROPE calls for consistency of the new Proposal with 
existing EU legislation such as the Audiovisual Media Services 
Directive (AVMSD) and the Digital Services Act (DSA). 
 We warn against the Proposal’s potential consequences in the fight 
against disinformation. In particular self-declaration of media service 
providers to VLOPs may inadvertently give a level of protection to 
rogue actors. 
 With the objective of guaranteeing technical feasibility, proportionality 
and pluralism, we raise concerns on the right of customisation of user 
interfaces (Article 19).   
 DIGITALEUROPE advocates for coordinated and consistent application 
of audiovisual media law across Europe and to this aim welcomes the 
proposal to establish a new Board for Media Services.

Towards the harmonized application of media law across 
the EU 
Standardisation - Article 15 (4) 
DIGITALEUROPE supports the creation of a legal framework that ensures the 
harmonised application of media law across the EU, instead of differing national 
transpositions that create regulatory fragmentation and barriers to the free 
movement of goods and services in the internal market. 
In this context, DIGITALEUROPE acknowledges the important role international 
standardisation bodies play in guaranteeing a harmonised internal market 
for audiovisual media services and products. We support the intent to foster 
cooperation between all stakeholders in the standardisation process (media 
service providers, regulatory authorities, manufacturers and developers). 
However, we advise against the creation of additional layers to the existing 
process for the creation of harmonised standards.  
However, the term ‘technical standards’ is misleading and too generic. It is not 
clear if the Proposal will lead to the creation of ‘harmonized standards’ or 
‘technical specification’. Contrary to ‘harmonized standards’, which refer to 
agreed standards in European or international standardisation bodies, ‘technical 
specification’ describes a regulation that is issued by the Commission on the 
basis of an explicit authorisation in the law. Based on this, we would like to 
emphasize that the Commission is not authorised to issue technical 
specifications for digital signals, for the design of devices, or for user interfaces 
as mentioned by Article 15 (4).   
Furthermore, the provision of Article 15 (4) remains unclear in its purpose and 
disproportionate in its scope. Digital signals for the reception of audiovisual 
media services are already standardised worldwide (e.g. DVB project); while 
mandating the standardisation of the devices' design and user interfaces could 
negatively impact competition, obstacle entrepreneurial freedom and restrict 
innovation.  
Partial Minimum harmonisation - Article 1(3) 
Article 1 (3) provides for the possibility for Member States to adopt more detailed 
rules in the fields covered by Chapter II and Section 5 of Chapter III. Such a 
clause might obstacle to the efforts towards market harmonization if Member 
States adopt divergent rules beyond those detailed rules in the referred sections 
of the proposal. On the contrary, all other provisions not mentioned by Article

Establishment of the European Board for Media Services - Article 8 
DIGITALEUROPE advocates for coordinated and consistent application of 
audiovisual media law across Europe and to this aim we support the objective of 
the new Board to promote a consistent application of national rules 
implementing the AVMSD throughout the Union (art. 12). Indeed, diverging 
implementations of the Directive by Member States endangers the single market 
and the provision of cross-border media services. However, the competences of 
the European Board for Media Services must remain clearly distinct from the 
competences of the national authorities. 
Consistent and comprehensive adoption of guidelines 
Guidance on media regulation matters - Article 15 (b)    
DIGITALEUROPE welcomes the intention behind the provision of Art 15 (b) and 
calls the Commission to ensure the coherent application of the legal framework 
to avoid divergent implementation and enforcement by regulatory bodies. 
DIGITALEUROPE has been actively following the transposition Art 7a of the 
Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD), which led to the creation of 
prominence rules in Member States and caused fragmentation of the Internal 
Market.    
In fact, several Member States (e.g. Germany and France) have introduced or 
are in the process of introducing user interface regulation (based on Art. 7a 
AVMSD) that would require substantial adaptation of software specifically for 
these markets.  
In this context, DIGITALEUROPE calls for initiatives which ensure the coherent 
transposition of prominence obligations with respect to user interfaces of 
devices. With this purpose, the industry will support the European Commission 
and the Board in drafting a guideline document with a list of recommendations on 
specific areas that could benefit from harmonization, in case of prominence 
measures according to Art 7a of the AVMSD are implemented by the Member 
States. 
Finally, the non-binding nature of the guidelines that will be issued by the new 
Board, might only have a limited effect on the convergent implementation of the 
AVMSD. Market Fragmentation can only be reduced by the comprehensive 
adoption of the upcoming guidelines on prominence by Member States.

Right of customisation of audiovisual media offer - Article 
19 
DIGITALEUROPE supports the main objective of the Proposal, namely, to 
guarantee freedom and pluralism of the media. However, the proposed right of 
customization might not contribute to achieving such a goal and instead have 
undesired side effects.  
First, the obligation provided by Article 19 for manufacturers and OS developers 
to provide the full right of customization to the user does not take technical 
feasibility or pay-tv environments into account. In fact, technological 
preconditions to fulfil the regulatory requirements, like standardised signalling 
supported by content providers and labelling of services as general interest 
services for prominence, must be tackled before any regulation can be enforced. 
A right to rearrange or sort the media offer on user interfaces should only apply 
provided that its implementation is technically possible and it could be realized 
with a proportionate effort. 
Moreover, more clarity is needed in defining the scope of such provision, which 
appears to include software, as well as hardware configuration as suggested in 
Recital 37. National adaptations of software already allow the user to find and 
access local services. As a matter of fact, user interfaces provided by smart TV 
and set-top-box manufacturers already allow this today. On the contrary, any 
provisions mandating changes in the hardware of certain products impose an 
obvious unreasonable additional burden for manufacturers of devices, which 
operate in a highly competitive and innovative horizontal open market.  
Furthermore, the provision contradicts the overall purpose of the regulation. In 
fact, excluding from this provision, national measures implementing Art 7a of the 
AVMSD could lead to the opposite of guaranteeing media plurality and freedom 
of choice, by strictly enforcing the so-called “services of general interests” on 
user interfaces in each country rather than just granting them prominence. For 
instance, a Spanish citizen living in Poland (and not speaking polish) would have 
no choice but to be forced to see national public polish services on her home 
screen all the time. This goes against the principle of pluralism notably in 
cases where the government has a significant influence on public broadcast 
services. 
Also, DIGITALEUROPE cautions that in the case of PayTV operator provided 
set-top boxes, allowing complete user interface customisation by the end-user 
could result in reducing the editorial independence of that pay-TV providers. Pay-
TV operators order and present content and services based on editorial 
decisions like the relevance of current trends or topics, e.g. sports during World 
Cup, public services due to state funerals, and entertainment during big releases. 
Customers specifically subscribe to pay-TV services for their offering so there 
should be a presumption of users trusting curation.

Content of media service providers on very large online 
platforms - Article 17 
DIGITALEUROPE calls for consistency of the new Proposal with the already 
existing comprehensive EU legislation such as the AVMSD or the Digital 
Services Act (DSA) in regard to provisions on Very large online platforms 
(VLOP). Article 16 of the DSA requires providers of hosting services, including 
providers of online platforms, to put in place easy-to-access and user-friendly 
notice and action mechanisms, and to process notices they receive in a timely, 
diligent, non-arbitrary and objective manner. Under the DSA, providers of hosting 
services, including providers of online platforms, will also be required to give their 
users, including media services providers, clear and specific ‘statements of 
reasons’ why their content has been removed or blocked. Any overlaps or 
conflicts with such a provision should be avoided.  
In this context, Article 17 of the Proposal, aims to improve communication 
between VLOPs and media service providers through a self-declaration 
process. However, allowing outlets to self-declare themselves as media service 
providers could potentially lead to negative outcomes, by being an obstacle to 
any attempts to fight disinformation. As there are limited checks in place to 
ensure that only reputable media service providers can avail of this process, this 
provision could lead to the risk of circumventing VLOPs’ risk mitigation 
obligations in the DSA with respect to preserving civic discourse and countering 
disinformation. This could therefore impede the commitments made by online 
platforms which have signed the EU Code of Practice on Disinformation. 
While media freedom and independence are paramount, a clear understanding 
of who qualifies for media service provider (MSP) is an important critical 
first step to ensure that the rights of MSPs are not abused and potentially lead 
to disinformation. Such a challenge became apparent during the implementation 
of the Copyright Directive, as most Member States have no official definition of 
what qualifies as a media organisation.1 
Additionally, the scope should be clarified to ensure that only online platforms 
whose principle aim is to enable the sharing of audiovisual media content are 
obliged to comply with this article. Otherwise, it risks creating legal uncertainty for 
platforms which connect users for other reasons, e.g. travel, accommodation, 
travel etc.  
Audience measurement - Article 23 
The Proposal does not clarify the rationale behind the provisions on audience 
measurement relating to uses for “planning, production or distribution of content”.

This might lead to potential consequences for the market of audiovisual media 
services and is not in line with the aim of creating transparency in the advertising 
market. 
The obligation provided by Article 23 (2) could create uncertainty on which 
actors, should be entitled to have access to the audience measurement data 
methodologies. It is already common practice among online content providers 
and providers of services of general interest to assess audience measurement 
methodologies, for different reasons, e.g. advertising. Media service providers 
especially, already cooperate heavily on the creation and development of 
audience measurement methodologies through joint-industry communities (JIC) 
(e.g. Auditel & AFG). All JIC members contribute significant resources, including 
financing, and share their work on these methodologies. Obliging free-of-charge 
access for any player would disincentive cooperation and development also 
to the detriment of advertisers.  
It could be easier for any third party- including those with malign aims- to gain 
access to the methodology and dispute it in order to destabilise the market. 
Therefore, safeguards need to be put in place to ensure that access is granted 
to only relevant players, who act in good faith to advance audience 
measurement and bear part of the implementation costs. 
In addition, it is also common practice that JIC members sign strict service 
agreements and that access to the methodologies happens under strict 
confidentiality as these methodologies are part of intellectual property. Those 
parts that are not under the control of closed environments, as inferences, are 
easily made to the IP parts. It would be very damaging to the media sector if 
anyone making a limited purchase of advertising would gain. 
Finally, it is important to consider that both the ePrivacy Directive and the 
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) set clear rules regarding 
transparency and consent, which are key to consumer protection in the 
advertising market. Whereas Article 6a of the DMA, already provides for 
advertising measurement rules.  
Definitions 
DIGITALEUROPE supports the intention of the Proposal to guarantee media 
freedom and plurality. However, Recital 8 of the Proposal introduces worrying 
language which states that some providers of video-sharing platforms (VSPs) or 
very large online platforms (VLOPs) could also qualify as media service 
providers, as some of these platforms have started to exercise editorial control 
over a section or sections of their services. In stating this, Recital 8 goes against 
the well-established definitions of the Audiovisual Media Service Directive 
(AVMSD), which clearly delineates VSPs from the general definition of 
audiovisual media services. This distinction is rightly based on the nature of the 
services provided, with the understanding that the algorithmic presentation of 
7  
 
 
 
 
 
 
content does not equate to editorial control over that content (note this was 
recently confirmed in Joined Cases C-682/18 and C-683/18). VSPs are open 
platforms that fall under the host liability rules established by the DSA – the 
Proposal should complement these rules, not undermine them. Therefore, we 
would suggest deleting Recital 8 from the Proposal and relying on the well-
established distinctions between audiovisual media service providers and VSPs 
established in the AVMSD.

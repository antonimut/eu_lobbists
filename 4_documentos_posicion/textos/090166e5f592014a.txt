DIGITALEUROPE Consultation Response 
on the SMEI proposal 
 
 
 Executive summary 
DIGITALEUROPE appreciates the initiative of establishing Single Market 
Emergency Instruments with the aim of facilitating the market access of 
supply of on-crisis products and services, although we are concerned 
about the interventionist tone of some of the measures in the SMEI 
package. Those include areas such as insufficient involvement of 
stakeholders in the Advisory Group, mandated B2G data-sharing 
requirements which pose risks of sensitive data exposure, as well as the 
lack of sufficient checks and balances before a crisis is activated. We 
have previously expressed similar concerns related to Pillar 3 of the EU 
Chips Act proposed by the Commission1.   
In this paper, we focus specifically on recommendations to improve the 
directive amending some NLF-aligned legislations. Issues with potential 
business impact include: consistency with the European Chips Act 
proposal; mandated B2G info requirements; priority rated orders; adoption 
of common or mandatory specifications. We propose instead some 
additional tools that might be relevant for emergency modes, e.g. 
presumption of conformity. 
We also note that some NLF-aligned legislations are not included in the 
proposal for the amending directive, such as RoHS and ErP.  
Finally, DIGITALEUROPE and its industry members offer their support to 
further discuss our contribution with interested stakeholders.2

Key Points 
 DIGITALEUROPE supports the SMEI initiative with the aim of facilitating 
the market access of supply of on-crisis products and services.  
 DIGITALEUROPE’s concerns with the SMEI proposal, beyond those 
related to the whole package, relate to the directive amending some NLF-
aligned legislations. We instead propose additional tools that might be 
relevant for emergency modes, e.g., postponement and presumption of 
conformity as well as other aspects of the package. 
 We call for consistency with the European Chips Act proposal and the 
adoption of common or mandatory specifications.  
Consistency with the European Chips Act 
DIGITALEUROPE calls for consistency of the Proposals with the provisions of the 
European Chips Act. 
Both the proposals for a Regulation and a Directive of the SMEI package refer to 
the Chips Act monitoring and crisis response mechanism. In this regard, 
DIGITALEUROPE's main concern relates the  consistency of the  monitoring 
mechanism in the SMEI proposal and in the Chips Act. While semiconductors are 
supposed to be out of scope of the SMEI, we would see it relevant to exclude also 
the entire supply chain for chips. This would allow for more clarity in the market in 
times of crisis.  
Moreover, a centralised monitoring and reporting approach is the most preferable 
way for companies , in order to prevent them from providing the same data to 
different sets of authorities (i.e., for the SMEI and Chips).  
Additionally, DIGITALEUROPE is concerned with provisions outlined in the SMEI 
and the Chips Act regarding vigilance and emergency modes . This is primarily  
because:  
• Emergency mode entails mandatory business-to-government (B2G) which 
may impinge on trade secrets. 
• The definition of crisis/emergency , although  understandably vague, 
provides for significant discretion to the Commission in triggering it. 
Finally, DIGITALEUROPE has additional  general concerns regarding the SMEI 
package when understood in the context of the Chips Act, including:  
• Mandated B2G information requirements and risks of fines if the data is not 
provided in time.

Suggestion for additional tools 
Considering the measures that can be activated by EC implementing acts by 
the Directive amending NLF-aligned legislations 2022/0280 (COD), we 
suggest some additional tools may be considered during a crisis situation, 
such as: 
• the postponement of the enforcement date of a new regulation and the 
postponement of date of cessation of presumption of conformity for 
harmonized standard cited in the Official Journal,  
• the presumption of conformity based on European Standards not cited 
in the Official Journal. 
Postponement of the enforcement date 
The enforcement of new requirements means an additional workload for the 
economic operators and for assessment bodies such as test laboratories. New 
requirements may also mean a significant modification of the design that needs 
to be implemented at the manufacture level.  
During Covid 19 situation, DIGITALEUROPE kindly requested the postponement 
of the date of cessation of presumption of conformity for the standard EN 55103-
2. The European Commission services agreed with this proposal. This decision 
allowed manufacturer to continue the procurement of professional audiovisual 
equipment until the end of the crisis.  
Therefore, we kindly suggest adding an article encouraging, in Emergency mode, 
the European Commission to adopt implementing acts deferring the 
enforcement of new requirements and date of cessation of presumption of 
conformity of cited standard, in consultation with the Advisory Group and the 
Expert Group of the relevant sector.  
Presumption of conformity based on European Standards 
DIGITALEUROPE members appreciate the route for offering the presumption of 
conformity based on national and international standards, in addition to the 
European harmonized standard cited in the Official Journal. 
Nevertheless, we consider that many European Standards are currently not cited 
due to procedural complexity that we already highlighted in some previous 
position paper3. These European Standards are available and acknowledged by

Concerns about common specifications 
Our members raised concerns about the activation of common specifications, 
more particularly in the context of an emergency procedure. 
Indeed, the drafting of such engineering specifications needs the involvement of 
all stakeholders to ensure the proportionality of the requirement, and not to 
discriminate a sub-sector from another.  
As an example, the limits for electromagnetic compatibility emissions and 
immunity are the result of negotiations between different subsectors, such as the 
broadcasters, market surveillance, radio amateurs, the manufacturers of 
sometimes antagonistic sub-sectors (e.g., PLC vs power supply, railway 
signalling system vs traction systems…) that need to agree on compatibility limits 
and margin considering the impact on the design and the necessary protection of 
the spectrum.  
Considering the above, we, therefore, have significant doubts that the Advisory 
Group and the sectorial expert group are fully aware of the impact of their 
decision. We also doubt that such a tool is practicable with the necessary level of 
quality of such specification and impact assessment when considering that the 
emergency mode is intended to be activated for 6 months (even if extendable). 
Moreover, a mandatory common specification in the context of an emergency 
mode means that such a requirement will be enforceable without delay. In such 
conditions, the risk that manufacturers and other economic operators are not 
able to make the product available in compliance with this mandatory common 
specification is extremely high.  
As a side effect, even when such a common specification is withdrawn together 
with the emergency mode, and considering the situation for citation of standard 
affecting the principle of presumption of conformity since 2016, there is a high 
risk that some market surveillance and economic operators continue to consider 
these common specification established during a crisis situation as remaining the 
state-of-the-art document to be considered during the conformity assessment, 
even after the withdrawal of the emergency mode.  
Furthermore, the proposal does not provide for guidelines for non-CE marked 
products after the emergency mode is deactivated, nor if they can circulate in the

We conclude that common specification, and more particularly the mandatory 
option, in the context of a crisis situation are in contradiction with the main 
objective of the SMEI regulation to facilitate the placing on the market for product 
and services procurement crisis. We kindly recommend withdrawing such options 
from the proposal, with even more strength when the use of module A is not 
conditioned to the citation of harmonized standards. In case a decision is taken 
to maintain such a tool, we do recommend including the requirement for a 
consultation with the relevant sectorial expert group and relevant 
standardisation technical committees in the drafting of the common specification. 
Finally, any technical specifications should be considered for drafting harmonized 
standards after the emergency mode is deactivated.  
Other suggested improvements 
Notified body and testing facilities 
We highlight that “Notified bodies” are not supposed to be necessarily test 
laboratories. Indeed, the role of the notified body is to study and assess the 
relevance of the technical documentation provided by the manufacturer. The 
technical documentation usually contains test reports issued by independent test 
laboratories contracted by the manufacturer.  
Notified bodies and independent test laboratories are two different entities having 
their own role and responsibilities in the conformity assessment process, as 
described respectively in EN ISO 17020 (conformity assessment) and EN ISO 
17025 (testing and calibration laboratories). 
Therefore, we recommend: 
- replacing “testing capacities” with “conformity assessment capacities”4; 
- if possible, add an article about prioritization of the testing capacities for 
independent test laboratories. 
Presumption of conformity based on the national standard of the 
member state of manufacture  
We see issues that the presumption of conformity based on national standards is 
limited to the “national standard of the member state of manufacture”. Indeed, the 
presumption of conformity could be also based on the national standard of one of 
the member state of destination. A second issue with this sentence is that many

2014/30/EU: relevance of articles 40b and 40c 
Both articles mention their applicability only in case the Notified Body route is 
compulsory. Nevertheless, there is no situation where Module A is not possible in 
the context of the EMC directive.  
Therefore, we don’t see the relevance of both articles in the context of the EMC 
directive. 
2014/30/EU: EMC is not safety 
In many occurrences, the proposal refers to “essential safety requirements” or 
“safety level”. We recall that, as mentioned in the guide of the EMC directive, the 
EMC objectives are not intended to cover the safety of the product.  
We, therefore, suggest deleting “safety”, or substituting “electromagnetic 
compatibility”. 
Derogation from the conformity assessment procedures requiring 
mandatory involvement of a notified body 
Such derogation is provided by any competent national authorities and is limited 
to the member states' territory.  
We believe that this tool may have some relevance for fixed installation or wide-
size products such as machinery.  
Nevertheless, we doubt that such kind of derogation may be practicable for most 
of the consumer product. Indeed, it is almost impossible for the economic 
operators in the context of a single market and European free movement area to 
control that an end-user will not cross a national border with a portable device 
subject to such derogation. This is not practicable for a manufacturer to add CE 
marking on mass-produced equipment after placing it on the market, more 
particularly when considering the requirement for indelibility of such marking.  
Also, in absence of compliance marking, we wonder how a consumer having 
limited knowledge of product compliance may ensure that the device in the scope 
of such derogation is compliant and safe. This is an example, where e-labelling 
may have some relevance as the destination website from the e-label may easily

About DIGITALEUROPE

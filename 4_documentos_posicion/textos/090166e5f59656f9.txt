From wastewater treatment plants to resource plants – time to materialise the 
EU’s circular economy ambitions  
On the 26th of October 2022, the European Commission published its proposal for revising the Urban 
Waste-water Treatment Directive (UWWTD). Ragn-Sells supports the overall ambition to introduce 
stricter emission requirements and to increase the recovery of essential nutrients. We do, however, 
see the need for a more ambitious holistic legislative change to unlock the full potential of resource 
recovery from wastewater treatment plants.  
The revision of the UWWTD was long overdue, and the Commission’s proposal is an important step in 
the right direction, but unfortunately not enough. Wastewater and sewage sludge contain valuable 
raw materials, such as phosphorus and nitrogen, that are essential for food production. For the EU to 
become more self-sufficient, and to have a stable production of fertilisers and feed, the solution starts 
with high recovery demands on nutrients from the wastewater treatment plants. As such, these plants 
need to be considered resource plants, rather than merely wastewater treatment plants. This is a key 
change, needed for the EU to become less dependent on mining and imports from countries outside 
the EU. It is also required for the EU to fulfil targets and goals in, e.g., the Circular Economy Action Plan, 
the Green Deal Package, the Common Agriculture Policy Strategy Plans, the Critical Raw Materials Act, 
the Integrated Nutrient Management Action Plan, and the EU’s overall food security and agri -food 
policy. Against this background, we believe that the following aspects are missing from the 
Commission’s proposal: 
• Minimum requirements for nutrient recovery  
As the Commission mentions in its roadmap for the revision of the UWWTD, the Directive needs to be 
more aligned with the EU’s increased circular economy ambitions. To achieve this, we need to 
introduce requirements for wastewater treatment plants to recycle a certain share of the nutrients 
present in wastewater and sludge. The Commission wants to set minimum reuse and recycling rates 
for phosphorus and nitrogen from sludge at a later stage (using delegated acts) but does not mention 
any minimum reuse or recycling rates. We thus propose a minimum requirement on nutrient 
recycling applicable to wastewater treatment plants. A requirement of 60% recovery of phosphorus 
and 15% recovery of nitrogen, based on the incoming amounts should be the minimum requirement 
for wastewater and sludge in the EU. 
• Establish stringent detoxification obligations 
Strong safeguards need to be put in place to ensure that nutrients recovered from wastewater and 
sludge contri bute to a toxic -free environment and food system. The revised UWWTD should, 
therefore, contain a requirement for hazardous contaminants to be removed in the recovery process 
and not merely diluted.  This can be achieved through the introduction of high -quality, scientifically 
based limit values  and function demands for all recovered materials and end -use applications. This 
would also establish a level playing field for the use of such recovered nutrients in a wide range of 
products (e.g., fertiliser) and markets. Clear principles for recycling nutrients fulfilling the above criteria 
would lead to an alignment with the EU circular economy action plan and the goals set out in the green 
deal package and the farm -to-fork strategy (e.g., a zero -pollution ambition for a toxic -free 
environment, preserving and restoring ecosystems and biodiversity, healthy and environmentally 
friendly food systems, and mobilising industry for a clean and circular economy).

• Enabling the expansion and development of wastewater treatments plants 
As a result of the Court of Justice of the EU’s so -called “Weser Ruling”1 during the implementation of 
the Water Framework Directive, initiatives  to improve, develop, and expand wastewater treatment 
plants might today be stopped, even though the net impact would be positive for the climate and the 
environment at large. For all its good intentions, the ruling has set a precedent that severely limits the 
possibilities to transform wastewater treatment plants into resource plants. As such, the ruling poses 
a major obstacle to the roll -out of innovative technologies that would increase overall sustainability 
and benefit our societies. These undue legislative barriers prohibiting the expansion and development 
of wastewater treatment plants must be removed. We believe that a more holistic framework to 
assess the environmental impacts of nutrient recycling is needed at the EU level.

ANNEX I – NITROGEN AND PHOSPHORUS 
 
Nitrogen 
Recycling nitrogen from wastewater means less nitrogen having to be removed in wastewater plants, 
which also makes it easier to meet the increasingly strict environmental requirements imposed on 
wastewater treatment, while also contributing to reduced climate impact. Bioavailable nitrogen is 
essential for agricultural production. Recycled nitrogen fertiliser can be used to replace conventional 
nitrogen fertiliser, most of which is currently imported and manufactured in a process that uses fossil 
fuel. If this nitrogen can be returned to agriculture, it will be able to reduce both costs and 
environmental impact for the wastewater plants while also substantially reducing emissions of nitrous 
oxide. Nitrogen is the nutrient that, in the short term (6–12 months), is most important to have in good 
supply from the perspectives of national resilience and food security.  
 
Phosphorous 
There are large quantities of phosphorous present in wastewater sludge, which is the waste left behind 
when wastewater is treated, and the water is drained off. For this reason, wastewater sludge has been 
used as a fertiliser in agriculture. Sweden has bee n successful in working upstream to phase out 
unwanted substances, as a result of which approximately 30 per cent of wastewater sludge can 
currently be recycled directly when it also makes a direct contribution to increased fertility in the form 
of organic matter. 
 
Today’s wastewater plants are potential production facilities for nutrients adapted to different needs 
and areas of use. It is now more important than ever before to recycle high -value raw materials from 
wastewater flows. New innovative technology can help  to detoxify flows, recycle nutrients, and 
generate energy and resources. Legislation , therefore, needs to be amended to facilitate circularity 
and supplemented by laws or other forms of incentive for circular social development, ideally by means 
of financia l incentives. The revision of the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive should:  
 
• Augment and advance the development of more circular systems for handling nutrients and 
other resources such as energy and water in our wastewater flows. 
 
• Promote increased recycling and the reuse of phosphorous and nitrogen and other resources 
from public wastewater plant flows in food production. 
 
• Amend current legislation and provide regulatory simplification to allow recycled nutrients 
and other resources to compete on the same market conditions as virgin products.

ANNEX II – FROM WWTPS TO RESOURCE PLANTS 
 
The ACTIONS needed should aim at closing nutrient cycles, pushing for the missing piece in the value 
chain – nutrient recycling. For this to happen, we need goals to create safe and sustainable loops 
when closing nutrients cycles with clear criteria:  
  
• Reduce the exploitation of natural (virgin) resources:  
Include a high demand for recovery of phosphorous (P) from sewage sludge (in cooperation with the 
new Sewage Sludge Directive (SSD) and Urba n Wastewater Directive) of at least 80%, to close the P 
cycle and reduce the dependency on imported mined P that also often contain contaminants (e.g., Cd). 
Include demands of nitrogen (N) recycling from e.g. reject water in the WWTPs. 
  
• Real detoxification, i.e., real removal and not just dilution of hazardous contaminants:  
High quality, clear scenically based limit values and function demands for all recovered products and 
end-use applications to both prevent contaminating the food cycle and the environment but also to 
get a clear level of playing field for all types of products (e.g., recovered nutrients in mineral fertilizers, 
biochar, sewage sludge = everything put on agriculture land) and markets (e.g. EU Fertilise Regulation, 
EU soil strategy, SSD etc).  
 
• Enable recovery and recycling of materials with good quality and efficient functions suitable 
for their applications:  
End of Waste criteria for recovered nutrients to bridge the gap between the recovered nutrients and 
the recycled end-products, to be able to reach the market focusing on high -quality demands and not 
origin.  
  
The above criteria will assure that debts are not passed on to future generations. Clear principles for 
recycling nutrients fulfilling the above criteria would lead to an alignment with the EU circular economy 
action plan and the goals set out in the green deal package and the farm-to-fork strategy (e.g., a zero 
pollution ambition for a toxic-free environment, preserving and restoring ecosystems and biodiversity, 
healthy and environmentally friendly food systems and mobilising industry for a clean and circular 
economy). 
 
Consequently, future actions to increase Europe´s self -sufficiency should aim at the recovery and 
recycling of nutrients with high quality.

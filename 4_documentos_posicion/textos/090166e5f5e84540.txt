   
 
PROPOSAL FOR REGULATION AMENDING SEPA REGULATION & REGULATION ON 
CROSS-BORDER PAYMENT AS REGARDS INSTANT CREDIT TRANSFERS.  
 
 
The Spanish Banking Association (AEB) welcomes the opportunity to provide comments in the 
context of the public participation process on the Regulation amending the SEPA Regulation and 
the Regulation on cross-border payments as regards instant credit transfers. 
In line with the review, the AEB has analysed the text of the proposal and the content of the 
regulation, and the main comments are included below. 
 
Article 1. Amendments to Regulation (EU) No 260/2012  
 
The SEPA Regulation establishes the technical and business requirements for credit transfers 
and direct debits in euro. The current amendment aims the widespread adoption of i nstant 
credit transfers in euro. This requires the use of payment schemes, defined as a single set of 
implementing rules, practices, standards and/or guidelines agreed between payment service 
providers for the execution of payment transactions at the Union level. 
 
As far as instant credit transfers are concerned, the existing scheme provides for the funds to 
be made available on the payee's account within 10 seconds. However, it should be understood 
that the starting point is the payment transaction, that according to PSD2 is an act, initiated by 
the payer […], of placing, transferring or withdrawing funds, irrespective of any underlying 
obligations between the payer and the payee , and not the mere payment order, which refers 
to the instruction given by a payer to its payment service provider requesting the execution of 
a payment transaction. Therefore, only when the payment order is authorised  and the payer’s 
PSP has successfully applied all the necessary checks (payer’s authorization, authentication, 
and availability of funds), the payment order “exists” and can be executed, being therefore this 
point in time, the starting point for its complete execution within 10 seconds.  
 
It should also be considered that, according to  the payment’s regulation, t he payer may 
authorise the payment order prior to the execution date, without this preventing the transaction 
from being executed, depending on the payer's request, within the i nstant credit transfer 
scheme, and therefore ensuring that the amount transferred is credited to the payee's payment 
account within 10 seconds after the execution. 
 
While it is true that in remote interfaces, which allow executing remote payment transactions, 
the time of receipt of the payment order  (accepted) and the execution of the transaction are 
usually almost simultaneous, and therefore the desired effect would be achieved, this is not 
always the case and  it has to be taken into consideration (i.e. orders submitted on paper or 
multiple payment orders submitted in batches) as it would be impossible to include the time 
needed for the processing of the order in the computation of the 10 seconds.

Article 5a. Instant credit transfer transactions 
The Spanish market can be taken as a clear example that the market can organise itself. This 
is the preferred option in order to have quality services for customers. 
As regards the  justification of the  EC for exempting payment institutions an d e -money 
institutions from the requirement, it should be noted that there are also banks that cannot 
directly access payment systems subject to settlement finality regulation, without this being an 
obstacle to carry out payment transactions. Once more, regulation is bypassing the activity, to 
regulate the type of entity and this drifts apart from the principle of "same activity, same risks, 
same regulation". 
However, there are PSPs (credit institutions) which should also be allowed to qualify for an 
exemption because their activity does not involve retail payment services, even though 
operationally they make/receive credit transfers. These are niche entities e .g., investment 
banks that are not focused on retail payments, subsidiaries or branches of banks abroad that 
do not deploy retail payments services even though they use SEPA credit transfers to send or 
receive funds.  
Therefore, an exemption should be available for credit institutions when they do not have retail 
payment services as their core business, even if they have the possibility to send/receive SEPA 
credit transfers. 
The criteria for a possible exclusion should consider, in accordance with the propo rtionality 
principle, variables such as core business other than retail payment services, volume of SEPA 
credit transfers, type of customers, etc.

Apart from that, the proposed regulation includes other obligations. It establishes, the obligation 
to offer instant credit transfers through all channels and to all payment service users. 
In relation to the interfaces (channels), although a detailed impact analysis is required, not all 
customer interfaces may be suitable for instant payments, due to the very nature of an instant 
credit transfer. This, together with the cost of adapting and maintaining the different interfaces, 
means that having to adapt all interfaces may be disproportionate for some of them. Article 
64(2) of PSD2 provides that consent to the execution of a payment transaction (or a series of 
payment transactions) shall be given in the form agreed between the payer and the payment 
service provider. In a way, the proposed regulation interferes in the freedom of institutions to 
offer or prioritise certain services in certain channels, which, as has been demonstrated in the 
Spanish, is not an obstacle to the widespread adoption of instant payments by customers. 
Consequently, not all channels through which PSPs allow users to initiate payment transactions 
are suitable for instant payments, hence the obligation should not affect all of them . N on-
remote channels should be excluded from the obligation, provided that the enabled channels 
do not undermine the main objective, ensuring that instant transfers are widely and effectively 
offered to customers who may be willing to use them. 
In addition, the obligation to offer instant transfers to all users by the mere fact that a PSPs 
offers the credit transfer service (maybe not even to all of them), limits the institution's freedom 
to adapt its services’ offer to its customers. At present, not all customers are necessarily granted 
to make credit transfers, or this service might be subject to certain conditions or limited for 
certain accounts . Some accounts, having the consideration of payment accounts, may be 
subject to restrictions, including legal or judicial restrictions, and may not be available to initiate 
transfers or immediate transfers or may only be available to receive funds. This should also be 
respected in the framework of instant credit transfers. 
Ultimately, PSPs should be allowed to set the conditions under which they provide the service, 
as well as the conditions whic h, exceptionally, may limit  the access to the service, without 
prejudice to specific reporting obligations to the competent national authority. This is the case 
of measures to prevent money laundering, precautionary measures, risk criteria, court-ordered 
measures, as well as cases of technological disruption, suspicion of fraud or mere commercial 
decisions. Further PSPs need to be able to control payment -related liquidity management at 
any time, especially in a context of instant payment transactions and, in particular, in the case 
of extraordinary and unforeseen situations. Likewise, the regulation should also explicitly allow 
PSPs to propose to the users, under certain circumstances, the reconsideration of orders for 
instant credit transfer to other types of payment transactions. Otherwise, under exce ptional 
circumstances, this could imply the rejection of a number of instant payment orders from 
customers that could lead to reputational risk for PSPs, which should have adequate tools to 
minimise this risk. 
The addition of a new paragraph in Article 5a is suggested to expressly recognize that payment 
service providers referred to in the first paragraph, not covered by the second paragraph, who 
wish to benefit from an exclusion from the scope of application may apply for an exemption to 
the National Competent Authority, in accordance with proportionality requirements to be 
established. 
EBA, in turn, should be mandated the elaboration of guidelines to define, in accordance with 
the principle of proportionality, the circumstances under which it is appropriate to grant an 
exemption and the information to be provided to the competent authorities in the request for 
such exemption.

Article 5b. Charges in respect of instant credit transfers 
The industry that the AEB represents is against price regulation . For payment services within 
the scope of the SEPA Regulation , it does not seem to be market functioning circumstances, 
that would justify an intervention. Once again, Spain is a clear example that the market is able 
to organise itself in free competition. There are examples of institutions charging  for instant 
payments the same price, others offering them at a lower price, and others marketing them as 
a premium service and charging a higher price. This responds to the different commercial 
approaches of entities in a competitive market. Each PSP sets the customer prices according to 
the costs and its commercial strategy. The costs of instant payments infrastructure are 
generally higher than the costs of ordinary credit transfers, which by itself, justifies the two 
services not to be priced at the same level. Although simi lar in appearance, they both are 
payment transactions aimed at crediting a payee's payment account on the basis of instructions 
given by the payer, they are provided under different conditions, with different rules and 
infrastructures. The general principle should aim that prices are freely set by the parties and, 
where applicable, that prices are proportionate to the costs incurred. It does not seem 
appropriate that certain services should be subsidised b ecause of  regulatory imposition by 
passing on costs to others. 
An alternative wording for Article 5b is suggested: 
“Article 5b 
Charges in respect of instant credit transfers 
1. Any charges applied by a PSP on payers and payees in respect of sending and 
receiving instant credit transfers transactions in euro shall not be higher tha n the 
charges applied by that PSP in respect of sending and receiving other, corresponding, 
credit transfer transactions in euro , unless justified and proportionate to the different 
costs incurred.

Article 5c. Discrepancies between the name and payment account identifier of a payee 
in case of instant credit transfers 
The industry's preference once again is not to regulate this type of service, but to allow the 
market, if deemed necessary, to develop it and to provide appropriate incentives for institutions 
to do so. It is understandable that the payments industry requires of a significant network effect 
for this type of service to be effective, and it is more valuable if all PSPs are involved in the 
service, but it is questionable whether imposition by European regulation is the best option. 
In fact, in those countries where there was a very specific fraud problem, they have adopted 
measures such as the one proposed. However, although they might have been initially effective 
for the intended purpose, the behaviour of criminals who seek other ways to achieve their 
objectives, has changed accordingly. This solution is not necessarily valid at cross-border level, 
and not necessarily "useful" in areas where the  problem does not exist. The fact that only a 
minority of countries within the Union has developed it suggests that a similar problem does 
not exist or is not significant in other countries, so whatever the contribution this measure can 
make in the fight against fraud is very limited and does not justify its development. 
The proposal of the confirmation of the beneficiary, if retained, requires an appropriate and less 
prescriptive formulation to accommodate other solutions, which may be equally valid or even 
more effective. Whatever the formulation is, it will introduce friction in the user experience and, 
regardless of the technological developments, it will lead to operational problems. In addition, 
there is a risk of misuse where the aim is to obtain information, or to validate ownership 
information of certain accounts (the attempts to get account related information is frequent in 
cases of divorce, relevant persons, sometimes for the commission of crimes, extortion, etc.). 
The way in which the obligation to offer confirmation of payee service, and the way in which it 
is to be carried out , according to the proposed text,  would make the widespread adoption of 
instant payments at point of interaction unfeasible because of excessive friction in the user 
experience. Instant credit transfers would be placed in a clear competitive disadvantage vis-à-
vis other payment instruments such as cards. 
It is therefore requested for this obligation to be removed from the text. If not, at least the way 
in which it has to be offered should be modified. It could be offered only in certain channels or 
for certain use cases. Perhaps, it could be a  voluntary available service, only if the customer 
wants to request it, but not a compulsory service to be offered in each transaction, unless the 
customer opts out (opt-in vs. opt-out). 
Anyhow, the text of the proposal calls on the payer's payment service provider to verify whether 
the name of the payee provided by the payer and the account identifier (IBAN) match. This is 
anomalous, since the payment account identifier refers to an account, which would have to be 
checked against a name, the payee's name, as provided by the payer. 
Then again, the PSP of the payer only has the information provided by the payer in relation to 
the payee and can therefore hardly cross -check the elements with other info rmation. As a 
result, when checking whether the IBAN number matches the name, the PSP can only detect a 
discrepancy. 
A proper check would require the beneficiary's PSP to provide comparable information, however, 
they are not obliged to provide any service in accordance with the proposal. Additionally, given 
that such a service is neither defined nor developed, the proposed deadlines are unrealistic and 
inadequate for that scenario . Should the obligation be maintained, it should only become

enforceable when all PSPs obliged to receive i nstant payments, are operational (30 months), 
given that the payer's PSPs cannot 'perform' the service that they are required under the 
proposal, to offer the service to their customers. And considering that the information on the 
beneficiary customers is held by the respective PSPs, they would have to be accessible for this 
service as well. 
It is questionable the value it may provide to the payer, having the information on the degree 
of discrepancy either in % or by indicating 'no match' or 'close match'. 
Another consideration, which should be included in a coherent proposal, concerns data 
protection. Whatever solution may be adopted, the data to be processed is the one related to 
the payee, whose consent, in the case of natural persons, cannot be obtained in real time. It is 
not necessary for the provision of the payment service (and since the payer can choose not to 
authorise the payment order), thus rendering the service open to misu se. In any case, for 
reasons of legal certainty, it is necessary to establish the legitimising basis in the regulation 
taking into consideration the principle of data minimisation. 
In no case should the obligation to offer this service be extended to all the channels, since in 
some cases it is pointless and would create unnecessary friction (instant payment solutions at 
the point of sale) and in others, there is no channel of returned communication with the ordering 
user (paper-based orders). 
It is paradoxical that the need for payment filtering has been modified to eliminate friction in 
instant transfers and other elements of dubious effectiveness have been incorporated. Elements 
that will cause friction, not only for the PSPs involved, but also for the pay ment service user, 
who will surely lack valid elements to discern when making a decision. 
In addition, it should be clarified who has to offer the service in cases where the order is given 
through a payment initiation service provider. 
It is understood that PSPs may charge a fee for the use of such a service, but the text does not 
clarify some related concepts. It seems that the one who is obliged to offer the service to the 
PSU is the one who can apply the fee to his customer, although the one who has the information 
to allow the verification —and thus would add value to the service — is the PSP of the payee, 
who would bear costs that would not be able to recover from his customer. 
Finally, this service only addresses a specific type of fraud, only if it can  be detected prior to 
the execution of the payment, without providing mechanisms to improve the ability to fight 
against fraud after the execution of the payment  transaction. It would be more effective, in 
providing security for payments, whether i nstant or not, if the proposed Regulation were to 
establish harmonised conditions within the Union to facilitate the blocking and recovery of funds 
from all fraudulently initiated SEPA transactions. 
 
Article 5d. Screening of PSUs with regard to Union sanctions in case of instant credit 
transfers  
First of all, it is difficult to understand that, if filtering is done for all customers  database, this 
is valid and applicable only for instant credit transfers and cannot be equally applied for all other 
payment services included in the regulation, such as non -instant credit transfers and other 
SEPA transactions. 
Secondly, the reference to "listed" requires a single criterion and clarity regarding the lists that 
are to be checked, as there are differences between countries. Otherwise, if there is no certainty

that all participating entities filter all the lists (or a unified list) to which they are obliged, entities 
will have to do additional screening for transactions and the friction will not be eliminated. 
In this sense, it is necessary to unify lists with a criterion of maximums, i.e., to ensure that the 
unified list contains all the records that the different PSPs have to verify, European or else. Only 
if this condition is ensured, can friction and possible rejections due to alerts in the screening 
process on either side be eliminated. Among the lists usually checked by PSPs are, of course, 
those of the European Union, but also those of the United Nations, the Office of Foreign Assets 
Control (OFAC), as well as other jurisdictions for business interests or location reasons. 
Further analysis is needed in collaboration wit h the AML authorities to clarify the scope of the 
proposed regulation, to unify the criteria of the national authorities, once adopted by the latter, 
and to give legal certainty to the process, regardless of whether the PSPs involved are located 
in the same or a different Member State.  
Despite the above, PSPs should be free and explicitly allowed to execute additional screening 
of PSUs according to their own policy risk approach or third territories legal requirements that 
might bind them. 
Under these assumptions, it is possible to replace the obligation to filter against sanctions lists 
in the processing of SEPA payment transactions, not only instant but any of them. 
Similarly, the penalties’ issue requires further analysis and possibly a second level regulation. 
If sanctions are applied on the basis of size, the fact that one entity may be obliged to 
compensate another for the damage caused as a result of penalties, may breach the 
proportionality principle. In addition, the definition of penalties by M ember States may lead to 
discrepancies that do not allow a clear view on the impact for cross-border transactions.

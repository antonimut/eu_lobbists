The Enel Group answer to the European Commission proposal 
for an EU Cyber Resilience Act 
 
 
The Enel Group  is a multinational company and a leading operator in the power and renewables markets of 
Europe and worldwide. As a cornerstone to state the principle of data sovereignty – people and organisations 
to have self -determination with regards to data they generate - the European Commission published on 23 
February 2022, its draft for regulation to ensure fair access and  distribution to data, the Data Act. Data 
protection and competition law requirements are interwoven in the proposal, with the Act drawing heavily on 
common themes in recent European legislation and enforcement actions in the digital field. 
The Enel Group welcomes the Act as a positive step towards a Europe fit for the digital age, and see some 
possibilities to improve the holistic, practical, executive side of the Data Act, summarised below. 
  
I.  INTERPLAY WITH OTHER EU LEGISLATION 
Enel welcomes the clarification on cybersecurity requirements for high-risk AI systems and, the possibility that 
notified bodies under the AI Act are entitled to control the conformity with this Regulation, supporting the 
development of a simpler as well as homogeneous cybersecurity legal framework.  
It is paramount that cybersecurity requirements applied to the scope of the Cyber Resilience Act should be 
aligned to standards and international laws in progress (e.g., Network Code for cybersecurity aspects of cross-
border electricity flows , NIS 2, EU Cloud Rulebook, EU Cloud Security Certification Scheme ), to further foster 
cybersecurity of the entire Ecosystem. 
 
Enel shares the vision that the Cybersecurity Act serve s as a centerpiece for EU legislation on products with 
digital elements, specifically Article 51 on security objectives of European Cybersecurity Certification Schemes 
which are: 
1. first designing connected devices or non-embedded software without known vulnerabilities, and  
2. provide them with mechanisms for secure updates for its whole lifecycle.  
 
II. CLASSIFICATION OF DIGITAL PRODUCTS 
Bringing these security objectives to the list of products with digital elements considered critical in Annex III 
(classes I and II) of the EC proposal for a Cyber Resilience Act, some first considerations arise: 
1. this ‘list-based’ approach will require periodic verification/update on its consistency  
2. since the inclusion of a specific product in Class I or in Class II is based on the risk assessment, the class 
of a product must be periodically checked as the risk level can change 
As far as IoT devices are concerned, their categorization in Class I or Class II will be linked to their use for 
‘essential entities’ defined by the NIS2 Directive (at national level, as the NIS2 Directive must be transposed); 
therefore, it will be necessary to ensure constant alignment between the CRA and the NIS2. In the light of the 
foregoing, the previous process for updating the critical products lists is of outmost relevance considering the 
impacts on the digital market in terms of the related security level, and on the activities needed to guarantee 
them. Hence, it is therefore extremely important to define how this process will be implemented , defining 
factors such as: frequency, impact, alignment, etc.  And time is a k ey cybersecurity factor that should be

III.  SCOPE 
To avoid having a Cybersecurity gap jeopardizing the security of the value Chain for essential services, it would 
be paramount that all devices connected to the Electrical Grid will be cybersecure . Hence, it is relevant that 
connected devices such as smart meters, solar inverters, small windmills, small batteries for residential and SME 
use and other IoT devices connected to electricity distribution network should be included in the scope of the 
regulation as Class I, maintaining Class II for core critical infrastructure components (e.g. SCADA).  
 
IV. VULNERABILITY MANAGEMENT  
According to Article 10.6, manufacturers shall ensure that vulnerabilities of that product are handled effectively 
and in accordance with requirements set out in vulnerability handling requirements (Section 2 of Annex I), for 
the shortest period among the expected product lifetime or for five years  from the placing of the product on 
the market.  
In this regard the product vulnerabilities management is not in any case ensured after 5 years, however in the 
case of Energy operators, a failure to manage vulnerabilities can have devastating impacts on the critical 
infrastructures 'attack surface'. For this reason, in case of lack of vulnerability management after 5 years: 
a. a new higher risk level should be assessed by a mandatory cyber risk assessment procedure, and 
b. it should also be assessed in which cases vulnerability management should be extended and, the ‘end 
of support’ communication process should be defined especially defining the actors involved. A proposal is that 
'Notifying authorities' should inform periodically on which digital products are starting the 'end of support' 
phase. 
However, the whole high-level process of communication (also defining channels) of vulnerabilities and patches 
should be defined, to allow effective and quick communication between the Manufacturers and the other Actors 
(especially the Users).  
Moreover, the vulnerability management period shall be aligned with the investments made ; in a way that 
the period is enlarged accordingly with the investments made. 
In addition, the vulnerabilities reporting, shall be linked to the associated risk, dedicating the appropriate time 
to fix the vulnerabilities, and avoiding unnecessary reporting. 
Related to vulnerability handling requirements (Annex I section 2), it shall be publicly disclosed information 
about fixed vulnerabilities to allow users to ide ntify the product with digital elements affected. When such 
vulnerabilities can be fixed in a short time (such as 3-5 hours), manufacturers shall delay the public disclosure 
to avoid creating a social alarm. 
 
As to the Vulnerability Database we would suggest a central ENISA Database and the definition of a protocol 
to standardize communications and vulnerabilities’ format, thus avoiding heterogeneous autonomous 
Manufacturers’ Databases that could lead to ineffective and uncontrolled disclosure of information. 
In fact, vulnerabilities’ information (Art.11) should not be publicly disclosed but restricted by profiled access to 
the Database  to reduce the risk of double -edge sword , thus allowing information sharing only between 
stakeholders. Moreover, the D atabase should not contain direct correlation of information between 
vulnerability and users.

V. RESPONSABILITIES OF THE DIFFERENT ECONOMIC OPERATORS 
In Articles 13 and 14  (paragraphs 1 and 2) of the proposal for a CRA, importers and distributors  are asked to 
verify, prior setting a product in the EU market, if the manufacturer complied with the Annex I requirements. In 
other words, importers and distributors are asked to perform a 'watch-dog' function towards manufacturers. 
Nevertheless, the Act should clarify whether an importer or a distributor fulfils its obligations by: 
a. choosing manufacturers compliant with the requirements of Annex I or whether,  
b. making its own resources available to monitor the compliance levels of the manufacturer and 
possibly implement corrective measures. 
It should also be noted that importers are asked to keep a copy of the declaration of conformity of a product 
for 10 years; considering that, after a maximum of 5 years, there are no more updates on product vulnerabilities, 
the retention times of copies of declarations of conformity could be reduced. 
When a product is not compliant, a clarification of the responsibilities of manufacturers, importers, and 
distributors, should be included in the CRA  as the possibilities of solving the non-compliance are different for 
each economic operator. In fact, a manufacturer will normally have more possibilities to detect and solve a non-
compliance than importers and distributors (which are often SMEs). 
The responsibility and quantitative number of fines should be differentiated and proportioned, based on the 
role of economic operators in the supply chain (e.g. Importer, Distributor, Manufacturer). 
 
VI. TERMINOLOGY 
 We suggest a further harmonization of the terminology in order to better clarify the scope of application (e.g., 
digital product, connected devices, network management systems), aligning it to other Cyber Security regulation 
definitions (e.g. NIS 2, Cyber Security Act).   
In detail: 
• as for ‘Network management systems’  are included as Class I product. Nevertheless, the term is not 
defined under the Regulation or there is no reference to a legal definition that would enhance clarity on 
which systems and networks it is applicable.  
• Security updates or Patching  are often used to apparently refer to the same concept, but a unique label 
could be clearer.

ACT | The App Association’s feedback to the proposal for a Regulation on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements and amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020  I. Introduction and statement of interest ACT | The App Association is submitting this feedback to the European Commission in response to its proposal for a regulation of the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements and amending Regulation 2019/1020 (Cyber Resilience Act, or CRA, from here). The App Association appreciates the Commission’s continued efforts to make the European Union (EU) fit for the digital age and its commitment to a stable and secure cyberspace.   The App Association is a global trade association for small and medium-sized technology companies. Our members are entrepreneurs, innovators, and independent developers within the global app ecosystem that engage with verticals across every industry, creating the software and apps used on mobile devices and in enterprise systems around the globe. In 2021, the ecosystem the App Association represents—called the app economy—contributed €210 billion in revenue to the EU, accounting for 0.7 per cent of the EU’s gross domestic product (GDP) and supporting up to 1.4 million jobs across the European economy. Alongside the world’s rapid embrace of mobile technology, our members create innovative solutions that power the internet of things (IoT) across all modalities and segments of the economy. Our members are small technology and app development companies, which makes them especially vulnerable to security threats in cyberspace. The consequences of malicious actors gaining access to our members’ technology and/or customer data could be disastrous for them.   Consequently, small businesses already have strong incentives to implement stringent cybersecurity measures and increase their resilience. A cyber attack on a small business can cause irreparable damage and the loss of reputation, constituting an existential threat. Our members’ continued ability to mitigate serious and dynamic cyber threats is, therefore, crucial to their livelihood. We believe that an EU-level regulation that aims to increase cyber resilience must address the issue of harmful actors by preserving the current security environment, especially on software distribution platforms like app stores, to ensure the continued success of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and startups. For these small businesses and startups, software platforms ensure high levels of security and trust in the interest of consumers and businesses and are fundamental in supporting small app developers who rely on consumer trust to compete with global brands on a level playing field.    While the rise of IoT and mobile apps holds great promise, it also raises security threats due to the increase in networked devices in a wide range of industries. Consequently, we believe companies using IoT devices will require more evolved and dynamic risk management practices. The App Association believes that additional guidance and the promotion of voluntary measures in the industry would allow companies to achieve this objective while preserving innovation and not overburdening smaller actors.

II. Feedback on the proposed Cyber Resilience Act The App Association welcomes the Commission’s efforts to further harmonise and improve cybersecurity in Europe, and we appreciate that it is taking a risk-based approach to the Cyber Resilience Act. Such a risk-based approach helps ensure that low-risk products only have to comply with minimal requirements and fewer compliance checks. We also welcome that the maximum period during which manufacturers must provide security updates for their product has been capped at five years (or the product lifecycle if it’s less than five years).  The text aims to create a uniform legal framework for essential cybersecurity requirements for placing products with digital elements on the market, which increases legal certainty for businesses of all sizes. By listing essential cybersecurity requirements, the CRA adds to the existing baseline cybersecurity framework by creating common, basic rules to be applied by all manufacturers, importers, and distributors of such products, enhancing overall consumer trust, security, and privacy in the EU.   Nonetheless, we are also concerned with the highly prescriptive approach the CRA takes. Instead of new horizontal legislation with stringent requirements, App Association members would benefit more from a regulation that builds on the success of the current cybersecurity framework, which includes the NIS 2.0 Directive and the Cybersecurity Act. A less prescriptive approach would also be more effective and future-proof in the long term. Adding to the existing baseline with a more flexible approach that sets up an incentive structure would enhance cybersecurity across the EU and advance the Digital Single Market without subjecting SMEs to potentially untenable compliance costs. We urge the Commission to consider the incentives that vendors do have in implementing adequate security safeguards.  SMEs especially, including our members, largely depend on consumer trust to succeed, and cybersecurity is a crucial aspect in building and retaining consumer trust. Given the horizontal nature of the Act, to avoid disproportionately burdening SMEs, the Member States, national authorities, and the European Commission must offer sufficient support to help SMEs understand and comply with the CRA’s requirements.   The current EU framework that applies to digital products already comprises several pieces of legislation, causing confusion for businesses that try to comply, especially as some of these regulations do not have specific cybersecurity provisions but may cover certain aspects linked to cybersecurity (e.g. the General Product Safety Directive or the Machinery Directive). Furthermore, not all digital products, including non-embedded software products are covered by existing legislative texts (e.g. the Radio Equipment Directive or Medical Devices Regulation). This gap risks creating legal uncertainty for businesses, and we welcome the Commission’s recognition of these issues.   For this reason, the harmonisation of the CRA with other EU-level cybersecurity instruments in terms of definitions and requirements is essential to increase legal certainty for both consumers and businesses. More specifically, incident notification and reporting requirements could benefit from additional harmonisation, considering that the CRA does not explicitly define what constitutes a cybersecurity ‘incident’ in Article 3. reporting requirements as well as obliging vulnerability disclosures before a company can implement mitigation measures, we recommend aligning the CRA’s definition of ‘incident’ with that of the Network and Information Security (NIS) 2 Directive. According to the NIS2 Directive, an ‘incident’ is any event that ‘compromises availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of stored transmitted or processed data or of the services offered by, or accessible via, network and information systems’, which would be an appropriate approach for the CRA to take as well. The CRA should further align with the NIS 2 Directive regarding the ‘significant incident’ threshold, clarifying that only incidents that cause notable impact should trigger reporting requirements. This threshold would enable companies to use their resources to address significant threats rather than overburdening them with reporting obligations for irrelevant or minor incidents.   Additionally, the CRA specifies a different timeline for incident notification obligations (24 hours, Article 11 (2)) than the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (72 hours for breach notifications) and the NIS 2 Directive (72 hours for full incident notification). We believe it would be best for the CRA to adopt a 72-hour reporting timeframe as well to avoid confusion and ensure that the time threshold is both significant and workable for all stakeholders, especially for smaller businesses that lack the resources for such a fast turnaround. Additionally, the CRA’s wording does not make it sufficiently clear when the 24-hour period begins, and we recommend that it starts after an incident has been mitigated by the company’s response team, as the GDPR and NIS 2 employ a similar approach. We also believe that the public and users should only receive notifications if the incident affected them negatively, or where appropriate to deal with or prevent the incident to avoid unnecessary reporting for businesses.   To further remove some of the above-mentioned legal uncertainties, we encourage the Commission to explicitly clarify the scope of the Regulation, namely the exclusion of Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) in Recital 9 and how it relates to Articles 3 (1) and (2). In its current form, the scope includes SaaS and Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS) products that rely on ‘remote data processing’ – which most PaaS and SaaS fundamentally do. This wording raises the possibility of these services unintentionally being included in the scope, although the Commission states in Recital 9 that it does not see them as part of the scope. Adding unnecessary complexity by including SaaS and/or PaaS could disincentivise companies from employing cloud-based software.    The essential requirements listed in Annex I appear reasonable and recognise the fact that following best practices and due diligence are essential to ensure cybersecurity during all stages of a digital product or software’s lifecycle, including development, deployment, and use. The list of ‘critical products with digital elements’ (Annex III), however, is overly broad and not limited by use cases. In its current form, it would oblige certain manufacturers to follow burdensome and costly conformity assessment procedures. According to the European Commission’s impact assessment, the CRA will add €29 billion in new compliance costs for software developers and hardware manufacturers in terms of new security requirements, conformity assessments, documentation, and reporting obligations, for an

estimated value of €1.485 billion in turnover. This number is unacceptably high and will likely have a disproportionate effect on small developers and manufacturers. Given these estimated costs and additional work to carry out conformity assessments, we recommend reviewing the product categories to ensure their inclusion is warranted and proportionate, particularly because the Commission can update the list of products via delegated acts (without much stakeholder input). We further encourage the Commission to explain its method for identifying the products in classes I and II, Annex III. In its current form, the CRA would add significantly to the already high bars companies have to clear when putting connected products on the market. We urge the Commission to recognise and assess the broader impact of these compliance costs on the internet of things (IoT) industry. Establishing another regulatory agency for the enforcement of these new cybersecurity rules will only make the legal landscape more challenging and riskier to navigate, especially for the smaller companies in this space.  Generally, overly-prescriptive requirements that do not permit the scaling of risk mitigation measures based on specific risks posed will force companies to comply in ways that are not the most efficient or effective for their individual circumstances. With more general risk management obligations, companies have the flexibility to find the tools and policies that are best suited for them to manage cyber risks most efficiently and effectively. Additionally, technological progress might outdate any prescriptive requirements rapidly, rendering them impractical and futile in a short time. Levels of risk management should be as harmonised as possible across different national markets so that the compliance burden is decreased for companies that operate across the EU. Concerning cybersecurity standards, the CRA must rely on internationally recognised standards as opposed to novel proprietary technical specifications with essential requirements in order to avoid fragmentation and decrease compliance costs.  The Commission also foresees that business users, consumers, and citizens may face higher prices of products with digital elements. While we appreciate that notification bodies are required to take the interests and capacities of SMEs into account when setting the prices for conformity assessment procedures, the CRA applies indistinctly to both small and large companies. Although SMEs have strong incentives to implement stringent cybersecurity measures and increase their resilience, a cyberattack on an SME can mean a loss of reputation and constitute an existential threat. Due to their size, however, they can lack resources to implement full-scale cyber defence programs. The Commission should address this by allocating funding to make information sharing more user-friendly, increase cyber education and outreach, and improve trust between information-sharing entities to incentivise SME information sharing. To ensure that SMEs aren’t unfairly punished for falling victim to cyberattacks, liability protections for defensive measures SMEs implement would be useful. We reiterate that introducing mandatory requirements to demonstrate compliance may be particularly cumbersome for small companies. Harmonised guidelines and recommendations would be favourable, as well as harmonised voluntary conformity assessment schemes that align across the various EU cybersecurity certification mechanisms, including those ENISA is currently developing for the Cybersecurity Act, the NIS2 Directive, and the CRA. the Cybersecurity Act, any EU-wide certification for cybersecurity included in the CRA should also remain voluntary and recognise self-assessments as the default conformity assessment mechanism. Given the significant impacts on both businesses and consumers described above, we strongly urge the Commission to reconsider the current structure and mandated provisions of this adopted proposal.  In terms of the proposed rules, we believe that they could impede or halt innovation and the launch of new products and services by establishing overly complex approval processes for connected products and stand-alone software. The scope is broad enough that it could include anything from anti-virus software and smart meters to operating systems for both mobile and desktop devices. As such, the proposed Act thus also has consequences for all the services that use the hardware and software that falls within the scope, e.g. messaging and email, cloud storage, search engines, online marketplaces, cloud storage, and social networks. We are particularly concerned by the CRA’s provisions on ‘substantial modification’ (Recitals 22 and 23) that Article 3 (21) defines as ‘a change to the product with digital elements following its placing on the market, which affects the compliance of the product with digital elements with the essential requirements set out in Section 1 of Annex I or results in a modification to the intended use for which the product with digital elements has been assessed’. The CRA foresees that after a ‘substantial modification’ the manufacturer of a product with digital elements will have to verify compliance and ‘where applicable’ undertake a new conformity assessment. This overly broad definition of a ‘substantial modification’ could then mean that the proposed CRA sets up a framework in which, for example, important software updates would need to go through new conformity checks before they would be available to European customers.   The CRA explicitly mentions software in Recital 22, stating that ‘a product with digital elements should be considered as substantially modified by a software change where the software update modifies the original intended functions, type or performance of the product and these changes were not foreseen in the initial risk assessment, or the nature of the hazard has changed, or the level of risk has increased because of the software update’. Because developers constantly update software applications to introduce new functionalities (possibly ‘modifying original intended functions or performance’) and patch vulnerabilities (likely because ‘the nature of the hazard has changed’), it seems that most software updates would have to go through new conformity assessments under this provision. As a result, EU businesses, as well as consumers, would potentially have to wait longer to be able to update their devices, exposing them to vulnerabilities longer than consumers in other regions, which would decrease a product’s cybersecurity rather than increase it. Requiring de facto conformity assessments for software updates will only overburden developers and harm consumers by delaying their access to potentially urgent security updates. Given the above, we urge the Commission to revisit Recitals 22 and 23 and its definition of ‘substantial modifications’, especially in the context of software and software updates and consider exempting software updates from this provision.

The App Association further notes that other EU legislation, like the Digital Markets Act and the Sustainable Products Initiative, risk lowering the current level of privacy and security, which may increase the likelihood of invasive trafficking, fraud, and piracy. While the CRA requires companies to provide mechanisms to securely distribute updates for products and ensure that security patches or updates are disseminated without delay and free of charge, the Digital Markets Act, and the proposed Sustainable Products Initiative mandate practices such as sideloading, the introduction of third-party app stores, or requiring the ability to reject/rollback software updates. Not only will such measures increase the costs for app developers in maintaining a cyber-resilient environment for the benefit of consumers, but it is also unclear which law takes precedence and which party is liable in case of a data breach or cyber attack. In its current version, the CRA fails to address or reconcile these conflicting provisions. The App Association urges the Commission to carefully consider the consequences of further regulating cybersecurity in a way that will entail excessive compliance costs and have a disproportionate effect on small app developers and evaluate how it can use the CRA as a tool to remedy cybersecurity issues that may originate from the implementation of provisions in the DMA or the proposed Sustainable Products Initiative.  While the App Association endorses the use of software bills of material (SBOMs), such as those currently being developed through open public-private partnerships in the United States,1 as a tool to assist in addressing the identification and mitigation of security risks and liabilities in software supply chains, the CRA enables the European Commission to define the format and details of an SBOM entirely through implementing acts which we find concerning. When determining the details of an SBOM, it’s crucial to involve stakeholders like software developers who have the most relevant expertise and day-to-day experience to ensure that EU requirements align with industry best practices and international standards. Thus, we urge the Commission to include stakeholders in its development of SBOMs under the CRA.  In its call for evidence earlier this year, the Commission stated that it is difficult for consumers to inform themselves and assess the security of products and services. The current draft of the CRA, however, does not address this issue. We believe end-user education is a crucial aspect of improving security in spaces like IoT because many cyber-based attacks are preventable. In evaluating and improving risk management, the Commission should also inform end users across the business and consumer communities of steps to take to ensure that digital literacy and cybersecurity skills are promoted and enhanced.   III. Conclusion The App Association welcomes the Commission’s commitment to enhancing and ensuring a consistently high level of cybersecurity of digital products in the Digital Single Market. Nonetheless, we regret that the Commission decided to abandon its intention to incentivise vendors to offer more secure products and chose to draft a horizontal piece of legislation that offers little flexibility for those firms that will have to comply.    1 E.g., https://www.ntia.gov/SBOM.

While we support the Commission’s efforts to further harmonise and improve cybersecurity in Europe, we note that cybersecurity and the market environment wherein digital players operate are uniquely complex and dynamic. Light-touch regulation is, therefore, best suited to maintain flexibility and be able to address the continuously changing dynamics in cybersecurity instead of horizontal cybersecurity requirements. A CRA based on and aligned with existing processes and structures could be a solid regulatory framework that offers flexibility to the Member States while emphasising the importance of harmonisation.  We reiterate that digital service providers can and do self-regulate effectively, and their business models rely heavily on consumer trust; thus, they have high incentives to adhere to strong cybersecurity standards. Additionally, ex ante rules as opposed to an ex post approach severely increase the compliance burden for businesses, which particularly hurts the SMEs the CRA would cover. As such, small businesses may be locked out of the market by the introduction of rigid requirements. The development of voluntary codes, guidelines, or best practices through an industry-led effort would much more effectively benefit the entire security landscape.

 IBM’S COMMENTS ON THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION’S  CYBER RESILIENCE ACT PROPOSAL   January 2023   
     INTRODUCTION    IBM welcomes the opportunity to provide comments to the European Commission’s proposal for a Regulation on “horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements” (so-called “Cyber Resilience Act”, hereafter “CRA”).    The proposed CRA, published on 15 September 2022, will be an important step forward in strengthening the security of digital products placed on the EU market and to better protect European businesses and consumers from the growing threat of cyberattacks. Faced with the new challenges brought by a more complex threat landscape, and with more professionalised cybercriminals using increasingly sophisticated tools and technologies, IBM has continued to dedicate significant investment in product security and in developing security standards, notably Open-Source Software (OSS) standards via the Linux foundation. IBM supports the Commission’s objective to raise baseline cybersecurity standards for connected products. We welcome the Commission’s decision to introduce a set of horizontal, risk-based rules in the proposed CRA’s based on the principles of the New Legislative Framework (NLF), which allows conformity to be demonstrated with self-assessment as a default method.    IBM nonetheless believes that some aspects of the proposal could be further clarified and improved. This would help raise practices throughout industry as well as facilitate effective implementation of the rules.    Generally, we believe the EU should seek to better consider the following fundamentals, without which we believe the security objectives of this proposal will not be met:    - The scope of the proposed CRA should be clarified and consistent: while we support the Commission’s intention to include a scope that is as broad as possible and make the proposed CRA “lex generalis” to facilitate the harmonisation of rules, the text creates confusion, for example, as to what type of “software” should be covered by the new rules, and whether Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) falls within the scope. We specifically support a clearer exclusion of SaaS from the scope of the proposed CRA as SaaS is a service, already considered as an “essential service”, and as such, covered by the requirements under the NIS 2 Directive. We also call for a clearer and more risk-based approach to designating products as “critical” in Annex III.    - The proposed CRA’s essential cybersecurity requirements should introduce a risk-based evaluation of any vulnerabilities associated with a product when placing the product on the market, as opposed to requiring such product reach an unrealistic state of presenting “no known vulnerabilities”.  This would incentivise manufacturers to identify and fix those vulnerabilities that could potentially impact

their clients, rather than incentivise them to avoid discovering vulnerabilities until after the software is released.        - The proposed CRA should be consistent with other – upcoming or existing - EU legislation: for example, incident reporting requirements (notably the type of incidents to report, and the authority to report to) for manufacturers under the proposed CRA are inconsistent with existing privacy and security legislation. This would significantly increase the complexity of notification processes, and thus hinder the manufacturers’ ability to deal with the incidents and for the authorities to receive valuable information in a timely manner. Additionally, EU cybersecurity certification schemes should remain voluntary, in line with the 2019 Cybersecurity Act (CSA).    - The CRA should not require manufacturers to share sensitive information externally: publicly disclosing too many details about the product (such as complete information about the product’s design, development including system architecture or software components), as well as complete risk assessments in the technical documentation, could significantly increase confidentiality, intellectual property, and security risks, thereby increasing the likelihood that malicious actors actively exploit such information. Better safeguards should be included in the text so that necessary information sharing between manufacturers and government authorities is not more exposed to malicious attacks.       SCOPE    We support the clarification that SaaS is excluded from the scope of the proposed CRA and encourage a consistent approach. Recital 9 specifies that the proposed CRA does not regulate SaaS except “for remote data processing solutions relating to a product with digital elements understood as any data processing at a distance for which the software is designed and developed by the manufacturer of the product concerned or under the responsibility of that manufacturer, and the absence of which would prevent such a product with digital elements from performing one of its functions”. However, article 3(1) defines “products with digital elements” as “any software or hardware product and its remote data processing solutions, including software or hardware components to be placed on the market separately”, creating potential confusion.     As mentioned in Recital 9, cloud services (which include SaaS) fall under the scope of the NIS 2 Directive as “essential services” and as such, are already subject to cybersecurity and risk management requirements, making compliance with the CRA requirements unnecessary and likely counterproductive.     
 
  4  
Not clearly excluding services from the proposed CRA would only add unnecessary complexity that might deter businesses from using cloud-based software.  IBM would therefore recommend EU institutions further refine the software category to ensure greater legal clarity to exclude services from the scope, such as SaaS, and focus on products.    Additionally, we consider the list of products classified as “critical” (in Annex III) to be overly broad, with few details on how the category of products listed are defined, and the methodology used to develop the list. Moreover, amendments and further specifications of the definitions of the product categories covered by Annex III, as well as specifying categories of “highly” critical products, are expected to be adopted by the Commission through delegated acts [Article 6(2), 6(3) and 6(5)]. This only brings uncertainty for businesses.    Therefore, IBM recommends a clearer approach to categorising digital products according to their risk-level, which should be considered as part of the risk assessments conducted in the course of the conformity assessment process and taking into account the specific use and application of the product: this would avoid wrongfully designating a whole range of products as “critical”, and forcing organisations to unnecessarily redirect valuable cybersecurity resources towards implementing overly stringent requirements, to the detriment of focusing on tackling real risks.     We also call for guaranteeing a transparent and inclusive process to consult relevant stakeholders when amending and further specifying the categories of products listed in Annex III.       OBLIGATIONS FOR ECONOMIC OPERATORS    IBM is generally supportive of the baseline obligations laid out in the CRA proposal. However, we believe that certain elements should be amended to reflect current industry best practices and to avoid causing unintended consequences that may lead to less secure products:    1- Security requirements – Annex I.1    We would caution against requiring the possibility for users to reset the product with digital elements to its original state [Annex I.1.(3)(a)]. Pre-configured settings are likely to differ based on who is using the product and for what purpose. Instead of “original state”, we would recommend including a requirement to provide the customer with the necessary documentation on how to “securely configure the features of the product” (which can be offered by default or by allowing the customer to configure features such as encryption – at rest and/or in transit – multi-factor authentication, or security event logging, by themselves).  
 
  5  
  2- Vulnerability handling – Annex I.2    We have significant concerns with the requirement to deliver a product “without any known exploitable vulnerability”: delivering products without any exploitable vulnerabilities is an impossible bar to set. A product’s security can be influenced by numerous factors, including the product’s deployment environment, the development of different technologies, and by the evolving cyber-attack landscape. These impact whether a vulnerability can actually be exploited or not. Such a requirement would discourage manufacturers from conducting meaningful security testing, potentially leading some to avoid scanning products (this way, keeping those potential vulnerabilities “unknown”), and thereby leading to less secure products being delivered on the market.     Instead, IBM recommends allowing economic operators to implement a risk-based approach to remediating vulnerabilities based on numerous factors and situational circumstances like the vulnerability risk level and the criticality of the data and the systems impacted. Such an approach would allow entities to focus on remediating the most critical vulnerabilities first and would also be aligned with existing global industry standards and frameworks.    3- Content of the technical documentation    We have significant concerns about the potential security risk of including the following elements in the technical documentation:    - First, manufacturers should not be required to provide complete information on the design and development of the product “including, where applicable, drawings and schemes and/or a description of the system architecture explaining how software components build on or feed into each other and integrate into the overall processing” [Annex V.(2)(a)] – Such a requirement would make products less safe, both from an Intellectual Property perspective, exposing possible trade secrets externally, but also from a cybersecurity perspective, providing a complete blueprint to malicious actors.    - Additionally, we also believe that detailed risk assessments should not be included in full in the technical documentation [Annex V.(3)]: we suggest including a statement or a summary instead, specifying that we can implement a procedure to provide complete information to the relevant authorities upon request.    Finally, the general description of the product including the versions of the software affecting compliance with essential requirements should be limited to major releases /

material changes only (not including every and/or minor releases, such as patches) [Annex V.(1)(b)].      VULNERABILITY & INCIDENT REPORTING    IBM has concerns about the reporting obligations laid out in Article 11. The CRA proposal suggests that “manufacturer shall, without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, notify to ENISA any actively exploited vulnerability contained in the product with digital elements” [Article 11(1)] as well as “any incident having impact on the security of the product with digital elements” [Article 11(2)].     First, we believe the term “actively exploited vulnerability” should be further clarified, i.e. whether this is about an incident on the product itself, or an exploitation that could potentially impact the product. The latter would be classified as a PSIRT issue (Product Security Incident Response Team), which typically are not reported externally or to competent authority until a fix/patch has been made available, in line with existing Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) practices and standards to protect customers. Releasing public information about an unmitigated vulnerability can lead to additional cyber-attacks and is out of step with global industry best practices. In this regard, we warn EU regulators of the possible negative consequences of introducing such requirement.      Moreover, Article 11 appears to be inconsistent with relevant parts of the recently agreed NIS 2 Directive. Indeed, the language proposed in the CRA does not define the term “incident”; clarify the type of incidents to be reported (e.g. “significant” incidents), as well as align the single point of contact, which is ENISA in the proposed CRA, rather than the competent authority of the manufacturer’s main establishment (Computer Security Incident Response Team or CSIRTs) in the NIS 2 Directive.    We believe these inconsistencies can significantly impact overall product security by creating unnecessary uncertainty for the manufacturers that need to undertake such reporting processes.     Additionally, we believe that the timing of this notification obligation (“without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of becoming aware” [Article 11(2)]) is unrealistic. Incident reporting requires complex multi-disciplinary coordination within the company (security operations, IT, legal, etc.) and externally (affected customers, partners, etc.). For such reporting to be effective and valuable, and to protect the security of customers’ systems, sufficient time needs to be given so that businesses can first focus on fixing the incident, while gathering the necessary complete information that can help competent authorities to understand the nature of the incident and conduct an effective triage so they can prioritise reports. Adding another layer of complexity (e.g., understanding whether this 
 
  7  
is a CSIRT or PSIRT incident / whether this falls under NIS 2 or the CRA) to an already unworkable 24h timeline, would only divert critical resources to determining reporting obligations rather than mitigating the cybersecurity risk.     Therefore, IBM calls on regulators to ensure that reporting timelines remain consistent with existing privacy and security rules (including GDPR) to be workable, i.e. “without undue delay and no less than 72 hours”).      CONFORMITY ASSESSMENTS    IBM generally welcomes the Commission’s proposal to align the proposed CRA’s conformity assessment procedure with the New Legislative Framework (NLF), maintaining self-assessment against existing international and harmonised European standards as the default option to demonstrate conformity, while introducing third-party assessments obligations for higher risk products.    With regard to software, we believe that the notion of “substantial modification” should be further clarified (Article 3(31) and Recital 22/23): indeed, the current language is too broad and may suggest that operators would need to undergo a conformity assessment procedure every time the software is updated, which happens too frequently for organisations to credibly keep up with the assessment and documentation process. We would suggest instead clarifying that the conformity assessment procedure only needs to be undertaken upon a major version upgrade only.      IBM also does not support the approach laid out in Article 19, giving the possibility for the European Commission to adopt “common specifications” via implementing acts, if it considers that the “relevant harmonised standards are insufficient to satisfy the requirements of this Regulation or to comply with the standardisation request of the Commission, or where there are undue delays in the standardisation procedure or where the request for harmonised standards by the Commission has not been accepted by the European standardisation organisations”. Such an approach does not offer sufficient transparency guarantees (e.g., clear criteria for when those common specifications can apply to ensure they are aligned with both product manufacturers and users’ expectations and market needs). Additionally, we believe that as soon as European harmonised standards are available, they should be accepted to demonstrate conformity with the CRA.     European standardisation organisations already have a structure in place to support any effort to develop such common specifications. IBM has long contributed to the development of security standards at both international and EU level and remains 
 
  8  
committed to provide the necessary expertise. It is therefore crucial that industry remains involved – alongside regulators – in the standardisation process.     Finally, Article 6 specifies that highly critical products should obtain a cybersecurity certification pursuant to the 2019 Cybersecurity Act (CSA) to demonstrate conformity with Annex I requirements under the CRA. However, to this day, even if some schemes are currently being developed by ENISA (e.g., EU Cybersecurity Certification Scheme for Cloud Services or EUCS), none has been finalised or implemented under the CSA yet. Thus, this would be premature to include such an obligation, and we believe that any European cybersecurity certification approach should remain voluntary, aligned with the language of Article 53(4) of the CSA.       SOFTWARE BILL OF MATERIALS (SBOM)    IBM supports the development and a more widespread use of SBOM. SBOM can be a very useful tool to provide software developers and users better visibility into the software supply chain of modern application development (such as better identifying software components). They can be a good starting point, alongside other elements of a larger cybersecurity risk management program, but will not address most, let alone all, of the daily cyber risks an organisation faces.     SBOM as a ‘stand-alone concept’ does not add much value. It needs to be part of an overall standards-based concept documenting details of the software but limited to essential information.     Indeed, SBOM are still in their infancy, and as such, have not yet achieved the required maturity level on how they should be implemented, shared, and used. For example, there is no single globally prescribed method for determining components names – so two different SBOM authors might use two different identifiers for the same component – this is because software components suppliers define those according to their own needs.    In this context, we ask EU institutions to carefully consider the following elements with regard to the use of SBOM in the proposed CRA:    - SBOM should use standard-based and machine-readable formats to support  their uptake, and the industry is already significantly investing in accelerating the maturation of SBOM standards and best practices. It is therefore crucial to allow the private sector to continue to coalesce on the standard-based formats that work best for given industries and organisations, and for regulators to ensure close industry consultation when defining SBOM requirements [Article 10(15)].  
 
  9  
  - SBOM should not be required in a cloud environment: applied to SaaS, SBOM would bring little value. Indeed, SaaS updates and patches can be done on a continuous basis (and automated). This leads to a faster resolution of vulnerabilities. Therefore, requiring SBOM in the cloud context would make those very quickly outdated. Consequently, the CRA should clearly specify that SBOM would be applicable for ‘on-premises’ software and, thus, the SBOM requirements should only apply when a software product is shipped. While, at this stage, Recital 9 specifies that SaaS is not in scope of the proposed CRA, we have concerns that such exclusion is not sufficiently clear as previously mentioned in this position paper. SaaS and cloud products should be required to attest that they manage their own supply chain and manage cybersecurity risk (as per NIS 2 requirements).    - SBOM should only need to go down one level deep: organisations should only have to produce SBOM for their suppliers, and contractually require suppliers to and provide an SBOM for their own suppliers. Organisations should therefore not be required to address all dependencies when producing SBOM as it is not feasible to establish all software component across the supply chain. This would lead to SBOM becoming overly long and complex, making them less likely to be used.     - There should be more safeguards, such as non-disclosure agreements, with regards to disclosing SBOM externally (users, market authorities, etc.): we would indeed caution against requiring companies to disclose details about vulnerabilities as part of an SBOM, as this could create a roadmap to be exploited by a malicious actor.    ***

UNIFE Position Paper on Cyber Resilience in Railways 
About UNIFE 
Based in Brussels since 1992, UNIFE is the association representing Europe’s rail supply industry at the European Union 
and international levels. UNIFE’s members include more than 110 companies – from SMEs to major industrial champions 
– active in the design, engineering and manufacture of rolling stock (i.e., trains, metros, trams, freight wagons) as well as 
rail signalling and infrastructure equipment. UNIFE also brings together the national rail supply industry associations of 13 
European countries. 
Introduction and Executive Summary 
In order to reap the benefits of digit alization, it is vital that cyber resilience is ensured. Cyber-secure digital products and 
services are crucial in the increased digitalization of rail transport  and digital products must remain cyber secured 
throughout their entire lifecycle. UNIFE considers cybersecurity as a priority and believes that a coherent regulatory 
framework cover ing the cybersecurity of connected products is of utmost importance . UNIFE’s Position Paper on 
Cybersecurity in Railways 1 published in September 2021  demonstrated the importance of this subject for the European 
rail supply industry provid ing our assessment and recommendations to secure , with a vertical approach,  Europe’s rail 
system. Given the size,  scope, and fragmentation of the European rail sector, as well as the multitude of stakeholders 
involved (including rail manufacturers, operators, infrastructure managers and users), cybersecurity has to be considered 
through a holistic approach when defining the European regulation to protect our rail systems.  
 
It is worth to highlight that t he European rail supply industry is putting huge efforts to protect rail systems from exploi ted 
cybersecurity vulnerabilities following the standard IEC 62443. Furthermore, the European rail sector has developed the 
CENELEC CLC/TS50701 – that builds on the existing IEC 62443 – considering the particularities of rail. This is a landmark 
and it is currently being migrated at international standardisation level. In addition, in the current rail regulation there are 
provisions on cybersecurity (i.e., C ontrol Command Signalling Technical Specification for Interoperability) and best 
practices included in ENISA’s report “Railway Cybersecurity - Good Practices in Cyber Risk Management”2  are nowadays 
implemented in rail projects. 
 
UNIFE acknowledges the European Commission’s proposal on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with 
digital elements and amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 (hereinafter referred to as the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)) and 
its aim to “increase the overall level of cybersecurity of all products with digital elements”. This regulation is indeed building 
on the 2020 EU Cybersecurity Strategy and the 2020 EU Security Union Strategy, ensuring that digital products, such as 
wireless and wired products and software, are more secure within the EU. It is paramount to consider the particularities of 
different industrial sectors, like the rail sector, where a vertical approach is key to success in increasing the cyber resilience 
of products and systems.  For the rail sector, it i s crucial to avoid the setting-up of additional layers of regulation in an 
already highly regulated industry , whereas cybersecurity provisions are already addressed through European and 
international standardisation organisations and can be integrated efficiently in the existing European rail  regulation. 
However, UNIFE is concerned by the lack of consultation of the European rail supply industry for the preparation of the 
CRA proposal so far. 
 
To avoid overregulation UNIFE firmly believes that the rail sector must be excluded from the scope of the CRA 
proposal. A non-exclusion of the rail sector from the CRA would result in high risk of overlap with the existing rail 
regulations and a huge financial impact on the European rail supply industry putting at risk its competitiveness  
(e.g. product definition in railway, safety & security lifetime ). To be compatible to the rail sector, t he CRA proposal 
would need to be substantially modified to avoid complex and burdensome overlaps with the existing rail 
regulations and standard isation framework, verification processes, as well as other European cyber -relevant 
legislation such as the Artificial Intelligence Act, the NIS2 Directive, the Data Act.  
UNIFE considers that the best way to ensure a cyber secure and resilient European rail sector is to implement 
appropriate cyber security provisions in the existing European rail regulation following careful consultation with 
all the European rail sector stakeholders and considering the measures already being taken by the railway sector

1. The European rail sector and the impact of the CRA on the 
competitiveness of the European rail supply industry 
Rail transport is a complex and sophisticated system  of systems  or subsystems requiring multiple vendors and their 
technologies to cooperate and work together . Rail products from various suppliers are combined into subsystems which 
are in  turn integrated in an overall system at the final stage to be placed in service. The large spatial scale and geographical 
distribution of the rail system, the co-existence of technology multi generation solution increasing the complexity of the rail 
system in future together with the increasing degree of connectivity and touchpoints between the infrastructure and rolling 
stock is creating an enlargement of their attack surface (e.g. between on-board equipment with track signalling). The need 
for a common approach to cybersecurity for all rail components/elements , adequately  considering the different rail 
stakeholders and their responsibilities in the rail ecosystem, is therefore recognised and must be defined carefully before 
being implemented. A holistic approach will minimise the risk of cybersecurity breaches. 
 
The following chapter provides an introduction to the existing  legislative framework , operational model and active 
cybersecurity initiatives of the European railway sector there by identifying areas for which the compatibility with the CRA 
proposal and its applicability to the rail sector is challenged by the European rail supply industry.  
► The legislative framework of the European rail sector 
UNIFE supports the existing rail  legislation in force, most notably the Technical Pillar of the Fourth Railway Package 
consisting of the Regulation (EU) 2016/796 on the European Union Agency for Railways (ERA), Directive (EU) 2016/797 
on the interoperability of the rail system within the European Union and  Directive (EU) 2016/798 on railway safety . The 
creation of a Single European Railway Area (SERA) by increasing technical and operational interoperability of the different 
national rail networks across the EU has been a key objective of Europe’s transport policy since the mid-1990s. To achieve 
this, the European Union has developed a common technical framework, primarily via the Interoperability Directive and its 
supporting Technical Specifications for Interoperability (TSIs) as the key tools in driving the  gradual harmonisation of the 
railway systems and in fulfilling the essential requirements to be met by the European rail system. These TSIs (addressing 
different rail subsystems)  are managed and regularly reviewed by the E RA following a mandate of the Eur opean 
Commission. 
 
As set out in the above directives, before being used in service/operation the different rail subsystems and vehicles must 
receive the applicable authorisation from the respective National Safety Authority (NSA) or the E RA. These authorisation 
processes already include rigorous safety requirements and assessment, including cyber risk assessment and 
cybersecurity requirements for some of the most important rail subsystems , as safety and cybersecurity are intrinsically 
linked and bound in Railway standards. Once authorised to be placed on the market or in service the railway undertakings 
and infrastructure managers are responsible for the operation and often the maintenance of these systems under their own 
respective Single Sa fety Certifications  and/or Safety Authorisations  granted by the NSAs/ERA  and their Safety 
Management Systems. 
 
Similarly, changes to existing assets may require reauthorisation by the NSA or ERA. This means that the manufacturer 
would need to repeat some of the processes carried out during the initial certification and authorisation process. This can 
also be triggered by the application of security patches, which means that vulnerability management has the potential to 
impact both systems currently in operation, as well as those that are still under development. Therefore, an agile process 
should be defined for the management of cyber vulnerabilities, which is compatible with the existing rail regulation 
and authorisation processes.

► Timelines compatible with the rail sector 
The manufacturing process of rail products is lengthy and complex with the final step of the manufacturing process  
managed by the rail system integrator. For the delivery of a rail subsystem or system (e.g. signalling subsystem or a 
complete vehicle), it is common for the complete verification process of a vehicle, including the contract, design phase and 
production phases, to typically last up to 7 years  before the corresponding subsystem or system (vehicle) is placed into 
service or placed on the market . In addition, once produced and in operation the lifecycle of railway systems is commonly 
above 30 years, so many s ystems in place today are based on legacy technologies  and new products (from a 
manufacturing perspective) coexist with products that were installed many years ago, following the principle of non -
obsolescence. 
 
As a general rule, the rail sector needs pred ictability and transparency of the applicable requirements in order to 
successfully design, develop and deliver vehicle products to the market using the standard long-term contracts applicable, 
to minimise industrial risk and to guarantee the investment of  all stakeholders for the projects already signed and 
calculated. Only as an exceptional case of a major critical safety issue should new requirements invalidate a vehicle design 
and require mandatory modifications to existing vehicle designs and/or vehicles/infrastructure in operation. 
 
The requirements and corresponding deadlines introduced by the CRA are not compatible with the processes in 
the rail sector . The application of the CRA  risks significant disruption to be caused to on -going railway projects  and 
vehicles in operation resulting in detrimental costs linked to project delays, redesign and reauthorisation. Contracts for rail 
systems that are going to be delivered after mid-2025 (end of transition period) have already been signed and the systems 
are already in development. 
► Enhancing cybersecurity in European rail regulation  
The European Commission's 2020 Mandate to the European Union Railway Agency (ERA)  for the ‘Digital rail and Green 
freight TSI revision package (2022 revision)’ contained actions on cyber security. This can be found in the concluding ERA 
Report on TSI Recommendation 2022 3 as change requests (CR) No. 242 and 337 – more information in Annex I of this 
paper. However, both of these CRs were not  addressed during the process of the 2022 TSI revision (primarily due to 
resource constraints of ERA) and therefore no proposals made within the final recommendation of the ERA to the EC in 
June 2022.  
 
UNIFE believes the implementation of these change req uests, in conjunction with existing measures, will result 
in an adequate level of cybersecurity for the rail sector.  This is the most promising way to efficiently address 
cybersecurity in the rail sector and avoid additional legislation in an already heavily regulated sector. UNIFE is ready to 
discuss with the European Commission about the enhancement of the cybersecurity requirements included today in the 
European rail regulation.  A strong cooperation between ERA and ENISA wi ll be key  to ensure an efficient 
implementation of cyber provisions in the rail sector benefiting from the return of experience of other sectors 
► Cybersecurity in railway standardisation 
The European rail supply industry is putting huge efforts to protect rail systems from exploited cybersecurity vulnerabilities. 
Currently, IEC 62443 4 - Security for Industrial Automation and Control Systems - is the main existing cybersecurity 
standard. In the rail sector, it is already a common practice to follow the guide lines’ requirements for manufacturing the 
products according to the IEC 62443 series – notably the 62443-4-1/62443-4-2. It is important to underline that the widely 
used standard IEC 62443 series is increasingly applied to products of the rail sector. Therefore, it is assumed that rail 
products complying with this standard already address state-of-art cybersecurity requirements which are fit for industrial 
components and systems. It is also worthwhile to highlight that t his standard is not only consistent with the requirements 
in the CRA  but also goes beyond  considering the preparedness for possible cyber -attacks. It also  aims at business 
continuity includingcyber resilience. 
 
Moreover, its railway-specific adaptation CENELEC CLC/TS 50701:20215 ‘Railway applications – Cybersecurity’ has been 
approved and was officially published in June 2021. This Technical Specification is considered a major landmark for the

► Impact on the competitiveness of the European rail supply industry 
UNIFE considers that the CRA proposal will have significant impacts on  the European rail supply industry, placing 
additional burden on the activity and trade at EU level, in several key directions:  
• Disruptions of the rail supply chain: in the rail sector, SMEs are very important. SMEs often supply critical products 
and for such companies, complying with the CRA regulation proposal could be extremely difficult. They would need to 
adapt their products, assume the costs for certification and implement cybersecurity policies in a very short period of 
time. This may also lead to disruptions  for larger companies, that  also need to quickly adapt their products and 
procedures.  
• International competitiveness of the EU rail supply industry: The CRA requires changes to development and 
manufacturing processes, which incur additional costs and lead to higher prices. This will impact the competitiveness 
of the European rail supply industry when operating in international markets that do not impos e similarly strict 
requirements, compared to companies operating in non-EU countries. Whereas, it cannot be assumed or guaranteed 
that the CRA will become a global reference and thereby placing the European rail supply industry at a disadvantage. 
• Higher costs of rail products: It can already be stated that if the requirements proposed in the CRA are implemented, 
the cost of all related products and software will increase significantly, as the new proposed approach requires an 
increase in resources and effort to produce and manufacture such products. This may lead to higher costs of railway 
transport affecting the competitiveness of the railways compared to e.g. private transport (since ticket prices would 
have to be increased in order to bear the costs). This in turn has a negative impact on other strategic goals of the EU 
agenda (e.g., reduce CO2 emissions, etc.) 
 
UNIFE also notes that the study conducted by the European Commission to assess cybersecurity requirements 7 
makes no reference to cybersecurity in the rail sector. Therefore UNIFE is concerned that the assessment on the 
cybersecurity requirements in the rail sector made by the European Commission was not complete and the impact 
of the CRA on the competitiveness of the European rail supply industry not considered. 
2. Additional constraints imposed by the CRA and modifications 
required to be compatible with the European rail sector  
The Cyber Resilience Act is very ambitious and would represent a major step change for digital resilience in the European 
rail sector. As such it has to be underlined that a non-exclusion of the European rail sector from its scope would significantly 
harm the European rail supply industry . In the case the rail sector is not excluded from the scope of CRA, it is essential 
that the new requirements included in the  CRA are compatible with the existing European rail legislative framework and 
operational model of the European rail sector.  
The following chapter presents an analysis of additional constraints that will be imposed by specific CRA articles if the rail 
sector is not excluded from it s scope  and potential recommendations to remedy these . UNIFE call s the European 
Commission, Member States and the European Parliament to assess carefully the particularities of the European rail sector 
before deciding on its inclusion in the CRA. 
UNIFE’s opinion is that to increase cyber resilience  of the European rail sector in scope of the CRA the following areas 
must be addressed in the CRA proposal:

► Timelines compatible with the rail sector 
Cyber resilience is a building process requiring time, especially in the industrial sectors such as rail where the verification 
process of a vehicle or a complex signalling system , including the design phase and production phases, can last up to 7 
years. To upgrade or develop new products complying with the CRA’s requirements, the schedule must be compatible with 
the processes in the rail sector . For the CRA to be compatible with the rail sector, additional definitions (Article 3) are 
required which respect the timelines of the verification process and transitional rules applicable to rail projects as defining 
by the rail Interoperability Directive and TSI LOC&PAS 2022, such as ‘project at an advance stage of development’, ‘initial 
assessment framework’, ‘certification framework’, ‘design phase’ and ‘production phase’  to make sure rail projects have 
the stability required to be viable . UNIFE believes that the proposed timetable included in the CRA is very ambitious. 24 
months to implement all technical changes and procedures in the railway sector is too short. 12 months to start notifying 
ENISA is also very short, especially considering that the update of critical products via delegated acts will take up to 12 
months after the entry into force of this act (according to Recital (69) and Article 6 (3) and Article 50, respectively). 
 
Moreover, the obligation of reporting actively exploited vulnerabilities shall be extended to 24 months after the entry into 
force of the CRA proposal (according to Recital 69), allowing companies to set up their process for reporting exploited 
vulnerabilities in the rail sector. 
► Impacts on the manufacturing processes 
UNIFE believes that the statement "any compliance costs for businesses would be outweighed by the benefits brought by 
a higher level of security of products with digital elements and ultimately an increase of trust of users in these products” 
fails to recognize the impact the CRA would have to the European rail supply industry. The industry would need to go 
through significant changes both in processes and technology in a very short time that is without precedent for the sector. 
Financial mechanisms will be needed to support companies in this process. Such support is vital to avoid disruption in the 
European rail supply industry, particularly to the SMEs who have less resources and capacity for rapid adaptation to new 
requirements. As such we consider the following financial support from the European Commission will be needed: 
 
a) Similar financial mechanisms applicable for cyber security in the rail supply industry as the Connecting Europe Facility 
(CEF) to support the rail industry in the transition and supporting companies to be more competitive in the internal 
market. 
b) Financial and technical support to SMEs in order to adapt to this new regulation (e.g., certification bodies, guidance 
in placing the new cyber security policies, economic support for new investments for the re -engineering processes, 
etc). 
► Change in mindset towards higher costs 
While efforts to address cybersecurity are not new, the CRA proposal brings cybersecurity to a new paradigm. If the CRA 
obligations are applied to the rail sector, this will lead to a general costs increase of all the products in the rail sector. This 
requires a change of mindset in the rail sector and acceptance that the rail products will be more costly.  
UNIFE considers that campaigns to raise awareness across the EU rail sector and simulation exercises would be needed 
in order to analyse the possible risks and to be able to materialise the benefit for paying more for a product. UNIFE calls 
the European Commission to support the European rail sector with this objective if the CRA becomes applicable to rail. 
► Appropriate allocation of responsibilities 
According to the operational mode of the rail sector , it is the responsibility of manufacturers to provide products /systems 
that do not have known vulnerabilities. However, once authorised to be placed on the market or in ser vice the railway 
undertakings and infrastructure managers are responsible for the operation  and often the maintenance  of the se 
products/systems. Therefore, the following rules would need to  apply in order to have coherent and balanced 
responsibilities of rail stakeholders within the CRA:  
• Obligations of manufacturers : The manufacturer shall be responsible for ensuring that the pr oduct has been 
designed, developed and produced in accordance with the essential requirements set out in Section 1 of Annex I  of 
the CRA proposal, shall undertake an assessment of the cybersecurity risks, and follow the obligations described in 
Article 10, including a vulnerability disclosure policy.  
• Reporting obligations of manufacturers and the “business consumer” or “busines s users” : However, 
manufacturer is not the main obligation party when it comes to certain obligations including reporting obligations. 
Firstly, the rail undertakings and infrastructure managers are the ones operating and most of the time maintaining the

technologies, and it might happen that an exploited vulnerability is known by the rail undertaking or infrastructure 
manager prior to the manufacturers, as they are the ones operating the technology. For this reason, it is also important 
to discuss together with the European Commission and the rail sector about the ‘vulnerability handling’ process of the

Primary obligation of the manufacturer to report incidents and exploited vulnerabilities (Article 11(1) & 11(2)) is ineffective

ineffective and extremely costly, resulting in “foolproof” devices with any misuse being “foreseen” ( according to Article 
5(1)). While this approach is defendable for retailed mass consumer market and business-to-consumer sectors, this is not 
valid for business-to-business sectors such as the railways where skills and responsibilities of largescale users are 
comparable to the manufacturers. 
► Vulnerability handling over the lifecycle of railway products 
In order to maintain in a secure state the products, subsystems and systems, the CRA considers it is the responsibility of 
the manufacturer to deliver patches for the expected product lifetime up to a maximum of 5 years (according to Article 
10(6)), which would clearly not cover the typical li fecycle of rail products and systems more than 30 years as mentioned 
earlier. In order to ensure that rail products are cyber resilient for their life cycle, UNIFE considers the CRA shall consider 
the entire lifetime rail products and the particular characteristics of the rail sector.  
 
Manufacturers can deliver patches in the case of exploited vulnerabilities occur, during the two  first years after delivering 
the system, as indicated in the EU guarantee of the products. However, manufacturers cannot be responsible for installing 
the patches. In the rail sector this is a highly costly procedure with the interaction with different subsystems.  During the 
lifetime of the products, in order to maintain the products as cyber secured and cyber resilient, the vulnerability handling 
and installation of patches shall be under the responsibility of the “business consumer” , “business user” or the 
‘cybersecurity maintenance supplier’ as defined in Annex I below. Due to the complexity of railway systems, patches can 
be de livered via service contracts with a benefit that the initial investment cost does not increase so much but their 
associated costs are distributed over the life cycle. 
 
The vulnerabilities handling is an extra cost for the rail sector as in order to implement patches, especially in critical 
products, it might require to re -open the safety case , leading to a recertification of the product and ultimately a  new 
authorisation of the rail vehicle type and an updated conformity to type authorisation for all vehicles in operation . Such a 
process might impact traffic management or maintenance activities as it could lead to the non-operation of trains for an 
unknown period of time. It is a very costly procedure and it shall be responsibility of the rail undertaking or infrastructure 
managers as owner of the systems. 
 
Moreover, the process for managing exploited vulnerabilities is not well defined in the CRA. The management of exploited 
vulnerabilities is a very sensitive issue related to IPR and sometimes to trade or commercial secrets ( according to Article 
11 and Article 52 ). Therefore, the process for managing exploited vulnerabilities shall be clarified and sanctions shall be 
imposed on any legal or natural person disclosing this kind of information. 
 
Annex I article 1 (2) states that “Products with digital elements shall be delivered without any known  exploitable 
vulnerabilities”. As stated before, products in the rail domain have  a serie s of activities after product finalisation ( e.g. 
integration test, factory acceptance test, site acceptance test, assessment). Current products contain a significant  amount 
of software (including standard operating systems and open source software ), it is therefore not predictable for any given 
moment, that a product is without any known exploitable vulnerabilities. A similar issue is addressed in the IEC 62443 6-2 
Evaluation method for IACS components security requirement (IEC 62443 4 -2). We stron gly advise to rephrase this 
requirement to “Products with digital elements for critical infrastructure shall be delivered only if there is a vulnerabilit y

management process in place which tracks the vulnerabilities of the product to closure. Known exploit able vulnerabilities 
of critical infrastructure products shall be addressed in a time-frame agreed with the customer” 
 
Annex I article 2 (4) states that “once a security update has been made available, publicly disclose information 
about fixed vulnerabilities”. Due to the planning time required for installing updates in a rail system, this requirement lowers 
the security for rail systems. Attackers today are analysing publicly available security updates and develop attack methods 
in a very short time (typic ally in a few days). This makes attack tools earlier available than the rail operators could install 
the updates. Therefore, this requirement should distinguish between products that can be patched quickly (as consumer 
goods and device in non-critical infrastructures). For devices in critical infrastructure the requirement should be rephrased 
to “once a security update has been made available, information about fixed vulnerabilities shall be shared with the 
customers”. 
 
Finally, all the products supplied fo r systems related to past contracts, i.e. contracts signed before the date of entry into 
force of the CRA proposal, shall not be subject to apply the requirements of the CRA proposal. These contracts shall be 
subject to follow the “grandfathering” principl e and considered as exempt. Some of the rail products/concepts are based 
on elements (e.g. protocols), that cannot be altered easily without jeopardising large infrastructure investments. 
► Definition of ‘products’ 
The definition of products (according to Article 3) is very broad, and the proposal for certifying 90% of the products via self-
assessment is very ambitious.  In its proposal, the European Commission differentiates between three types of products 
with digital elements: products with digital elements, critical products with digital elements and highly critical products with 
digital elements. UNIFE appreciates this approach in principle as such a differentiation follows the necessary risk -based 
approach. However, UNIFE considers the categories of products with digital elements to be defined as ‘critical’ and ‘highly 
critical’, which include, for example, microcontrollers, industrial automation and control systems or parts of the Industrial 
Internet of Things, as too broad. 
 
Rather than addressing product groups in terms of a general criticality, the EU co-legislators should consider the intended 
use of a product with digital elements, as one and the same product can perform a more or less critical function according 
to the concrete applica tion scenario. This shall be specified by the “business consumer” or “business user” as proposed 
and defined Annex I.  
 
In addition, considering the complexity of the rail sector products that are integrated to, at the end, have a system of 
subsystems, a process-wise approach shall be considered instead of a product-based approach for the rail sector. 
► Penalties 
In order to ensure that all manufacturers fulfil requirements (according to Articles 10, 11, 13 or 14 respectively) relating to 
the essential requirements and the procedures on vulnerability handling according to CRA Annex I, as well as the articles 
relating to the cooperation with market surveillance bodies, the introduction of fines is justified.  
In general, UNIFE perceives the differentiation according to three types of misconduct proposed as acceptable and would 
like to propose an additional type of penalty linked to the leak of confidential information . However, UNIFE urges the co-
legislators to take a company’s annual turnover stemming from the specific product as a basis for the level of fines rather 
than the annual worldwide turnover of the entire company, as proposed by the European Commission. Too stringent fines 
may discourage companies to investment in products with digital elements and thereby reduce competition on the market 
for these products. Please find in the Annex I of this paper proposals regarding the level of penalties to apply. 
► Overlapping with existent cyber-relevant legislation  
UNIFE considers t here might be an overlap and conflict with other existing or upcoming cyber relevant legislation, a 
situation which must be avoided. In particular, the AI Act, the NIS2 Directive and the Data Act should be looked at here. 
• The Artificial Intelligence Act excludes the transport sectors and therefore it excludes rail. As a result the AI measures 
of this act should not follow the approach included in the CRA (according to Article 8). 
• The NIS2 Directive introduces reporting obligation for ‘important entities’, in the case of the rail sector being the large 
manufacturers. Therefore, there might be an overlapping. In addition, the national cybersecurity strategies as 
introduced by the NIS2 Directive, shall include provisions on cyber resilience (according to Article 11) 
• The Data Act has not yet been approved and the overlap with this legislation should be closely monitored.

► Responsibilities 
Based on the responsibilities found within the railway sector, UNIFE propose t wo new definitions to be included for the 
business sectors (according to Article 3):  
• ‘Business consumer’ or ‘Business user’ means any natural or legal person that is the end user, that will operate 
the product, responsible for its operation, that takes the ownership as indicated by a signed contract, and responsible 
to install the patches in the exploited vulnerabilities unless by means of a contract, he authorizes other physical or 
legal entity to maintenance the possible exploited vulnerabilities. 
• ‘Cybersecurity maintenance supplier’ means any natural or legal person that is authorized by  the “business 
consumer “or “owner” to maintain by means of a contract the system cyber secure. 
 
In addition, the reporting obligations of manufacturers  (according to Article 11) shall be modified to add the ‘Business 
consumer” or “Business user” in the responsibilities for reporting.  If the ‘Business consumer” or “ Business user” are the 
ones notifying a vulnerability, they need to notify the manufacturer as well together with the notification to ENISA. An 
acknowledgement of the manufacturer shall be received and this shall be the date to start counting to deliver the patch, 
during the two first years after delivering the product. 
► Penalties 
As referenced earlier, please find below proposals regarding the level of penalties to apply: 
(a) The non-compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements laid down in Annex I and the obligations set out in 
Articles 10 and 11 shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 15 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, 
up to 2.5 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year stemming from sales of the respective 
product with digital elements, whichever is higher. 
(b) The non-compliance with any other obligations under this Regulation shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 
10 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 2 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding 
financial year stemming from sales of the respective product with digital elements, whichever is higher. 
(c) The supply of incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to notified bodies and market surveillance authorities in 
reply to a request shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 5 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, 
up to 1 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year stemming from sales of the respective 
product with digital elements, whichever is higher. 
(d) The leak of confidential information related to the disclosure of a vulnerability th at might affect the reputation of a 
manufacturer shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 15 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, up 
to 2.5 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher.

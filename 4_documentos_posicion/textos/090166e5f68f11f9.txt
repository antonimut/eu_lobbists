The European Federation of Journalists (EFJ) welcomes the European
Commission's proposed European Media Freedom Act. It is an important and
overdue legislative tool to strengthen free and pluralistic media systems, to
protect journalists and editorial independence, to protect public service
media from undue interference and guarantee its funding, to shed more
transparency on media ownership and state advertisement, and to create
more trust in the fragile media sector across Europe. However, to reach its
described objectives it must be strengthened and its sometimes incoherent
and vague language adapted so that it is in line with and enforces established
case law and international standards on media freedom. The EFJ proposes
urgent amendments which would guarantee a Media Freedom Act fulfilling its
proclaimed promises.
  
As confirmed by the Centre for Media Pluralism and Media Freedom (CMPF),
“times were ripe to give the European Union new tools to protect and foster
journalism as a public good in a digital environment.” The Media Pluralism
Monitor (MPM) 2022 findings highlight many risks for media pluralism and
media freedom, including that market concentration in the traditional media
sector is increasing and is at high risk in most MSs, combined with the rising
market power of a few digital intermediaries. 
The EFJ believes that the protection of media freedom and pluralism is a
fundamental right enshrined in the Charter, and a sine qua non for the rule of
law and democratic accountability in the Union, as shown by Commission
2022 Rule of Law Report (1). The EFJ welcomes that journalisms’ societal role
as a public good is acknowledged, which is so important at a time when
journalists are under unprecedented threats from many sides. Public goods
need to be protected legally, financially and socially. When it comes to
journalism, we need to make sure there’s additional protection.

On the scope: The EFJ, in line with the European Data Protection Supervisor
(2), recommends firstly to clarify the scope of the Regulation, which should
not only cover media service providers but all journalists independent of their
employment contract. This is vital to protect sources and editorial
independence as well as freelance journalists, already much less protected
than staff journalists. Therefore, the EFJ recommends adding an explicit
reference to journalists to the relevant provisions of the future Regulation, so
as to clarify that any journalist, including freelance or self-employed and
other media actors who contribute to the “public debate” (3), would fall
within the scope of the future Regulation and could also rely on a robust
protection of journalistic sources and communication and editorial
independence. 
On terms and expressions: The following terms and expressions should be
further clarified in corresponding recitals (and/or, where relevant, in the
definitions’ list of Article 2), in order to ensure more clarity and a harmonised
application of the EMFA: (i) media service providers; this definition is taken
from Article 2.4 of the Copyright Directive and thus focuses on a ‘traditional’
notion of press publications, i.e., those targeted by news aggregators or
media monitoring services, but the Committee of Ministers of the Council of
Europe has also recommended to “adopt a new, broad notion of media
which encompasses all actors involved in the production and dissemination,
to potentially large numbers of people, of content”..• (ii) “Media pluralism”
and “Significant impact on media pluralism and editorial independence”
(article 21) • (iii) “Substantial influence on the formation of public opinion”
(recital 39). We prefer that the terms “trustworthy information” (recitals 11, 13
& 31) & “trustworthy media content” (recital 16) are not used.

Article 7: While the EMFA calls for sufficient resources for independent
regulatory authorities (NRAs) at national level, the EFJ demands for
proportionate and sustainable resources that correspond to the additional
tasks and enforceable ways that guarantee their independence. The EMFA
should further strengthen such safeguards on the necessary requirements for
NRAs to ensure their effective independence, including sufficient human and
financial resources (10). In addition, the regulation should provide details
beyond the provisions in the AVMSD on the requirements for NRAs to be
effectively independent at national level. Such independence is key for the
proper enforcement of the EMFA. The implementation of the AVMSD (Art. 30
/30a) must be assured.

Article 8-12: European Board for Media Services: There is widespread
concern, the EFJ shares, that a new European Board for Media Services
(EBMS), to replace the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media
Services (ERGA) lacks independence from the European Commission. Their
opinions and actions should not be executed ‘in agreement with the
Commission’ or “at the request of the Commission”, but also at its own
initiative. The EFJ has chosen the sub-option B, a governance system based
on a board assisted by an independent EU office with enough resources to
fulfil their extended tasks justified by a perceived lack of coordination in
developing new standards on media pluralism and freedom across the EU.
Only this option can guarantee the board’s independence. The EFJ endorses
ERGA in their adopted position to ensure that the wording of Article 9 fully
reflects the independence both of the Board and the national regulatory
authorities which it sets out to guarantee, and preserves this independence
from any institution, including from the European Commission (as described
by the ERGA with reference to Article 11 and 12) (11).
Article 13:  The EFJ recommends broadening its scope of Art 13, (para 2) and
also include the risks to the political independence of the regulatory
authorities and bodies. 
Article 17: On content moderation by very large online platforms, the EFJ
welcomes the inclusion of media-specific obligations for the online
environment, complementing the DSA, but is concerned that Article 17
effectively leaves platforms as the final arbiters of who is eligible for such
media privilege. Assessing the editorial independence of media service
providers is an extremely complex task. With the lack of legal certainty, this
cannot be left to privately owned platform providers (12). For the EFJ any
content moderation should be extended to journalists, bloggers and other
media actors with a public interest function to achieve a public interest
framework.

Article 20.4: The EFJ proposes also granting the European Board for Media
Services the right to draw up an own-initiative opinion where a national
legislative, regulatory or administrative measure is likely to affect the
functioning of the internal market for media services. 
Article 21: The concentration of media ownership is recognised as a unique
danger to media pluralism and editorial independence and some safeguards
(objective, non-discriminatory and proportionate criteria) are introduced via
impact assessment of media mergers on media plurality (Article 21). The EFJ
welcomes such principles and believes that in those MSs where it is not yet
the case, the assessment of media market concentration should be enforced
by law (see recital 40). There should be a formal obligation for the National
Competition authorities (NCAs) to consult the NRAs on the effects of the
foreseen transaction on media pluralism and possible remedies. The advisory
nature of the proposed provisions risks not responding to the high risk
regarding media concentration surveyed by the MPM. The EFJ proposes that
the board assisted by the European Commission should issue guidelines on
public interest tests of media mergers as a minimum action and include civil
society (21.3). While market concentration may not have a negative effect on
media freedom where, for example, it helps smaller media outlets to survive,
and where internal safeguards are guaranteed, it is absolutely necessary to
act both at national and at European level against concentration where it
leads to information monopolies. 
The EU must take action in particular against media capture by media moguls
and oligarchs who are often in close relations with leading politicians in the
country, or even governments of third countries. Therefore, guidance and
enforcement on media mergers’ public interest tests are crucial. 
Article 22: The EFJ recommends also granting the European Board for Media
Services the right to draw up an own-initiative opinion on media market
concentrations. 
Article 24: The EFJ welcomes the introduction of basic criteria for the
allocation of state advertising. This form of public funding can have a
significant impact on the independence and sustainability of media actors.
This is why it is encouraging to see the European Commission mandating that
its allocation follows transparent, objective, proportionate and non-
discriminatory criteria and procedure. However, the EFJ objects to the criteria
used in the text, i.e. the safeguards excludes local governments with less
than 1 million inhabitants. Should this remain in, governments who do not want
their expenditure to be scrutinised could use direct funding through local
governments.
Article 25 and 26: On monitoring and evaluation: there is a clear lack of
effective enforcement. The EFJ believes that the EMFA fails to offer proper
enforcement of the existing and newly established media rules.

Final remarks
The EFJ calls on the co-legislators to pay due attention to the proposal
and to work towards strengthening it in order to achieve a regulation
that serves journalists, media freedom, media pluralism and democracy
in the EU before the next elections in 2024. The European Media
Freedom Act should not be seen as a single EU measure to protect
media freedom and media pluralism, but as part of a larger package of
initiatives included in the European Democracy Action Plan (EDAP); a
strong directive on SLAPP; a good implementation of the Audiovisual
Media Service Directive and the Digital Service Act (DSA) and financial
support for journalism as a public good at arms’ length both a European,
national, regional and local level. The proposal by the European
Parliament to create a permanent News Media Fund is an appreciated
start, as media viability is the missing economic aspect of the EMFA.
Adopted by EFJ Steering Committee, Brussels, 13 January 2023

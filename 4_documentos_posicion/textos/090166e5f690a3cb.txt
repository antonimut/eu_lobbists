EMMA – ENPA comments on the European Media Freedom Act proposal 
19 January 2023 
 
The European Magazine Media Association (EMMA) and the European Newspaper Publishers’ Association 
(ENPA), that represent the vast majority of European publishers of newspapers, magazines, periodicals 
and specialised press both in digital and in print,  appreciate this opportunity to provide feedback on the 
proposal for a European Media Freedom Act (EMFA). EMMA and ENPA represent European press 
publishers from a wide va riety of EU Member States, including both Member States that are a global 
benchmark for press and media freedom and Member States where the situation is more fragile. Our 
feedback is therefore intended as reflecting the variety of legal frameworks in the Member States, as well 
as the specificities of each national media market. 
 
Whilst this feedback to the EMFA proposal should not be understood as a final official position paper, we 
still wanted  to hereby share our initial reactions  to the Commission’s proposal. We believe that the 
perspective of press publishers will  and should be of interest for policymakers not only as stakeholders 
directly impacted by the EMFA, but even more so as the inclusion of the press within the scope of a general 
EU media regulation is unprecedented. Indeed, the EMFA would constitute the first regulation of the printed 
and digital press at the level of the European Union. 
 
Press freedom is at the core of press publishers’ activities and the necessary precondition f or a thriving 
and pluralistic media landscape, online as well as offline. As highlighted in the Preamble, the media are key 
for the green and digital transition of the EU economy and essential for a healthy civic sphere and 
fundamental rights. Hence, while we fully support the Commission’s general objective to safeguard press 
and media freedom  in Europe , and appreciate the attention given to this important matter , the EMFA 
provides a deeply problematic policy choice. Not only is there no EU competence for  a general media 
regulation such as the EMFA , as noted by the national parliaments of several Member States , but the 
chosen legal basis as well as several concerning provisions of the EMFA  would also lead to grave 
interferences with functioning national media systems, whilst at the same time not addressing the root 
cause of the issues identified in certain Member States. 
 
Furthermore, the  EMFA fails to take into account that the main threats to media pluralism and 
independence today are economical, with the growing difficulties for media outlets to generate sufficient 
revenues to fund editorial content - which is quite para doxical, as it takes the functioning of the internal 
market as its main legal basis. Considering the objectives, it is even more problematic that the narrative of 
the EMFA proposal might undermine trust in media as it depicts the sector as lacking independence and 
needing control and regulation. Therefore, should the work on this proposal continue, it should at the very 
least consider the redlines described below in order not to harm the sector it aims to support. 
 
• Concerns on EU competence and the EMFA’s legal basis 
 
Given the lack of clear competence for the EU to regulate media, the EMFA is based on the premise of the 
existence of an “EU media market” and that obstacles to its well-functioning justify the choice of a binding 
and directly applicable Regulation, thereby harmonising the rules applicable to all media across the Union 
and all national markets . We are not aware of  any internal  market problems which would justify  the 
Regulation. Furthermore, we criticise the very notion of an “EU media market”. Due to linguistic, cultural, 
demographic, legal and political differences and national, regional and local specificities , there is no EU 
single market for media. This reality still makes Member States well-suited and best equipped to manage 
their media markets. 
 
We also note that several provisions go beyond  mere market considerations and attempt to regulate the 
very functioning and internal decisions of media outlets. T he EU only has a supportive role in the policy 
area of culture and media, which lies primarily in the Member States’ exclusive competence. In light of the 
subsidiarity principle, the EU should refrain from any general media regulation such as the EMFA. Instead,

the EU can and should provide political pr essure and recommendations 1 to Member States, where 
necessary. Lastly, as the media freedom problems in a minority of Member States are often rooted in an 
overarching rule of law issue in those countries,  they should rather be addressed through the rule of law 
conditionality mechanism and other available tools. 
 
With regards to the specific situation of the press sector, we would like to highlight that historically the 
press has been exempted from any ex-ante regulation, due to its sensitive role in our democracies and 
due to the absence of practical limitations i.e. a limited number of frequencies. As a consequence, press 
publications do not need any prior authorisation or licence , and self-regulation, combined with ex-post 
treatment on a case-by-case basis by the judicial system when needed, has proven to be a successful and 
best-suited solution to promote a critical and pluralistic democratic debate, free from political interferences. 
Until the EMFA, the EU had also refrained from proposing any binding general media regulation2 and has 
rightfully differentiated between audiovisual media services and other media3. We criticise this horizontal 
one-size-fits-all approach as it does not take into accou nt the specific characteristics of each media and 
attempts to de facto extend EU competences into media policy. 
 
• Restrictions to press publishers’ editorial freedom 
 
Editorial freedom is a fundamental condition for democracy and enables a free and critical public debate. 
The first defender of this freedom and of a publication’s editorial line is its publisher. Therefore, any 
European regulation interfering in the relat ionship between publishers and editorial teams would be 
counterproductive, incompatible with the rules governing the internal market and altogether harmful to 
press freedom and pluralism. Therefore Article 6(2), recitals 20 and 21, as well as any further restriction or 
interference in the organisation of editorial teams and day-to-day decisions taken from the 
Recommendation, are unacceptable.  
This should, however, not be conf used with the sensible Article 4, which  deals with external political 
interferences in editorial decisions and the protection of journalists and journalistic sources.  
The notable difference between articles 4 and 6(2) is that the former protects from external interferences, 
while the latter interferes in the normal management and functioning of media outlets. Not only is it part of 
publishers’ freedom to decide on a functioning system for their editorial teams, generally influenced by 
national traditions which differ from Member State to Member State. But most of all, the ability of publishers 
to interact with the newsroom is justified by its mission to protect journalism, by bearing all the legal, 
economic, and reputational risks of editorial decisions.  
As such, press freedom includes the right of the publisher to adopt edi torial guidelines, to leave editorial 
freedom fully to the editorial teams, or to exercise this freedom on a case-by-case basis. Any restriction to 
this fundamental and sensitive freedom by the EU equates to a restriction of press freedom and, finally, of 
pluralism. Therefore, we oppose any EU regulatory interference in the internal functioning of press 
publications, be it the composition of editorial teams, the determination of the editorial line and day-to-day 
decisions of the editorial teams, the adoptio n of guidelines as well as any obligation to adopt a general 
editorial line in agreement with the editors.  
We also oppose the related Article 25 and the Commission’s task provided by paragraph 3 to review and 
assess the measures taken by press publishers to ensure independent editorial decision, as it would not 
promote media pluralism but just bring the internal processes of a publishing house under political scrutiny.

• Unprecedented European regulatory oversight of the press 
 
The EMFA would, for the first time, bring the printed and digital press under the European regulatory 
oversight of the new European Board for Media Services (“Board”). This would eliminate a core component 
of press freedom, the principle that the free press is solely responsible for its content before the law and 
the courts. We oppose any regulatory oversight of the media at EU level beyond audiovisual media services 
as provided by  the AVMS Directive, as the regulatory oversight of b roadcasting arises from specific 
technical circumstances and should not be extended to the private press.  The special case of the press 
sector and the possible conflicts with national (constitutional) law have also been acknowledged by the 
European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA) in its own position paper on the EMFA, 
where ERGA “explicitly and unambiguously state that it is neither its vocation nor its intention to regulate 
the press sector”. 4  
 
Furthermore, we have grave concerns about the role and involvement of the Commission in the Board . 
While the press sector should be excluded from the oversight of the Board altogether, at the bare minimum 
the role and involvement of the Commission has to be completely removed from the tasks and operations 
of the Board as a guarantee against political interference. The independence of the Board is hardly 
justifiable if the Commission provides the Secretariat (Article 11), if the Commission shall be involved in 
the preparation of the work programme (Article 10), and if the Board almost exclusively acts “in support of 
the Commission”, “when requested by the Commission ”, “in agreement with the Commission ” etc (Art. 
12). As such, it must be ensured that the Board can operate free and independently from political 
institutions, including the Commission.  
 
• Media concentration rules add obstacles to pluralism-saving consolidation 
 
The provisions on media concentration in the EMFA are based on the assumption that media mergers and 
acquisitions are necessarily detrimental for media pluralism. On the contrary, media consolidation is an 
indispensable instrument to salvage media outlets during economic downturns and to better sustain the 
costs of running a professional media outlet. In fact, in several Member States media concentration rules 
have been softened or removed . As such, consolidations help preserve editorial teams and pluralism  on 
the market. Against this background, we do not see how Article 21 helps investments in media markets. 
For example, Article 21(2)c would make media consolidation overly difficult and provide that essentially 
only media businesses on the brink of insolvency can be acquired. This would make investments risky and 
unappealing and the procedures longer and more cumbersome, leading to the risk of decreasing media  
pluralism, while existing  EU and national competition law is suitable  to evaluate  media consolidat ion 
operations. 
 
• Lack of adequate protection for editorial content on very large online platforms 
 
The Digital Services Act (DSA), for the first time, legally authorised very large online platforms to remove 
legal editorial content on the basis of their terms and conditions (T&C), thus allowing T&C to determine the 
limits of press freedom, rather than the general laws. During the DSA debate the media never called for a 
blanket protection of all media content online. On the contrary, publishers and other media only called for 
a protection limited to legal content published under editorial responsibility  and liable under national and 
EU law, and only on very large online platforms  (VLOPS), which are increasingly important distribution 
channels for the press and media and critical for the formation of public opinion and the public debate.  It 
is an absolute necessity to protect press freedom from private interventions by VLOPs. 
 
While Article 17 attempts to remedy, or rather mitigate, the green light for VLOPs to delete legal content 
on the basis of their T&C,  it fails to provide an efficient and adequate solution for it and de facto even 
confirms the right for platforms to remove legal content on the basis of their T&C. While the early warning 
system introduced in the EMFA is a small improvement compared to the DSA, it is insufficient and does

not provide an appropriate protection for legal editorial content , but a mere procedural safeguard which  
has no concrete effect on the removal of conten t and does not fully account for the protection of media

interventions from VLOPs. 
 
• Audience measurement and state advertising 
 
Article 23 on audience measurement represents a step forward, acknowledging the need for transparency 
and comparability of the internal audience measurement by gatekeeper platforms. However, the definitions 
are not sufficiently clear, and it should be further and unmistakably clarified that the new rules apply to all 
audience measurement of the large platforms, and not just “users of media services”, which would leave 
our large parts of the big platforms’ users. If the requirement is to be applied to the large platforms as well, 
as implied in Recital 9, this should be stated more clearly.  It is important that the measurements of the 
platforms are accessible for external review in order to establish a level playing field. 
On the other hand, in light of the importance and specificities of regional and local media markets, the 
audience measurement provisions should also ensure that the markets considered are small enough to 
accurately showcase the relevance of local media in their area. This would prevent unbalanced rankings 
that would make it difficult for local media  to receive an investment proportionate to their relative 
importance.  
We support Article 24 on the fair and transparent allocation of state advertising. However, the transparency 
and fairness rules for public advertising allocation should also apply to public advertising allocated to 
platforms, while state advertising must also cover advertising from EU institutions and agencies.  
 
While these are preliminary comments, we will provide a finali sed position paper in due time and remain 
at the disposal of the Commission and of the co-legislators for any clarification.

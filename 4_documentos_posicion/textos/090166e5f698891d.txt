Cyber Resilience Act 
January 2023 
Ensuring effective cybersecurity regulation  
A business users’ perspective 
Executive Summary 
Effective cybersecurity regulation is crucial to enabling digital transformation of European 
companies. Beltug, Cigref, CIO Platform Nederland and Voice-e.v. welcome the Commission’s 
ambition to improve the cybersecurity of products with digital elements to “enable businesses 
to use products with digital elements securely ” in its Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). We support 
the focus on ensuring software security and the risk-based approach as core principles. 
Together we represent the communities of Chief Information Officers (CIO’s) and other senior 
leaders that are responsible for digital technologies and digital transformations within private 
or public organisations. All our members are European business users of digital technologies; 
they acquire, implement, and use digital technologies in practise. Thanks to our expertise, we 
recommend the EU co-legislators to focus on the objectives below to ensure a balanced and 
effective Cyber Resilience Act: 
 
I. Adopt a coherent approach with other EU legal instruments on cybersecurity  
II. Design a meaningful and practicable conformity assessment on the vulnerabilities 
III. Introduce the ‘poluter pays for the damage’ principle in software  
IV. Mitigate the risk of disclosure of the vulnerabilities 
V. Strengthen the provisions regarding software and open-source elements 
VI. Include cloud orchestration as critical products

VII. Definition and practical implementation of appropriate measures (art. 10.12) 
 
I. Adopt a coherent approach with other EU legal instruments on cybersecurity  
 
In terms of cybersecurity, the EU adopted several legal instruments that together constitute 
a solid corpus of texts. To ensure legal certainty and the effectiveness of EU cyberse curity 
policy, it is critical that the definitions in the CRA are aligned with the definitions and 
requirements developed in the NIS2 Directive recently adopted.  
Likewise, we recommend aligning the definition of “incident” on the one in article 4 of the 
NIS2 directive.  
The concepts of “resilience” and “exploitable vulnerabilities” could also be better defined in 
the regulation.   
As the technology is continuously developing, either as a result of or independent from 
legislation, it is probable that discussions will arise about a technology being in or out of scope 
of CRA (or another legal framework for cybersecurity) at some point in time , or definitions 
needing readjustment. There should be some mechanism for coordinating such developments 
in the scope, definitions etc. across the EU, and across the various cybersecurity related laws, 
to prevent loopholes from appearing or remaining in place to the de triment of cybersecurity 
within the EU. 
 
II. Design a meaningful and practicable conformity assessment on the vulnerabilities  
 
Annex I Section 1 point 2 of the CRA states that products with digital elements “shall be 
delivered without any known exploitable vulnerabilities”. While we understand -and support- 
the overall objective, we doubt the practicality of this provision. No product will ever be 100%

secure. Cybersecurity is a continuous process. Moreover, this provision does not integrate the 
necessary time between the development of a product and its commercialisation. The reverse 
effect of this provision is that it will prevent new products with better security processes to 
be placed on the market.  
Moreover, each vulnerability does not have the same level of impact. A risk -based approach 
seems more appropriate. Remediating to the most critical vulnerabilities must be a priority.  
We believe that the CRA should focus more on the reporting of significant incidents, just like 
the NIS2 notification obligation or international standards. This would avoid the burden of 
reporting minor or irrelevant incidents.  
III. Introduce the ‘poluter pays for the damage’ principle in software  
We believe that the CRA should include a provision regarding the compensation of the costs 
borne by business users that are a direct result of faulty software and digital products. Indeed, 
the management of the vulnerabilities is a growing burden for enterprises. They must bear 
the costs of frequently patching security leaks, taking mitigating measures for weaknesses 
that are not yet patched and other deficiencies in software they procure from a software 
provider. It is important to note that software providers often waive their liability and are able 
to do so due to dependency/lock -in of the user. Also, the business users cannot themselves 
alter the software code, should they want to, due to the software source code being closed 
or because of Intellectual Property Rights held by the provider.  
At the foundation of the Cyber Resilience Act, should lie the assumption that producers of 
digital technology products should have a strong incentive to deliver safe and secure products, 
services and processes and that consumers and business users should be protected from, or 
compensated for, costs and other damages caused by non-secured products. Just as a business 
that pollutes the environment should pay for the cleaning, so software providers should pay 
for the costs incurred by the users for insufficiently secure digital technology they put on the 
market. This is a missing element in the current situation and in the Commission’s proposal.

IV. Mitigate the risk of disclosure of the vulnerabilities 
 
We fear that the requirements described for incident notification might be unpracticable. As 
business users would be the collateral victims of a security incident, it is critical not to disclose 
vulnerabilities before that the mitigations measures are in place. This warning is too weak in 
the current proposal and should be made more explicit.  
The reporting obligations must come only once the notifying entity has effectively mitigated 
the incident. It would be detrimental to the product’s end -user if a vulnerability would be 
disclosed before the mitigation plan would be implemented, as it would jeopardize the 
security of its users.  
Penalties upon providers that do not comply with the obligations  to mitigate the 
vulnerabilities identified, are necessary to ensure the right incentive to act. 
The CRA lays out an obligation for manufacturers to notify about actively exploited 
vulnerabilities under 24 -hour (Article 11 paragraph 1).  This is an appropr iate timeline that 
should be kept. Yet, it is necessary to clarify when the “24 hours” period starts. The mention 
of “after becoming aware” is far too ambiguous, thereby lacking legal clarity and certainty. 
We also question the limited resources of ENISA  to cope with the reporting obligations and 
the ability to secure the database where all the vulnerabilities will be compiled.   
We recommend aligning the provisions on the public disclosure of vulnerabilities on the 
requirements developed in international  standards for vulnerability management and 
disclosure (ISO 29147 and 30111). 
 
V. Strengthen the provisions regarding software and open-source elements 
 
  
5 
 
The exclusion of Software -as-a-Service (SaaS) from the scope of the CRA should be better 
linked with the application of the NIS2 directive. Moreover, the wording in recital 9 (and article 
3(2) on remote data processing) does not provide a clear vision on the scope of this exclusion. 
The language used in the CRA is ambiguous as to whether SaaS and Platform -as-a-Service 
(PaaS) products are excluded from the scope. 
Specifically, we understand that where a product with digital elements within the scope of 
CRA relies on a cloud-based service, this service will be in scope of the CRA. As cloud services 
(including SaaS) are considered as essential entities under the NIS2 Directive, they will need 
to comply with all its cybersecurity and risk-management requirements listed in Article 18 and 
20 of the NIS2 Directive.  
We call on the legislator to clarify that all kinds of software products (including SaaS) are within 
the scope of the CRA, regardless of the way they are distributed.  
The CRA should cover digital technologies in products, services and processes, during the 
whole economic life cycle from a user perspective. 
The CRA requires manufacturers to ensure that vulnerabilities are handled effectively for the 
expected product lifetime or for 5 years, whichever is shorter (Article 10 paragraph 6). This 
provision overlooked products with a long-term lifecycle.  
Moreover, we believe that the current drafting of the text could lead to planned obsolescence 
by manufacturers, a practice that could be costly for European companies, forcing them to 
change their software prematurely for instance. Additionally, this would hav e a negative 
impact from an environmental/sustainability perspective.   
This context makes it very urgent  that the software provided is secure, right from the start, 
and that security is maintained throughout the deployment and operational use of the 
software by business users. It is important to force software manufacturers to develop, repair, 
maintain and t est a particular software version for 5 years after it is placed on the market.

Producers and vendors should be obliged to keep providing necessary updates and patches 
throughout the economic life cycle, to keep ensuring the security of the software, and b y 
extension all connected systems and users. The life cycle must not only consider the 
producer’s perspective. It is critical that the provisions stating that software developer will 
have to undergo multiple conformity assessments during a product’s lifeti me are upheld and 
enforced.  
We believe that if a manufacturer has decided to stop updating a software and does not want 
to commercialise it anymore, it should ensure that other actors will be able to provide security 
patches in the future. This will allow business users that might be dependent on this software 
to operate to continue working in a safe environment.  
We also notice that the regulation does not cover the progressive update of the software 
currently placed in the market. A provision could be added to organise a progressive alignment 
with the CRA requirements for them as well to ensure an increased level of security.  To 
improve the legal certainty of the text, we believe that the notion of “substantial modification” 
defined in Article 3 (31) should be better defined to avoid any bypass strategy. Also, we would 
urge for the required 5 years period of security updates etc. to be reset after such substantial 
modification or progressive updates, giving users at least five years to use the software 
securely. 
The CRA current drafting lacks clarity regarding the situation for open -source elements, 
especially when products are using both digital elements and open -source components. 
Therefore, we recommend that the CRA clarifies the role and limitations of  third-party 
component manufacturers in the supply chain. 
Additionally, we ask for inclusion of provisions in the CRA to prevent software providers from 
evading their responsibility for not sufficiently secure software products they have already put 
on the  market, by publishing its source code and putting the product in the open source 
domain.

VI. Include cloud orchestration in the list of critical products  
 
In Class II Annex III the European Commission lists the types of critical products that will 
require third party conformity assessment, thus ensuring that such products will fully comply 
with the provisions of the CRA.  We believe that Cloud Orchestration  should be included in 
this list. 
Cloud Orchestration is the process of automating the tasks needed  to manage connections 
and operations of workloads on private and public clouds. As such it is critical for the digital 
transition of European compagnies.  
 
VII. Corrective measures (art. 10.12) 
Article 10, paragraph  12 states that a manufacturer who knows or has reason to believe that 
its product is not in conformity with requirements “shall immediately take the corrective 
measures necessary to bring that product with digital elements […] into conformity, to 
withdraw or to recall the product, as appropriate.” Business users stress that, should such a 
case arise, the manufacturer is not required or even allowed to directly remove the insecure 
product from a (business) user’s system. This should be made clear in the CRA.  
In such a situation, a notification to the business user of the non-conformity and its effects 
on the business user should be made by the manufacturer, allowing the business user to 
take appropriate measures to ensure its business continuity and security.  
The CRA should clarify that all costs associated with this non-conformity, including i.a. the 
patching of software, interruption of business processes and decommissioning, must be 
borne by the manufacturer.

We look forward to continuing to work closely with the European Commission, Parliament, and 
Council to ensure digital resilience for European economy. 
We are the Belgian, Dutch, French and German CIO -associations; the communities of Chief Information Officers 
(CIO’s) and other senior leaders that are responsible for digital technologies and digital transformations within 
private or public organisations. All our members are business users of digital technologies; they acquire, 
implement, and use digital technologies in practise. We do not represent ICT providers and consultants.  
Business users of digital technology are a key link in the digital transformation of society and economy: they rely 
on the digital technologies they use, either hardware or software, and need these to be secure and safe to use 
and compliant with EU laws and regulat ions. Business users also play an influential role in keeping digital 
technologies used in their organizations and across Europe secure and compliant with European standards and 
values through their combined purchasing power. To reap the benefits of digita l technologies in Europe, the 
position of business users - companies and government organisations - of digital technology must be taken into 
account.

Siemens Healthineers’ feedback to the European Commission’s proposal for  
a Regulation on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements and 
amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 (Cyber Resilience Act) 
 
Siemens Healthineers welcomes the  European Commission ’s initiative to introduce horizontal 
cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements . We believe that cybersecurity by 
design is crucial for the security of EU citizens, in particular for patients in Europe, as well as for safe 
and responsible innovation in healthcare.  
 
Cybersecurity readiness is an integral part of the Siemens Healthineers company culture: we 
implement a security framework for development and design, we support security-aware 
deployment, and we apply post-market surveillance continuously. We strive to constantly improve 
our systems and processes , and train our teams in cybersecurity matters, so that high cyberthreat 
awareness remains high priority.  
 
This position paper  aims to bring specific concerns and proposals  to the attention of  legislators to 
support the creation of a well-suited framework for better secured hardware and software products 
in the  European Union. We look forward to participating in the ongoing discussions on the Cyber 
Resilience Ac t (CRA) by sharing our expertise , in partic ular regarding products from the Siemens 
Healthineers portfolio that are in the scope of the CRA proposal . We stand ready to work with 
policymakers to ensure an effective and successful strengthening of cyber resilience in the European 
Union. 
 
Against this background, we would like to highlight the following key suggestions before providing 
a more detailed analysis: 
 
BUILD ON ESTABLISHED SECTORIAL FRAMEWORKS 
The European Commission's proposal takes into account the different phases of the lifecycle of a 
digital product. Thereby, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) will contribute to a significant increase in cyber 
resilience in the European Union . The approach of the CRA towards  products with digital elements 
that are within the scope of Regulation EU 2017/745 and 2017/746 (Medical Devices Regulation and 
In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation, abbreviated as MDR and IVDR) is reasonable: both 
MDR and IVDR are robust, comprehensive, and up -to-date regulations for the medical technologies 
industry that ensure legal certainty to all market players and society at large. Building on the success 
of the MDR/IVDR, the legislators should  enable the reus e of certain healthcare sector specific 
processes for applications and products that are covered by the CRA and are intended to be used in 
the environment of a healthcare provider and its value chain. 
 
RECOGNIZE SHARED RESPONSIBILITY 
We believe that a high cybersecurity resilience can only be achieved by shared responsibilities  of all 
stakeholders that are impacted by a product with digital elements during its lifecycle. F or the 
healthcare sector these stakeholders can be manufacturers, suppliers, healthcare providers, patients, 
integrators, operators, and regulators . We support the explanation by the Medical Device

actors for ensuring a secured environment for the benefit of patients’ safety , for example when

ALLOW FOR PRODUCT-SPECIFIC DIFFERENTIATION 
The CRA proposal has a wide scope across sectors, heterogenous products and for both business-to-
consumer (“B2C”) and business-to-business (“B2B”) transactions. We believe that the proposal as it 
stands does not yet provide proportionate regulation for all cases. The CRA should follow a risk-based 
approach, including appropriate and commercially reasonable measures  depending on the specific 
product with digital elements and its intended use. The manufacturer’s post-market obligations need 
to be clearly differentiated from a greatly ext ended warranty period, and maintenance services 
(including patches, updates, upgrades) may be fee -based – at least in connection with business -to-
business transactions. 
 
EXTEND THE TRANSITION PERIOD 
Since the scope of the proposed law is very broad and encompasses a wide range of also physical 
products, we believe that the proposed transition period of 24 months  is too short  and should be 
prolonged. We also suggest that the European Commission starts the standardization request for the 
CRA as soon as possible, to ensure that standards for the implementation of the CRA are ready before 
the CRA becomes effective, so to avoid uncertainty during product development. The implementation 
of measures shall be based on  technical consensus established by standards . Further more, the 
transition period should be extended based on the criticality of the product, which in turn should be 
based on the device-specific intended environment of use. 
 
AVOID DUPLICATE REGULATION 
Due to the upcoming NIS 2 Directive many companies in the EU will be considered important entities 
or even essential entities and as such must establish further cybersecurity measures or  become 
indirectly affected if being a supplier to those entities.  It will be important to ensure consistency 
between the requirements of NIS2 and CRA to avoid legal uncertainty and additional red tape.  
 
MAKE REPORTING OBLIGATIONS PRACTICABLE 
The period for reporting should consider that cyberattacks are often complex and require the 
resource-intensive investigation of experts. Therefore we suggest that the period should be at least 
72 hours , corresponding to the reporting period for a data breach under the GDPR. With shorter 
reporting periods, a flood of unnecessary, over -cautious incident reports is likely to happen  – 
therefore it is important to start counting towards the reporting deadline only when the manufacturer 
has become aware of  a significant incident based on thresholds  which need to be specified . The 
general public should only be informed if such a notification is necessary to handle  the incident or 
prevent further incidents.  
 
MAKE SANCTIONS PROPORTIONATE 
Administrative fines based on the worldwide turnover subject to Article 53, must be proportionate to 
the concrete event and the level of n egligence/misconduct, and in any case should not apply  in case

In the following chapters, we would like to take the opportunity to comment on the Cyber Resilience 
Act proposal in more detail: 
 
I. Scope and definitions 
We consider the approach of the CRA’s towards the products with digital elements within the scope 
of Regulation EU 2017/745 and 2017/746 (Medical Devices Regulation and In Vitro Diagnostic Medical 
Devices Regulation, abbreviated as MDR and IVDR respectively) as justified and considerate. MDR and 
IVDR are robust, comprehensive and up-to-date regulations that ensure legal certainty to all market 
players and society at large , further specified by additional guidance on cybersecurity ( MDCG 2019-
16 rev. 1 ). We believe that the MDR/IVDR c ould be further ext ended by the EU Commission via 
additional standards or common specifications , as already intended in Article 8 and Article 9. We 
hereby suggest that the co-legislators enable the reuse of certain healthcare sector specific processes 
for applications and products that are covered by the CRA and are intended to be used in the 
environment of a healthcare provider and its value chain.  This could be achieved durin g the 
standardization process. 
MDR/IVDR address the entire cycle of the medical product from development to placement on the 
market, and continuous monitoring throughout its life cycle . As a result of the latest revision of the 
Medical Devices Regulations,  as of 2017 almost all medical software is subject to scrutiny by the 
Notified Bodies designated under MDR and IVDR. It is the only  NLF sectorial legislation to date that 
also requires CE marking of a product which is merely a stand-alone software – to be affixed upon a 
successful third-part conformity assessment. The regulatory framework for medical devices follows a 
risk-based approach by placing each medical device – either a  hardware device with or without 
embedded software, or a stand-alone software device – into one of four categories: the higher a risk 
class the stricter the requirements. Three of such classes – IIa, IIb, and III of medical devices and B, C, 
and D of in vitro devices – are subject to external oversight by Notified Bodies, which account for an 
estimated 90  - 95 % of all medical devices. Beyond the legal framework, Siemens Healthineers 
acknowledges its part of  the responsibility towards end -users, especially patients, including placing 
safe and highly performing products on the European market.  
 
Furthermore, we propose that the processes for medical devices, including quality management, risk 
management, change management, product lifecycle management, market surveillance 
management, as well as harmonized standards complying with the regulatory framework for medical 
devices should be presumed to comply with the requirements of the CRA with regard to products with 
digital elements that do not fulfil the definition of a medical device but are used in the healthcare 
sector (e.g., Radiation Dose Management, Billing Software, Device Training Software, Clinical Research 
Applications, Medical Image Viewers). In addition, in the healthcare sector there are internationally 
accepted standards, such as the HIMSS/NEMA MDS² (Manufacturer Disclosure Statement for Medical

Device Security )1, that have been established as best practice for information sharing between  
manufacturers and hospitals , which should be presumed to comply with information sharing 
requirements under the CRA.  
 
Software as a Service (SaaS) should be generally excluded from the CRA’s scope. In the definition of 
“products with digital element” remote data processing solutions should be excluded and regulated 
only under NIS 2, in order to achieve legal clarity and certainty. The interplay with NIS 2, for example 
regarding reporting obligations, needs to be clarified.  
 
II. Technical Requirements / Lifecycle 
From an overarching perspective, it is important to acknowledge that cybersecurity is a result of 
responsible design, manufacturing and use of a product. We want to underline that cybersecurity is 
not a static product feature. The intended use of digitally connectable products, may lead to misuse – 
therefore we consider cybersecurity as a capability of the processes employed pre- and post-market. 
To maintain high levels of cybersecu rity, standards and labelling should rather focus on  secure 
processes than on (volatile) technological features. Siemens Healthineers suggests that the CRA 
focusses on implementing processes and existing certification schemes – and avoids double 
certification towards schemes that do not reflect application needs (like e.g. Common Criteria). 
 
Siemens Healthineers in principle welcomes that products with digital elements shall be made only 
available on the market if they comply with a list of essential requirements, and if the manufacturer 
has put in place vulnerability handling procedures according to Annex I.  However, Siemens 
Healthineers proposes several adaptations: 
• As it is impossible for manufactures to ensure that complex products are co mpletely 
invulnerable to cyberattacks, the obligations under the CRA should not exceed commercially 
reasonable effort s, taking into account the state of the art, the costs and 
advantages/disadvantages of implementation and the intended use of the product with digital 
elements as well as the related risk of varying likelihood and severity (cf. Article 32 GDPR). As 
the CRA is intended to cover a wide range of heterogeneous products (e.g., kitchenware as 
well as Industrial Automation & Control Systems), it should be recognized that the extent of 
what is commercially reasonable will not always be the same for all products covered by the 
CRA. For example, the protection of (any) data on the digital product in question should be 
subject to a risk-based approach after the corresponding data has been classified instead of 
requesting encryption by default for any data as requested in Annex I (1 (3)(c)). Furthermore, 
there might be safety or performance reasons conflicting with encryption depending on the 
specific case. 
• It should be recognized that the design of serial products cannot always be changed 
immediately by a manufacturer without entailing excessive costs and effort, including 
development, testing and validation effort and the time for the approval process. Therefore, 
new knowledge, techniques or standards that were not available when a product was 
designed, may be considered by the manufacturer for the design of subsequent product

• We believe that a high cybersecurity resilience can only be achieved by shared responsibilities. 
For the healthcare sector, we support the following explanation by the Medical Device 
Coordination Group: “While the MDR and the IVDR provide legal obligations only with regard 
to manufacturers, however it should be noted that for the provision of secured healthcare 
services, it is important to recognise the roles and expectations of all stakehol ders, such as 
manufacturers, suppliers, healthcare providers, patients, integrators, operators and 
regulators. All of these actors share responsibilities for ensuring a secured environment for the 
benefit of patients’ safety.” (MDCG 2019-16 rev. 1, p. 12). For example, a secure configuration 
(Annex I(3)(a)) can be achieved in complex scenarios only in a close collaboration with the 
operator and/or user  and therefore should not be an essential requirement of a product. 
Sometimes default configuration as desi gned by the manufacturer cannot be applied by the 
operator due to the need to connect certain legacy devices. In such cases the operator might 
choose alternative measures to protect their device. Furthermore, the required possibility to 
reset the product to its original state leads to uncertainty from the view of the operator since 
the original state is undefined as soon as the device i s in operation mode and might change 
as soon as user data is generated or patches, updates or upgrades have been performed in-
between. 
• It is not clear what would be required under “effective and regular tests and reviews” (Annex 
I, Section 2 (3)) in addition to the vulnerability monitoring set forth in Annex 1, Section 2. We 
propose that the described vulnerability handling process should be sufficient to regularly 
monitor the security of the product with digital elements.

prices for maintenance services. I.e., at least  for business-to-business transactions, fee -based 
maintenance contracts with customers are customary because providing maintenance services incurs

IV. Reporting and communication 
Excessive reporting obligations should be  avoided and only relevant incidents should be reported, 
which may be subject to certain thresholds.  Again, we strongly advocate for a risk -based approach 
that takes into accoun t proportionality, considering the potential impact of vulnerabilities and 
incidents to users and patients. 
 
Since a notification s hall happen “without delay ” – to be more accurate, the wording should be 
“without undue delay” – it shall be possible to provide more  details (corrective or mitigation 
measures) of the vulnerability subsequently. 
 
The period for reporting should consider that cyberattacks are often complex and require the 
investigation of experts. The period should be at least 72 hours corresponding to the reporting period 
for a data breach under the GDP R. With shorter reporting periods, a flood of unnecessary, over -
cautious incident reports is likely to happen – therefore it is important to start the reporting deadline 
only when the manufacturer has become aware of a significant incident has happened. The general 
public should only be informed if such a notification is necessary to handle the incident or prevent 
further incidents. Unrestricted public disclosure may increase security risks as also potential attackers 
will be informed , users may be inadequately alarmed and reputation of manufacturers may be 
inadequately harmed. 
 
The obligation to provide “clear, understandable, intelligible and legible” information (Article 10 (10)) 
should consider the relevant address ee (e.g., IT administrator in business -to-business transactions). 
Sharing SBOM (Software Bill of Material s) and CSAF (Common Security Advisory Framework) 
documents makes only sense in the context of industry and professional users. For the definition of 
the SBOM and CSAF formats, international alignment (e.g., NTIA and CISA) could help to reduce double 
effort for manufacturers operating at global scale. 
 
Since cybersecurity is a global challenge and to avoid isolated EU solutions, we would appreciate if the 
EU Commission can align the reporting timeline within the European market and with the 
international partners. This could reduce the regulatory burden for all parties. Furthermore, it would 
be beneficial if only one authority (e.g., the sectoral authority) needs to be informed and all other 
parties are to be indirectly informed by the authorities. 
 
As already mentioned, we see cybersecurity as a shared responsibility. Therefore , we would like to 
suggest to the co-legislators to apply the same rules to the EU authority and all member states to vice-
versa notify the manufacture r of any exploitable vulnerability or weakness unknown to the 
manufacturer. For critical vulnerabilities we would like to propose a coordinated  vulnerability 
disclosure procedure agreed with the manufacturer.

V. Liability 
It should be recognized that the concept of “strict liability” irrespective of fault under general product 
liability law as set forth in Recital 16 shall not be applicable within the scope of the CRA. As no 
manufacturer can ensure invulnerability to cybera ttacks, liability of the manufacturer for damages 
caused by such an attack shall only apply depending on fault. 
 
Where third party components are sourced, it should be clarified that a manufacturer is neither 
responsible nor liable for the fault of the respective third party, provided that the manufacturer 
exercised due care when integrating components sourced in acc ordance with the first sentence of 
Article 10 (4). 
 
VI. Sanctions and Powers of Authorities 
Administrative fines based on the worldwide turnover subject to Article 53 must be proportionate to 
the concrete event and the level of negligence/misconduct and in any  case should not apply in case 
of only “non -essential” in -compliance. The calculation of penalties should be based only on the 
amount of the turnover that corresponds to the relevant product that is incompliant.  The margin in 
connection with the turnover should be considered for the calculation of penalties. 
 
The jurisdiction of the authorities of the Member States in cross -border cases appears unclear; 
multiple punishment must be excluded. Multiple punishment shall also be excluded with regard to 
fines under other regimes due to the same event (e.g. GDPR). The surveillance authorities should not 
be authorized to intervene without cause (e.g., Article 17). 
 
Any admin istrative fines shall not be enforced as long as no harmoni zed standards have been 
published in the EU Journal (OJEU) or common specification have been implemented.  
 
In several  new legislations, EU legislators are significantly increasing sanctions. We would like to 
encourage the European Commission to consider other ways or to strengthen investment in more 
cybersecurity through incentives and funding. 
 
VII. Standardization 
Currently industrial standards of the series such as IEC 62443 are not harmonised under EU  NLF. 
Furthermore, the international standards such as ISO 27001 and ISO 22301 which are used for the 
Management Systems are not harmonized by EU bodies but can serve as a valuable baseline to handle 
processes in the context of cybersecurity and resilience mana gement. Vulnerability and Disclosure 
Policies (ISO 29147) and Vulnerability Handling standards like ISO 30111 could serve as a good baseline 
too, similarly to ETSI EN 303 645 for consumer devices.

requirements with the EHDS and therefore co-legislators need to clarify the definition of EHR systems 
under the EHDS3 as well as the interaction between CRA and EHDS. 
 
VIII. Transition Period 
The transition period should be adapted to enable harmonized standards or common specification 
beforehand, to apply once  the notified bodies are designated and accredited to assess conformity. 
Implementation of measures can only be achieved  based on technical consensus established by 
standards. The request for standardization shall be initiated as soon as possible and the delegated acts 
should be published as soon as possible to make  the classification scheme  under the CRA more 
transparent to all stakeholders.  Additionally, the transition period should be adapted to empower 
notified bodies to ensure the availability of sufficient cybersecurity experts/expertise to prevent time 
to market blocks of innovation.

into force  should not fall under the scope of the CRA, even if the manufacturer  provides

IX. Spare Parts and Upgrades 
Only a modification of the intended use should lead to a substantial modification. Therefore recital 24 
needs to be clarified: It should not be a substantial modification, for example, if functionalities are 
changed or extended without changing the intende d use, or if compliance with cybersecurity 
requirements is ensured, not only by providing patches or updates, but also by providing upgrades for 
products that have been placed on the market. 
 
In addition, it should be clarified that providing spare parts for products that have been placed on the 
market will not be regarded as separate placing on the market of spare parts.

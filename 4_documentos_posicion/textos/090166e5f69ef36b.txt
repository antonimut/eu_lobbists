2 FEEDBACK 
InfoCert appreciates the opportunity provided by the European Commission to provide feedback 
on the Commission's new European Cyber Resilience Act and strongly supports the goal of 
strengthening the cybersecurity of digital products, recognizing the increased threat to security 
and privacy caused by the growing number of such products.  
In order to support the European Union's goal of simplifying cybersecurity requirements and 
extending their coverage to all types of digital products and ancillary services, InfoCert supports 
Policy Option No. 4, which is a blended approach involving both horizontal regulatory intervention 
and a phased approach. Such a strategy would allow for the creation of a regulatory framework 
capable of regulating the subject in a phased manner and without disrupting the current market. 
   
InfoCert recommends taking into account the principle of proportionality between 
implementations required of private companies and costs: while it is true that every market actor 
is a key player in systemic security, small and medium-sized companies need to be protected from 
excessive compliances that would risk not allowing them to be competitive.  
 
Moreover, such regulations, should be proportionate to the actual risk that the hardware or 
software poses to the user, in order to avoid both overly restrictive measures that could limit the 
technological growth of products and overly concessive solutions that would not allow for an 
organic evolution of the topic.  
 
The Proposal does not clarify at the legal level the liability of companies throughout the product 
life cycle, especially in terms of compensation, as such, a vertical intervention on the topic that 
would describe in detail the responsibilities and the entities that must respond would be 
propitious. 
 
Taking this into consideration, it would be appropriate to maintain consistency in the regulatory 
framework by taking into consideration not only the GDPR1 and the eIDAS Regulation2, but also 
the proposed Regulation on general product safety3 and the proposed Regulation establishing 
harmonized rules on artificial intelligence4. Setting from the start a coherence between the 
different requirements of the subjects and objects involved is indispensable for the creation of a 
regulatory system that is free of contradictions, concretely applicable and comprehensive, as well 
as a trust framework for the digital world based on the solid foundation provided by the eIDAS 
Regulation, which is increasingly found to be the pillar of the governance framework of the digital 
economy.

With regard to Artificial Intelligence and Iot (Internet of Things), it is also crucial to set up an 
ecosystem where the roles and responsibilities of all those involved in the production chain are 
manifest: in this way, the latest technological advances can be exploited without endangering the 
rights of EU users and citizens. 
 
Finally, it is necessary to take into account how cybersecurity, in addition to the merely 
technological aspects, is functional to the operational design choices (and training, in the case of 
Artificial Intelligence) of the actors involved in the production process. In order to keep track of 
the actions of relevant actors in the supply chain and to attribute them with certainty to the 
responsible parties, the use of trusted tools such as digital identity and, in the future, EU-DIW and 
other trusted services such as QSeal, QWAC and QTimestamp is appropriate.

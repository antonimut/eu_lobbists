  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
SIEMENS POSITION 
Proposed EU Cyber 
Resilience Act (CRA) 
 
Siemens comments on EU-Commission proposals for a European Cyber Resilience 
Act: 
In accordance with the European Commission’s work program for 2022, on 15 
September 2022 the European Commission published a “Proposal for a regulation 
on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements and 
amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020” (COM(2022) 454).

Key Aspects1 
 
#1 In general, Siemens welcomes a horizontal EU -wide cybersecurity regulation that ensures the 
functioning of free movement of goods in the EU internal market for covered products.  We ask to 
consider the specifics of products intended to be used by professionals solely in industrial and critical 
infrastructure domains  (e.g., rail way technology2) such as extended development, i nstallation, and 
approval periods. 
#2 The CRA should create a simple, coherent, and effective legal framework that horizontally regulates the 
cybersecurity of all covered products when placed on the market. It should be designed in such a way 
that existing, product -specific regulations are not taking prece dence according to the “lex specialis” 
principle. This includes existing and upcoming legislation (e.g., RED Delegated Regulation, Machinery 
Regulation, Artificial Intelligence Act). Simplification of the existing legal framework is crucial to allow a 
correct implementation by all stakeholders to strengthen cyber resilience.  
Therefore, the CRA should be construed as a “lex specialis” (a total harmonisation legislation) and should 
thus replace the existing and upcoming cybersecurity-related harmonisation legislation (“CE directives”). 
Article 2(4) of the draft CRA should be modified so that conformity with the CRA results in conformity 
with other existing and upcoming harmonisation legislation regulating cybersecurity. 
#3 The CRA should be aligned with the basic principles of the NLF as closely as possible. Any deviation should 
be justified based on the specific cybersecurity aspects, or otherwise removed (e.g. Article 20 (5) “EU 
declaration of conformity”). 
#4 The CRA should equally address harmonised standards, co mmon specifications, and certification 
schemes with respect to the presumption of conformity. CSA schemes and common specifications need 
to be subject to the same test procedure and assessment with regard to the coverage of essential 
requirements of the CR A as harmonised standards (verification by the Harmonised Standards  
Consultant). Harmonised standards should be available and listed at minimum 12 months before the 
application of the CRA. 
#5 Siemens supports the concept of high-level essential requirements in the CRA (as set out in Annex I) and 
their technical concretization in harmonised standards which are based on and aligned as close as 
possible with international standards. 
#6 Siemens welcomes the inclusion of requirements addressing development and design processes as well 
as the vulnerability handling process to support the cybersecurity of products. 
#7 The period between the entry into force of the CRA and its applicability (Article 57 of the draft CRA) must 
be sufficiently long to allow its implementation. Siemens calls for a transitional period of 36 months. 
#8 The CRA should also address the aspects of spare parts and define a realistic transitional period that 
allows the repair of products. 
The following sections further detail the key aspects and provide additional information.

1. An efficient legal framework without regulatory overlaps 
(key aspects #1 and #2) 
 
The CRA should create a simple, coherent, and effective legal 
framework that horizontally regulates the cybersecurity of all 
covered products when plac ed on the market. Therefore, it 
should be designed as “lex specialis” (a “total harmonisation 
legislation”). Thus, it should replace the existing and 
upcoming cybersecurity -related provisions in the 
harmonisation legislation (“CE directives”). 
Siemens recommends to 
• modify Article 2(4) of the draft CRA to ensure that the 
conformity with the CRA results in conformity with other 
harmonisation legislation regulating cybersecurity in a 
horizontal or vertical manner; and 
• repeal Article 7 (General Product Safety), Article 8 (High-risk 
AI systems), Article 9 (Machinery products)  and the RED 
Delegated Regulation. 
Article 3 of the draft CRA (Definitions) should be complete, 
concise, and in addition define and assign unique classes for 
all categories of products with digital elements: 
• (non-critical) product with digital elements (Class 0); 
• critical product with digital elements (Class 1, Class 2); 
• highly critical product with digital elements (Class 3). 
Siemens recommends avoiding multiple misleading terms 
(e.g. “whole life cycle” in Article 1(c), “expected product 
lifetime” in Article 10(6), Article 10(12), Article 23(2)) and 
instead using and defining the term “cybersecurity support 
lifetime”. This period of product-specific cybersecurity support 
is determined by the manufacturer and communicated to the 
user.

2. Product-specific regulations using the New Legislative Framework (NLF) 
(key aspects #3, #4, and #5) 
 
Siemens recommends  
• that any changes of the NLF principles in the CRA that are 
not cybersecurity-specific shall be avoided or otherwise – if 
deemed necessary - addressed in a recast of the NLF; 
• to avoid double conformity assessment for the same 
protection objective (see Article 18(4)). Conformity 
assessment and the results thereof carried out under other 
legal acts (in particular schemes under the EU Cybersecurity 
Act) shall provide presumption of conformity insofar as the 
technical specifications were listed under the CRA; 
• not to mix up conformity assessment procedures between 
different legal acts (see Article 8, AI Act); 
• that products shall be presumed to be in conformity with the 
essential requirements based on verified and listed 
specifications only (harmonised standards, common 
specifications, or CSA schemes). Therefore, the related CSA 
schemes and common specifications need to be subject to 
the same test procedure and assessment with regard to the 
coverage of essential requirements of the CRA as 
harmonised standards (verification by the Harmonised 
Standards Consultant); 
• the precedence of harmonised standards over common 
specifications. Common specifications should only be 
created in exceptional cases, based on defined rules; 
• that the CE marking is the sole indication for the conformity 
of the product with all relevant harmonisation acts including 
the CRA; 
• that a product delivered in conformity with the essential 
requirements at the time of placing on the market may not 
be regarded as non -compliant with these requirements if 
new vulnerabilities have been disclosed  after placing the 
product on the market. Manufacturers shall only be obliged 
to act in accordance with the vulnerability requirements 
defined in Section 2 of Annex I for a period defined in Article 
10(6) and should be limited to the supply of adequate 
software updates. Additional activities, e.g., necessary 
hardware modifications, reside in the responsibility of the 
product user/operator;

• to clearly define what is meant by expected product lifetime 
and who determines it. From Siemens’ point of view, the 
manufacturer is the one who determines best this period 
and communicates it to the potential buyer/user (e.g., in the 
user documentation, sales brochures). Siemens welcomes 
the limitation of the support period to a maximum of 
5 years; 
• that the obligations of economic operators are realistic and 
manageable. For example, the detailed examination of the 
conformity of components by manufacturers (Article 10(4)) 
and the verification of the manufacturer’s vulnerability 
handling process (Section 2 of Annex I) by th e importer 
(Article 13(1)) are deemed as not manageable; 
• that the CRA itself shall be construed in a way that it ensures 
that the scope and basic definitions are understandable, 
precise, and complete to ensure a common understanding 
and correct application . Siemens asks to amend (e.g., 
device, network, free software, open source software) and 
revise (e.g., coverage of “components” in Article 3(1) and 
3(8)) the definitions accordingly; 
• that the manufacturer has to define and document the 
intended use of the product it places on the market. The use 
of a product in an environment/application not foreseen by 
the manufacturer shall not be considered as a “reasonably 
foreseeable misuse”. Otherwise any product classification 
becomes obsolete and the manufacturer has to construe the 
product always according to the highest cybersecurity risk 
level; 
• to amend Article 6(1) to ensure that only those products are 
covered as Class 2 that are intended by the manufacturer to 
be used in critical applications within such environments.

3. Processes, instructions, documentation, and obligations of users 
(key aspect #6) 
 
Siemens recommends 
• to define requirements detailing the development and 
design processes (e.g., using IEC EN 62443-4-1), as they are 
key for the fulfilment of the essential requirements defined 
in Annex I. The current conformity modules/procedures do 
not sufficiently address this aspect and need to be amended 
accordingly; 
• that the user is obliged to update the product according to 
the information about fixed vulnerabilities publicly disclosed 
by the manufacturer (e.g., installation of security updates) 
during first commissioning/installation of the product and 
throughout its use. The involvement of and measures taken 
by the user are crucial to achieve the protection objective; 
• that products with digital elements shall be delivered 
without any known exploitable vulnerability based on the 
risk (Section 1 of Annex I, para 2). An exploitable 
vulnerability does not necessarily lead to a risk to the 
product; 
• that the format and elements of the Software Bill of 
Materials (SBOM) resides in the responsibility of the 
manufacturer. Therefore, Article 10(15) should be removed. 
As part of the technical documentation the SBOM can be 
based on e.g., European or international standards;    
• to remove the requirement that the manufacturer must 
provide security patches free of charge (Section 2 of Annex 
I, para 8). This approach might be common practice in some 
sectors (e.g., consumers) but handled differently in other 
sectors (e.g., industrial). Security patches are often not 
separated from bug -fixing or even functional/performance 
enhancements. Patching software is a service of the 
manufacturer to maintain the operability for the end user, 
including to minimize the vulnerability to cyber threats – in 
particular to ensure trouble -free operation within industry 
and critical infrastructure applications in the long term. It 
should therefore remain the responsibility and decision of 
the manufacturer which service it offers free of charge; 
• to provide the user information specified in Annex II solely 
based on the intended use/purpose foreseen by the 
manufacturer (Annex II, para 5).

4. Reporting obligations and transitional requirements 
(key aspects #7 and #8) 
 
Siemens recommends to 
• align the reporting requirements of the CRA with those 
foreseen in NIS 2; 
• report only significant exploited vulnerabilities to ENISA. 
Siemens is opposed to mandatory reporting of unpatched 
and not yet exploited vulnerabilities, as this opens up new 
attack scenarios if this information is disclosed to the public; 
• limit the reporting obligation of the manufacturer to a time 
period as defined by the manufacturer or 5 years, whichever 
is shorter. Otherwise, the manufacturer has to comply with 
this requirement infinitely; 
• remove reporting obligations for products placed on the 
market before the applicability of the CRA; 
• have a period between the entry into force of the CRA and 
its applicability of 36 months to allow all stakeholders its 
implementation. Reasons are the timely availability of 
harmonised standards as well as limited resources to adapt 
new or enhance existing products. 
• address the aspects of spare parts and define a realistic 
transitional period that allows the repair of prod ucts, 
including products placed on the market before the 
applicability of the CRA. Products for professional use or 
used in industrial environments or critical infrastructures 
have typically an expected lifetime of decades.

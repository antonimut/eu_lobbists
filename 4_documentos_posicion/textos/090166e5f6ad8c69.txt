Warsaw, 23 January 2023 
 
INTERACTIVE ADVERTISING BUREAU POLAND’S (IAB POLSKA) COMMENTS ON THE  
 
Proposal for a 
REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL 
establishing a common framework for media services in the internal market (European 
Media Freedom Act) and amending Directive 2010/13/EU  
  
IAB Poland is an organization with more than 200 members, including both the largest Internet 
portals, broadcasters and providers of audiovisual media services and press publishers. For 
IAB Poland, the issue of freedom of speech, the right to information as well as the related media 
pluralism, which are the foundations of a democratic society, is extremely important, an d 
therefore we consistently support activities aimed at protecting these freedoms and the 
independence of the media. At the same time, we believe that, in its current form, the European 
Media Freedom Act (hereinafter: "the Act") will not contribute to ensu ring the realization of the 
goals for which it was created; on the contrary, it may lead to a whole range of negative 
consequences, including a greater restriction of media independence, a reduction in funding for 
independent media or a decrease in their p luralism. IAB Poland believes that the road to 
improving the situation of pluralistic media in Poland does not lead through the enactment of yet 
another piece of legislation, as there are already a sufficient number of provisions in Polish law 
that guard the freedom and independence of the media. The fundamental problem lies only in 
the lack of compliance with them. At the same time, IAB Poland is surprised that the idea for 
the Act has not been properly consulted and clarified with the media community, at least with 
regard to the news media. Meanwhile, it is, after all, for their supposed benefit. 
Doubts about the Act fundamentally stem from the fact that it addresses numerous aspects of 
media operation by treating audiovisual media, as it were, equally wit h other types of media. 
The best evidence of this is the plan to establish a new pan -European media regulator. Such 
optics are fundamentally wrong, as they do not in any way take into account the dissimilarities 
between different types of media. How can th e linear nature of television broadcasts of daily 
news be viewed uniformly with the interactive formula of Internet news portals, or the printed 
press, which is characterized by perhaps even more dissimilarities?   
Another objection to the commented regulation is the enormous generality of most of the Act's 
provisions, which, leaving a very broad interpretative framework, can significantly affect the 
operation of the media. This freedom of interpretation is, moreover, at odds with the nature of 
the Act, which is planned as a regulation, i.e. a legal act directly applicable in the member states  
The European Commission's authority to regulate the subject of "European media" must also 
be questionable. It must be questioned whether the concept of "European media" even exists? 
More reasonable is the thesis that we are dealing with de facto separate media systems based 
on the differing traditions of media regulation in individual EU countries. In this situation, 
therefore, there is no room for adoption of legislative competence by the European Commission.

Please find below IAB Poland's detailed comments on the proposal: 
 
[General comments] 
1. In our opinion, the promotion of media pluralism and press freedom should be carried 
out through existing mechanisms and enforcement, rather than through the creation of 
another regulation at the EU level. An increasing number of regulations is not a solution 
that supports the realization of freedom. We believe that due to the varying nature of the 
media market, depending on the member state in question, it should counteract 
violations of media pluralism and supp ort its independence by tailoring specific 
measures to the needs of the specific problem, and act through political pressure or 
recommendations from the European Union. The Commission should first promote 
media freedom and pluralism by strengthening the financial stability of the media. 
 
2. We note that in each of the 27 member states the media market has different historical, 
political and social conditions, there is no unified European media market. The proposal 
to regulate such disparate markets by applying common regulations to them, particularly 
in the form of a regulation, seems to be a challenge that is extremely difficult for individual 
member states. It is impossible to speak of pluralism without taking into account all the 
circumstances peculiar to each member state – introducing a new regulation without 
taking into account these differences will not ensure a unified EU media market, but 
rather will lead to greater disparities, without providing the support necessary to ensure 
media freedom. There is a serious risk that the regulation will become nothing more than 
a dead law that will not be respected in member states where problems are observed in 
respecting existing regulations aimed at protecting media freedom, including the press. 
In such a situation, however, the question aris es about the advisability of enacting the 
Act. 
 
3. Despite the fact that the Act aims to achieve goals with which it is impossible to disagree, 
we believe that in its current form the Act will not contribute to their realization. An EU 
regulation is a legal act that is binding and directly applicable in all member states - as 
such, regulations should be written in a concrete and unambiguous manner so as not to 
create discrepancies and so that their content is not in doubt. As it stands, the Act 
resembles a dir ective more than a regulation in the way that it indicates a specific 
direction and result to be achieved, more than it lays down specific provisions to ensure 
that the stated objective is achieved. The Act's use of generalities that can be interpreted 
and adapted differently in individual member states will mean that the Act, despite its 
form as a regulation, will not contribute to achieving uniform results across the European 
Union. Harmonization is not possible when clear and precise messages do not emerge 
from the Act. At the same time, we would like to point out that we do not believe that 
changing the form of the Act from a regulation to a directive is justified. Leaving to the 
discretion of national authorities the freedom to choose the form and means  by which 
the Act's goals were to be achieved would create the danger of greater interference in 
independent media, especially in countries where media pluralism and freedom are 
under threat. 
 
4. In our position dated on March 25, 2022, we again point out that it is inadequate to have 
a uniform approach to the media understood as both linear broadcast media and press. 
These media share fundamental differences regarding the basic principles of their

operation. With the current state of technological development , the barrier to entry for 
the digital press is very low. Another difference is the requirement to obtain a license to 
start operations - in some cases linear broadcasting, while operating online is in principle 
exempt from the need to obtain approval from state authorities - and these are just some 
of the many differences between press and radio and television. It is now extremely easy 
to start a media business using digital tools and platforms, and a growing number of 
entities and individual journalists a re taking advantage of these opportunities, forming 
an increasingly resilient part of the media landscape. In the face of the crisis of traditional 
media, they are often a source of more valuable and in-depth analysis and commentary 
than the mainstream med ia, while maintaining full independence and control over the 
material they produce. Importantly, an elaborate system of financing such ventures is 
emerging, including digital subscriptions, advertising revenue or direct audience support 
(crowdfunding). The digital market and the tools available on it are also making it easier 
for audiences to quickly access a variety of information sources, promoting pluralism 
and market competition.  
 
5. In our opinion, the Act does not comply with the principle of proportionality. As indicated 
in the Act's explanatory memorandum: [the Act] builds on the existing legal framework 
and will focus only on areas where additional EU action appears necessary for the 
proper functioning of the internal media market [...]. Moreover, the  Act's explanatory 
memorandum indicates that it aims to establish a common framework for the proper 
functioning of the internal market for media services, and the provisions proposed in the 
Act do not go beyond what is necessary to achieve this objective. In our view, the Act 
will not contribute to this objective, because a European market for media services does 
not exist - there are media markets in individual member states, because the 
circumstances of media markets - both historical, social and politica l - are different in 
each member state. Taking further additional regulatory actions, will not improve the 
situation in the media markets in member states and will not ensure media pluralism. 
Moreover, there are different problems and challenges facing the media market in each 
member state - solving these problems should be tailored to each country and should 
be done through recommendations and pressure from the European Union, not by 
creating yet another piece of legislation. With regard to the principle of subsidiarity, the 
EU legislator points out that the problems to be solved by the Act are of a cross-border 
nature and are not limited to individual member states - we do not share this 
argumentation due to the fact that in each member state the media market problems are 
different and require specific measures to solve them. Adoption of the Act will not 
contribute to helping member states ensure media freedom and pluralism. 
 
 [Detailed comments] 
 
1. The European Media Freedom Act must be based on clear definitions (vide Article 2 of 
the Act) to enable effective implementation and enforcement and avoid fragmentation of 
the internal market, according to the legal basis of the draft under discussion. It states 
that very large online platforms may also fall under the definition of "media service 
provider" if they exercise "editorial control" over their services or parts thereof, but the 
meaning of "editorial control" in this context is not clear. 
 
   
 
4 
 
The Act must provide clarity on the services covered and make clear distinctions to 
ensure that both media service providers and other stakeholders (e.g., third parties 
providing services to media service providers) can fulfill their respective obligations 
under the Media Freedom Act, and that authorities are able to enforce these provisions. 
The current text of the Act leads to a confusing situation in which the same very large 
Internet platform could potentially be classified as both a "media service provider" and a 
"device or user interface that controls or manages  access to and use of audiovisual 
media services."  
 
2. We are concerned about the establishing of a new European Media Services Council 
replacing the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA) and 
the expansion of its scope. Ensuring pluralism and promoting press freedom should not 
take place within bodies that include representatives of national authorities or regulators. 
The European regulator should not interfere in the media of individual member states. 
In addition, further to the recent adoption of the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets 
Act and the 2018 amendment to the Audiovisual Media Services Directive, it is important 
that the regulatory framework and enforcement set forth in these texts are not 
undermined or confused by t he new regulation, the Freedom of Expression Act. It is 
therefore necessary to ensure that the country of origin principle is not violated 
 
3. We are pleased to note that the EU legislator in the Act attempts to limit the influence of 
those in power over the information provided by the public media, particularly the articles 
on attempts to provide guarantees for the independent operation of media service 
providers with a public mission and the allocation of state advertising. At the same time, 
we believe that the phrases used in the Act contain general and vague expressions, 
which, when translated into their implementation, may end up in a dead law. In our 
opinion, the provisions on such an important issue as the financing of the media from 
public funds in the broadest sense require clarification. In addition, the measures 
provided for in Article 24, paragraph 2 should be expanded to include an obligation to 
publish the criteria adopted in deciding how to allocate public funds for media financing. 
Provided in pa ragraph.3 only the possibility of requesting such information by the 
national regulatory authority is insufficient, the provision of such information by the 
funding entity should be mandatory. At the same time, we realize that in many member 
states where there is currently a crisis of the rule of law and where there is a noticeable 
problem with ensuring media pluralism, regulations already exist to ensure press 
freedom - the problem is not a lack of regulation, but rather a lack of enforcement. 
 
4. We support the Act's ban on spyware and surveillance tools for media service providers, 
their employees or their families. At the same time, we note that the ban on such 
surveillance techniques can already be derived from the provisions of Polish criminal 
law or press law. 
 
5. To the extent that the Act establishes provisions controlling media market concentration, 
we would like to point out that many publishers, through cooperation and concentration, 
have been more resilient to the financial crisis and the drastic decli ne in advertising 
revenues, and have helped accelerate the digital transformation. We believe that it is 
inappropriate to further restrict the ability of many newspaper and magazine titles to 
remain in business by establishing further impediments to media investment. Ensuring 
pluralism cannot rely solely on fine-sounding assurances, but must also be grounded in 
the reality that economic factors are also important. Creating more restrictions and 
regulations will certainly not contribute to greater support fo r traditional media, which 
   
 
5 
 
often to compete with online media must be able to take advantage of concentration 
mechanisms. Reiterating our position dated on March 25, 2021, we would like to 
emphasize that it would be a mistake to view media concentration in a negative light. On 
the contrary, at a time when financing editorial teams and reaching readers is becoming 
increasingly difficult, many national media markets depend on concentration processes 
to ensure the survival of newspapers and magazines, and thus  ensure pluralism in the 
market. Competition law at the national and EU level has been, and continues to be, an 
appropriate and effective means to assess concentration and consolidation in media and 
online markets. 
 
6. We also draw attention to Article 6(2) of the Act (in conjunction with Recitals 20 and 21), 
which sets out obligations for the media, including editorial requirements. The provision 
is intended to apply to publishers who provide content on current affairs. According to 
the provisions indicated, publishers should establish internal procedures to ensure that 
editors are free to make individual editorial decisions. Recital 20 speaks to the goal of 
protecting editors from undue interference with their decisions in the course of their daily 
work. It is worth considering whether it is possible to implement this kind of principle of 
independence of editorial teams using a legal provision. This seems doubtful, since 
adherence to a culture of respect for editorial content should be an obvious part of 
organizing the activities of any reliable media outlet. If this is not the case, the real 
problem is not the lack of legal regulation, but the culture of publishing. Concurrently 
with the Act, a complementary document containing EC recommendations on the 
implementation of this obligation (Commission Recommendation of 16.9.2022 on 
internal safeguards for editorial independence and ownership transparency in the media 
sector) is being processed. It contains numerous EC guidelines on how to run a 
publishing house, both in terms of strictly publishing and journalism. Although the 
document is only of optional importance, it is hard to doubt that the recommendations 
contained therein will not become a market benchmark over time. If this were to happen, 
the publishing industry would probably be the first area of business with such rigorously 
outlined rules for its conduct. At this point, then, it is appropriate to repeat the rhetorical 
question of whether this circumstance will contribute to the development or further 
degradation of the publishing industry? Let us also reiterate that the guarantee of 
journalistic independence does not depend on the introduction of yet another legal 
provision, which, it must necessarily be noted, is formulated in an extremely enigmatic 
way. There are already legal guarantees of journalistic independence in Polish law (e.g., 
Article 10 of the Press Law of January 26, 1984 (i.e., Journal of Laws of 2018, item 
1914)), and freedom of speech or expression has its constitutional anchoring. The 
problem, however, is compliance with these regulations and enforcement of violations 
of these provisions.   
 
7. Referring to the above point, it is imperative to remember that an integral part of media 
freedom is also the welfare and interests of newspaper publ ishers. It is difficult to 
understand a situation in which the role of the latter is limited only to co -founding the 
general editorial line (see recital 20 of the Act: (...) after their owners and editors have 
agreed on the general editorial line). After a ll, it is the publishers who bear the 
multidimensional (legal, political, ethical or financial) responsibility for published content. 
That the role of publishers is to be limited to agreeing on a general editorial line with 
editors does not support press freedom, but is in fact an assault on it. If the regulations 
only allow for "general arrangements," how is a media publisher supposed to face the 
challenges of today's particularly complex reality, let alone the whole range of challenges 
of the future? The very process of digital media transformation requires intensive 
   
 
6 
 
interaction between management and editorial teams. The current wording of Article 6(2) 
of the Act also harms press diversity in that it makes media investment less attractive. 
   
8. The criteria that media service providers must meet to self-declare that they can benefit 
from Article 17 are unclear. In particular, it is unclear what constitutes "editorial 
independence from Member States and third countries," as well as what would constitute 
a " c o-regulatory or self -regulatory mechanism governing editorial standards, widely 
recognized and accepted in the relevant media sector in at least one Member State." 
The primary purpose of the Media Freedom Act is to protect media independence and 
safeguard media pluralism. Who qualifies as a media service provider is therefore an 
important first step, requiring greater clarity to ensure that the benefits granted to such 
providers are not abused in any way or potentially lead to the spread of misinformation 
or disinformation on the Internet. Policymakers should seek legal clarity through a stricter 
definition of media services. 
9. Finally, it is also worth noting that giving users more choice over how and what content 
they watch is important. However, as currently drafted, the parameters of Article 19 are 
unclear, including how it would address the Digital Services Act's existing obligation 
requiring online platforms to allow users to choose and change their preferred options 
for the recommendation system.

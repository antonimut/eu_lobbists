Executive summary 
The promotion of best practices and raising standards in the realm of cybersecurity must be 
supported. The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) is a unique opportunity to mitigate the piecemeal 
approach so far on cybersecurity requirements and to ensure coherence among an ever-
growing number of existing or proposed legal acts that aim to regulate products or 
organisations. The CRA can serve as a strategic compass for industry – it should aim to 
simplify, direct and guide while raising the horizontal level of harmonized cybersecurity 
standards across verticals and sectors. The CRA proposal widens the scope of application to all 
digital products and will have a huge impact on the competitiveness of the EU single market.  
There must be a commitment to raising cybersecurity standards across the EU and we support 
the intent of the European Commission to focus on technical measures to raise higher levels of 
cybersecurity for digital products in Europe. Strict market surveillance of digital products will not 
only enable higher cybersecurity in industry, but also protect consumers from risks. This 
requires that non-discrimination and the removal of political criteria in the carrying out of third 
party conformity assessment are enshrined under the CRA. This will give manufacturers and 
entities that will fall under the operational scope of the CRA business certainty as to how the 
CRA will be rolled out across the 27 member states of the EU. The CRA should ensure a level 
playing field regardless of the origin of manufacturers as this will ensure vendor diversity and 
improve cybersecurity. Removal of references to static, “non-technical factors” and an 
adherence to better regulation guidelines are strongly recommended to mitigate the potential for 
market fragmentation within the European Union. 
In addition, it will be important to take into account the additional costs and burdens that will be 
imposed on industry under the CRA. SMEs must be supported under the CRA and more work 
needs to be done to improve the level of cyber skills across Europe. One way to achieve this is 
through the implementation of objective, proportionate and transparent essential requirements 
to roll out the CRA in Europe. Furthermore, a reliance on international standards as guiding 
yardsticks to ensure the effective implementation of the CRA should be supported – and thus 
preserving the horizontal character of the CRA itself. 
There should be stronger efforts to preserve the “Brussels effect” and strengthen EU open 
strategic autonomy. The European Union has traditionally set global technology standards by 
allowing all stakeholders to have an influence in a structured and open way and in a manner 
that respects technological neutrality. Under the draft CRA proposal, the European Commission 
can use flexible criteria to justify the introduction of new critical products (for e.g. in Article 6 
language referring to “significant concerns related to materialization of adverse impact”) as well 
as unilaterally being able to re-call a specific product based on unclear criteria (Article 46). In 
addition, the European Commission can also specify “common specifications” (Article 19) which 
could ignore work done following the multi-stakeholder approach of European standards bodies. 
This could harm the “Brussels effect” as it reduces trust between different industry players. It 
can also lead to a lack of stakeholder participation through the procedure described in Article 19 
where the European Commission can take such unilateral action.

Key recommendations on the Cyber Resilience Act proposal. 
1. Strict market surveillance reduces politicization and uncertainty  
Sharpen language that relates to “significant concerns in relation to materialization of an 
adverse impact” – this is vague and general with a disproportionately wide remit and 
toolkit of measures. In the absence of relevant specified schemes, the European 
Commission should lean on international standards and schemes (e.g. ISO) and 
language should be added to state this position. Manufacturers should decide through 
self-assessment on appropriate measures. Add a text on non-discrimination. Address 
the mechanism that permits the removal of compliant products from the market. 
 
2. Wide scope creates market inefficiencies and adds costs 
At a time of rising prices, there is a balance or trade off to be struck between higher 
requirements and better efficiency in the market. Arbitrary enforcement and burden on 
industry could have knock on effects on prices and economic costs. Address the skills 
gap in industry through investment and a “level playing field”. 
 
3. Static requirements reduce vendor diversity and security  
Static criteria open the door for requirements to be introduced that can be subject to 
political interpretation by EU Member States. 5G toolbox implementation contributed to 
fragmentation of the EU single market while political criteria reduces vendor diversity. 
 
4. Reduce uncertainty in conformity assessment 
Provide reasonable timelines for conformity assessments and avoid Article 19 common 
specifications. International and European standards should be part of the final CRA 
texts. Which guiding principles are being used by the European Commission to decide 
which products fall under Annex III Class 1 and Annex III Class II of the CRA? 
    
5. Boost support for SMEs under the CRA 
While SMEs should implement equally high security requirements, it is important to 
provide support to SMEs to allow them to compete.  
 
6. Re-examine link to NIS2, RED Delegated Act and other legislation 
In light of possible distortions to the single market caused by NIS2 implementation, it is 
prudent to reconsider the inclusion of Article 6 para 5 altogether and it is suggested to 
delete the additional concept of “highly critical products”.

enforcement responsibilities in cross-border cases. Furthermore, Article 46 of the CRA 
creates a mechanism for the European Commission to trigger a process to remove 
products that are compliant if they have a “significant cybersecurity risk”.

Recommendations  
֍ Enshrine non-discrimination: This will ensure that market surveillance authorities 
carry out supervision in a proportionate, fair and strict manner, while at the same time 
protecting a level playing field for manufacturers. 
֍ Ensure proportionality: In addition, ensure that the Article 46 mechanism is 
proportionate by ensuring criteria for risk assessment & supervision is evidence-based 
and is non-discriminatory. Ensure that the timeline specified in Article 46 (4) is in line 
with existing market surveillance practice, and does not create unreasonable delays for 
vendors’ access to markets1.  
 
2. Wide scope creates market inefficiencies and adds costs 
 Burden on industry and costs to consumers: Scope is too wide and includes 
software as well as hardware, and components. At a time of rising prices, there is a 
balance or trade off to be struck between higher requirements and better efficiency in the 
market. Arbitrary enforcement and burden on industry could have knock on effects on 
prices and economic costs. To ensure the burden is not heavy on smaller firms or is 
directly passed down to consumers, manufacturers should be free to decide through 
self-assessment on appropriate measures. Another burden is the inclusion of free and 
open source software (FOSS) within the terms of the CRA.  
 
 Market inefficiencies: The impact assessment and call for evidence rightly points out 
the impact of the Cyber Resilience Act on the competitiveness of the EU market. In order 
to ensure that there is harmonization and to prevent fragmentation, the CRA should 
focus on preserving a level playing field for companies engaged in CRA compliance. For 
example, reference to the EU 5G toolbox as a soft law instrument is ‘not entirely 
consistent with good governance principles’ 2 and has created fragmented regimes 
across the single market and contributed to delays in the 5G rollout across the EU.

3. Static requirements based on political criteria reduce vendor 
diversity and cybersecurity  
 Risk assessment criteria should follow fair governance principles: A risk-based 
approach is a nuanced view of security that takes into account both the probability and 
the potential impact of an adverse event. However, probability of risk can easily be 
politicized, and the “probability” of any event should be based on facts, evidence and 
track record.  
 Static criteria reduces cybersecurity: Static criteria open the door for requirements 
that could be subject to political interpretation by Member States. Remember that the 
ICT supply chain is global as products are built from hundreds of components (hardware 
& software) obtained from across the globe. R&D and manufacturing are distributed 
across the globe too. Therefore, such criteria inspired by the 5G toolbox (as referenced 
in the CRA and in NIS2) serves to reduce vendor diversity and increases dependencies 
on incumbent “trusted vendors”. The reality of recent cyber-attacks underline the point, 
where “trusted” vendors were compromised by sophisticated actors with significant 
resources who exploited vulnerabilities and targeted consumers.  
 Use international approaches: A good example of a merit-based and an effective 
approach to the cybersecurity of digital products which focuses on the ability to deliver 
cybersecurity results, rather than prejudged static designations, are UNECE Regulations 
155 and 156. These regulations have introduced cybersecurity management and 
software updates management into road vehicles type approval processes, resulting in 
all new vehicle models being subject to cybersecurity type approval on EU markets from 
July 2022 and mandatory requirements for all new vehicles produced from July 2024. 
These regulations require OEM manufacturers to enforce best practice, security 
standards and security certification across the whole supply chain, in the true spirit of 
“Zero Trust” principles. While the Cyber Resilience Act does not apply to digital products 
used in type approved vehicles, it could be better to follow a similar model in the CRA, 
Page 9 of 17 
 
as arguably cybersecurity incidents in vehicles could result in more severe harm than 
IoT devices. 
 Focus on zero-trust and security by design: ENISA’s supply chain threat landscape 
report from 2021 showed that 62% of all supply chain attacks (including some of the 
largest ones like Solarwinds) occurred by “abusing the trust of a customer in the 
supplier”. EU should therefore develop resiliency standardization and certification 
framework e.g. following Zero-Trust principles similar to those articulated by European 
Standards Organisations (ESOs) in some areas. These have been successfully proven 
for over 10 years by global cloud providers, to withstand sophisticated adversaries.

Recommendations 
֍ Add non-static criteria to risk assessment: Recital 33 focuses on the link to the 5G 
toolbox (non-binding and political) and explicitly enshrines non-technical factors as a 
measure to also be upheld under CRA thus risking a fragmentation of the single market. 
Vendor diversity of products in critical networks, cyber hygiene and zero-trust 
architecture, as well as non-discrimination must be key principles of the CRA. 
 
4. Reduce uncertainty in conformity assessment 
 Preserve the Brussels effect: The European Union has traditionally set global 
technology standards by allowing all stakeholders to have influence in a structured way 
that respects neutrality and the rule of law. The CRA should seek to minimize and 
harmonise requirements to preserve the horizontal character of CRA and link to 
international standards, conduct impact assessments, and improve transparency. If 
national standards exist, they should be aligned with international standards. As ICT 
standardisation is already global in nature, an existing standardisation infrastructure can 
be used with the involvement of all stakeholders (ISO/IEC JTC1, CEN/CLC/JTC 13, 
ETSI TC CYBER). Involving industry expertise through participation in trade associations 
promotes business certainty. Furthermore, higher risk assessment requirements are 
supported for critical products, but these should only focus on technical and dynamic 
security criteria. Cybersecurity is a dynamic exercise with threats from across borders 
evolving all the time.

 Include harmonized standards to show conformance: Manufacturers should be able 
to decide their appropriate levels of assurance. This includes the ability to demonstrate 
conformity with international standards. A reduction in complexity between different,

and use of harmonised international standards in EU product rules under the NLF.

specify whether the obligation of a manufacturer to carry out a third-party conformity 
assessment for the corresponding requirements is waived. 
 Avoid innovation penalty on security updates: Note that even if products were

Recommendations 
֍ More clarity around the “Brussels Effect”: The European Commission can require 
manufacturers to produce an EU cybersecurity certificate in lieu of an exemption for third 
party conformity assessment (Class I), and then still decide whether this is adequate 
based on some criteria decided through an implementing act. In addition, the European 
Commission can also specify “common specifications” on the grounds that there are 
“undue delays in the standardization procedure” which leaves a lot of room for the 
European Commission to simply bypass work done through the multi-stakeholder 
approach of the European standards bodies. This harms the “Brussels effect” as it 
reduces trust between different industry players and leads to either the 
elimination/exclusion of non-European ICT firms in the standards development 
ecosystem, or the lack of stakeholder participation through the procedure described in 
Article 19 where the European Commission can simply take action. This must be 
avoided and stronger efforts at inclusion need to be made to preserve the Brussels 
effect. The work of the Stakeholder Cybersecurity Certification Group (SCCG) must be 
reflected in the CRA. 
֍ Ensure proportionate and objective conformity assessment: In addition to explicitly 
inserting language on proportionality and objectivity, as well as clarity around following 
EU regulations concerning processes for placement on the market, please note a few 
suggested additional recommendations to ensure proportionality in enforcement:  
► It should be made clear that the way to place products on to the market follows 
the 'Blue Guide', Manufacturers and other economic operators should utilize both 
EU existing cybersecurity schemes, as well as self-declaration of conformity with 
similar or equivalent international standards to demonstrate conformity with CRA 
essential requirements. In general, market participants should have a discretion 
on applying security guidance and best practices to implement the essential 
requirements. 
► Once clearer guidance in the form of harmonised standards, common 
specifications and/or European cybersecurity certification schemes is made 
available by the European Commission, manufacturers should have at least 12 
months to bring new products placed on the market into conformance.  
► There is a high level of legal uncertainty in the definition of “highly critical 
products” as the link to NIS2 makes it unclear which products will require 
mandatory third-party conformity assessments by notified bodies. There is also 
business uncertainty as to what actions are required of manufacturers to act on 
products already on the market but that have not been adequately categorized as 
“critical products”.  
► Lastly, timelines on incident notification, coordinated vulnerability disclosure, and 
sending security updates should be reasonable, proportionate and left to the 
discretion of manufacturers. It will also be important to revise Article 55 (2) to

further clarify the meaning of “substantial modifications” to encourage 
manufacturers to continue providing security improvements to products already

5. Minimise requirements on manufacturers, developers, SMEs 
 Open source embodies the business philosophy that success doesn't happen by working 
alone. The greatest achievements are the result of collaboration with others. Developers 
actively contribute to the work and operations of all the world's major open source 
software foundations. As currently drafted - perhaps unintentionally – the text targets 
open-source not for profit foundations rather than targeting the organizations that  
leverage open source for commercial activity. These organizations and the projects that 
they host are for the benefit of universal and global innovation and should be explicitly 
excluded from the terms of the Cybersecurity Resilience Act. This can be achieved by 
declaring software produced under open source license and distributed on not-for-profit 
basis are out of the scope for the certification requirements in the regulation and clarify 
the notions of “commercial activity” and “distributor”. Only such modifications would fit 
into the reality of the commercial use of OSS and recognize market realities.  
 
 Preserve horizontal nature and minimize requirements: The CRA does generally 
reflect strong product development and operational and business cybersecurity 
practices. CRA covers a wide range of products and it will also affect many entities 
including smaller and medium sized industrial firms. This is why it will be key to maintain 
the horizontal character of the CRA and minimise requirements. 
 Specify requirements in relation to risk: It will be key for the CRA to acknowledge and 
define end-use in terms of risk and assurance level, as well as clarify that the security 
profile, the nature of the products (hardware, software, components) and how they are 
serviced may differ. Given the potentially prohibitive costs related to changing a 
product’s design retrospectively, it is important to acknowledge that there are no silver 
bullets with regard to cybersecurity, and manufacturers can only ensure that products 
are robust and resilient to threats.  
 Clarity on components: The CRA should clarify the role of component manufacturers 
and whether conformity assessment procedures must be followed in cases where 
“placement on the market” applies when the component itself is not a “finished product” 
as per existing product legislation. This is an issue because Annex I requires that 
security should be considered holistically at system-level for a finished product. Given 
the broad definitions of hardware and software components, the requirements on 
components should consider its interaction with other parts of the finished product, as 
this is in line with existing requirements (see for example ETSI 303-645 or IEC 62443). 
The rationale behind the broad scope, as well as inclusion of general-purpose compute 
processors as critical products remains unclear from a security perspective. 
Page 13 of 17 
 
 Incident reporting: Article 11 (2) requires manufacturers to report any security incident 
to ENISA within 24 hours. In addition to not taking into account the severity of the 
incident, this article also departs from the recently agreed text in NIS2 that limits 
reporting to incidents that have significant impact to 72 hours at the earliest, with the full 
reporting following only after the impact from the incident has been fully addressed. This 
is more reasonable as 24 hours is a very short timeline for experts to gather reliable data 
and to also avoid over-reporting of minor incidents. Moreover, the definition of ‘actively 
exploited vulnerability” needs to be more deeply defined and such vulnerabilities should 
be handled by category and by level. 
 Vulnerability disclosure: Article 11 (1) requires that manufacturers report any “actively 
exploited vulnerability” to ENISA within 24 hours. This requirement is contrary to 
established cybersecurity practices that avoids the public disclosure of unpatched 
vulnerabilities given the security risks, and resources required by ENISA to confidentially 
handle this information. It would be advisable to only disclose those vulnerabilities that 
have been addressed, given the requirement in Article 11 (4) and Annex I for 
manufacturers to have processes to address vulnerabilities. In addition, Annex I, Article 
2 (1) should specify “publicly” known exploitable vulnerabilities. This will also allow for 
the CRA to take into account nuances across different products and align with existing 
legislation3. For e.g. it is not easy for all manufacturers to provide software or web-based 
changes and some vulnerabilities could exist in the hardware itself and will need 
sufficient time to address. 
1. Recital 37  
In order to facilitate vulnerability analysis, manufacturers should identify and document 
components contained in the products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software 
bill of materials. A software bill of materials can provide those who manufacture, purchase, and 
operate software with information that enhances their understanding of the supply chain, which has 
multiple benefits, most notably it helps manufacturers and users to track known newly emerged 
vulnerabilities and risks. It is of particular importance for manufacturers to ensure that their 
products do not contain vulnerable components developed by third parties. 
 
2. Article 3 – Definitions 
(37) ‘software bill of materials’ means a formal record containing details and supply chain 
relationships of components included in the software elements of a product with digital elements.

5. Article 11- Reporting obligations of manufacturers  
(1) The manufacturer shall, without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of becoming 
aware of it, notify to ENISA any actively exploited vulnerability contained in the product with 
digital elements. The notification shall include details concerning that vulnerability and, where 
applicable, any corrective or mitigating measures taken. ENISA shall, without undue delay, unless 
for justified cybersecurity risk-related grounds, forward the notification to the CSIRT designated 
for the purposes of coordinated vulnerability disclosure in accordance with [Article X] of NIS2 of 
Member States concerned upon receipt and inform the market surveillance authority about the 
notified vulnerability. 
(2) The manufacturer shall, without undue delay and in any event within 24 hours of becoming 
aware of it, notify to ENISA any incident having impact on the security of the product  with 
digital elements. ENISA shall, without undue delay, unless for justified cybersecur ity risk-related 
grounds, forward the notifications to the single point of contact designated in accordance with 
[Article X] of NIS2 of the Member States concerned and inform the market surveillance authority 
about the notified incidents.

Recommendations 
֍ Minimise requirements to help startups and developers: Small manufacturers 
should implement high security requirements and standards and it is important to 
provide some support to small startups, developers and SMEs in order to allow them to 
compete in the market. To achieve this, CRA should not be overtly prescriptive on 
technical requirements and minimize them to maintain the horizontal character of the 
CRA, while also discouraging Member States from using non-technical criteria for risk 
assessment (revisit Recital 33 or clarify definitions for e.g.). Another solution would be to 
provide an exemption for software and components from applying Article 11 obligations. 
Furthermore, it will be important to extend the notification window and further clarify 
when to inform ENISA about implemented mitigations. In addition, clarity is required on 
the handling of multiple notifications for the same security incident under NIS2 and CRA 
Art. 11(1). 
֍ Clarify definitions of requirements and products in scope: As mentioned above, 
clarity of definitions of requirements for components should be resolved by component 
manufacturers relying on manufacturers providing conformity for finished products, 
especially where testing is concerned. Definitions of “placing on the market” should, in 
addition to taking into account the peculiarities of software, also consider the ability of 
the device to function or support security capabilities without connecting to additional 
components. Similar clarification is needed to determine precisely when reporting 
obligations will start. Any reporting will be preceded by phases in which observations are 
made and in which an analysis of the observation will take place until a conclusion is 
drawn to report a positive result. Finally, it is key for the European Commission to clarify 
and further scope products that are designated as critical and also create unambiguous 
and clear criteria for the addition of new products to be designated as “critical”.  
֍ Stay with common security practices: The current state of practice is that security 
updates are checked when the product is powered on. This is applicable and is designed 
to ensure mitigations for known exploitable vulnerabilities without having negative effects 
on the business of economic operators. Concerning the envisaged labelling obligation 
for products, the labelling must be able to reflect the gamut of risks which the security of 
products potentially face during the product lifetime. Any information or uniform resource 
identifier (URI) should be labelled so as to make it clear where to find the most recently 
updated product security information. It is also important to require notification of actively 
exploited vulnerabilities to manufacturers by third-parties, when they become aware of 
such cases. 
 
6. Re-examine link to NIS2 and the RED Delegated Act  
• Re-examine link with NIS2: The CRA should clarify its relationship between NIS2, and 
other legislation that incorporates harmonized requirements, and explain which would 
apply in a given situation. Specifically, manufacturers of digital products used by

essential entities under NIS2 that use digital products in their supply chain will have to 
comply with requirements in the CRA. This is because the CRA uses the same 
categorisation method of NIS2. However neither legislation actually articulates the 
methodology itself, nor fully lists all products under the scope (as “critical products” for 
example), leading to uncertainty for industry. Furthermore, uncertain language in the text 
(Recital 11 for example) means that one entity (e.g. cloud services) would fall both under 
the CRA and the NIS2 disclosure and incident reporting requirements. Such uncertainty 
brings a potential for duplication of incident reporting and vulnerability requirements and 
processes, the types of products in scope, as well as in terms of the number of different 
reporting authorities. Removing this redundant link with NIS2 will reduce the cost and 
burden for compliance on EU industry, especially for smaller manufacturers of critical 
products, while increasing harmonization of the single market. 
• Further align with Radio Equipment Directive delegated act (RED DA): While recital 
15 acknowledges that the RED DA will be repealed, this is not clearly mentioned in the 
operative part of the legislation. Furthermore, the continued existence of a RED 
Delegated Act once the CRA is in place would lead to incoherence and fragmentation, it 
should be clarified in the operative legal part of the text that when both the CRA and the 
RED Delegated Act are both applicable, compliance with one should grant compliance 
with the other (presumption of conformity).

(5) The Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts in accordance with Article 49 to

(a) used or relied upon by the essential entities of the type referred to in Annex I to NIS2 or 
will have potential future significance for the activities of these entities; or  
(b) relevant for the resilience of the overall supply chain of products with digital elements 
against disruptive events. 
 
Recommendations 
֍ Clarify NIS2 requirements vis-a-vis CRA: In the current text, NIS2 criteria could add 
uncertainty and a political dimension, especially during coordinated risk assessment of 
Page 17 of 17 
 
critical products or supply chains. This is why, it is suggested that instead of linking the 
CRA to NIS2, it would make more sense to allow all requirements to apply regardless of 
criticality, and then introduce sectoral cybersecurity certification schemes to address 
emerging areas of risk, which will apply to specific products in those sectors. 
Furthermore, under NIS2, certification schemes for products can be made mandatory at 
national level once NIS2 is transposed. This underlines the importance of building 
certification schemes that rely on harmonised or international standards to ensure 
consistency across the EU27 countries. Vulnerability disclosure requirements in the CRA 
should be aligned with vulnerability remediation and disclosure practices agreed through 
NIS2 and should follow internationally well-known and accepted Coordinated 
Vulnerabilities Disclosure (CVD) procedures.  
֍ Clarify link between NIS2 and critical products: NIS2 already covers critical entities 
and their direct suppliers. By adding this link to the CRA, the danger of replicating 
requirements and adding uncertainty will be magnified, especially for global technology 
companies who sell their products across different EU member states. In light of these 
potential discrepancies to the single market, it is prudent to reconsider the inclusion of 
Article 6 para 5 altogether and it is suggested to delete this additional concept of “highly 
critical products” in addition to the Annex III list of Class I and Class II critical products. 
֍ Align RED DA and CRA through presumption of conformity: As it will be important 
to clarify the relationship between the RED Delegated Act and CRA when they are both 
applicable, it is suggested to add an amendment in the core legal, operative part of the 
text (for example Article 18) that explains a presumption of conformity with Article 3 (3) 
d, e, and f of the RED Delegated Act. Also it is important to clearly state in the operative 
text that all the standardization work undertaken through the RED Delegated Act 
process by European Standards Bodies should be taken into account in the preparation 
of harmonized standards under CRA. In particular in absence of dedicated harmonized 
standards for CRA, the harmonized standards developed for the RED DA shall be 
reused under the CRA conformity procedure.

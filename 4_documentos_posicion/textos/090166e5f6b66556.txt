 
 
European Media Freedom Act - Position paper by MFE – MEDIAFOREUROPE N.V. 
 
Introduction 
MFE-MEDIAFOREUROPE N.V. (henceforth, MFE) controls Mediaset S.p.A. and Mediaset España 
Comunicación S.A.; it also holds a stake in ProSiebenSat.1.  
MFE's cross -country development hinges on  the value of cultural diversity: structural integration 
allows economies of scale and organisational efficiencies  in baseline addressable costs, excluding 
content. Savings can be achieved mostly on digit isation of operations, company IT and data, 
administration, procurement, and advertising sales. Growth opportunities can thus be pursued with a 
view to investing savings in local content (news, entertainment, Tv drama, cinematographic works) 
and premium co -productions, development of European standards for connected TVs  and digital 
advertising formats.  
A solid and integrated organisat ional structure will be for MFE what a good plot is  to the  world-
acclaimed film “Perfetti Sconosciuti ” - co-produced by MFE’s subsidiary Medusa  - with successful 
remakes in more than 20 countries1: just like a good script can work in different cultures and different 
languages with local talent, a cost -efficient organization across the EU can support the financing of 
local content and promote cultural diversity. 
While t he pr esence of global players is steadily growing in the European audiovisual market , 
benefitting from economies of scale to streamline infrastructure costs will free up resources and 
facilitate investment in local content by European players. According to the European Audiovisual 
Observatory, TV broadcast is the less consolidated  sector in the EU’s AV market. European 
broadcasters must therefore gain scale in order to compete with global players which have resources 
to cater pan-EU offers and which operate in highly consolidated sectors of the AV market such as SVOD 
and pay-TV. To this end, ensuring a coherent approach to media  regulation throughout the EU will 
strengthen legal certainty for companies wanting to access new markets  and, ultimately, support a 
thriving and diverse media landscape in Europe. 
 
The European audiovisual market 
The European audiovisual market is worth about EUR 119 billion2. While each AV national market has 
its specificities, some trends can be observed throughout the singl e market. For instance, non -
European players have taken a strong foothold in  the EU. One in four of the top 50 TV groups and 
more than a third of the top 50 groups for on-demand services are controlled by non-European parent 
companies.

Comments to the Proposal 
MFE welcomes the Proposal for a European Media Freedom Act  (EMFA) presented by the European 
Commission (EC) on 16 September. “Ensure that media – public and private – can operate more easily 
across borders in the EU internal market, without undue pressure and taking into account the digital 
transformation of the media space ”7 is a forward -looking perspective aimed at enabling European 
media groups to grow and fully benefit from the single market. 
Both the legislative instrument and the legal basis are fit for purpose. In fact, a Regulation will ensure 
a coherent approach and harmonised enforcement throughout the EU. Over the years, the national 
transposition of minimum harmonisation directives - such as the Audiovisual Media Services Directive 
(AVMSD) – has created a certain degree of fragmentation, which prevented services from smoothly 
operating cross-border. A Regulation will prevent this from happening. This approach and the legal 
basis chosen by the EC (Article 114 TFUE) are aimed at strengthening the  internal mar ket and 
supporting the development of European media companies cross-border.  
The Proposal is consistent with a view to “laying down common rules for the proper functioning of the 
internal market for media services, including the establishment of the European Board for Media 
Services, while preserving the quality of media services 8”. For the first time, European law addresses 
the existence of a European market for media services. This may well be the achievement of a thirty- 
year endeavour to build what was “devised under the Delors Commission and which led to the Single 
Market becoming reality in 1993, [and that] came as an electroshock: it gave Europe the means to 
shape, at last, its political and economic destiny.”9.  
The Proposal does not to do away with national media markets , nor  does it ignore  the need to 
safeguard national cultural specificities, such as linguistic pluralism, but rather, local markets coexist 
within a greater European market for media services. Ther efore, national regulators shall take this

Chapter II – Rights and duties of media service providers and recipients 
A pluralistic and independent media landscape  and a stronger market for media  are closely 
intertwined and should be considered as a single objective . In fact,  pluralism and indepen dence 
constitute the prerequisites for a media service that chooses to operate across Europe.  
Article 3 provides that “ Recipients of media services in the Union shall have the right to receive a 
plurality of news and current affairs content, produced with  respect for editorial freedom of media 
service”. While minimum standards for quality media in view of a common European interest  are a 
welcome step forward, in the majority of Member States , media service providers exercise editorial 
responsibility by abiding by the highest quality standards and following prescriptive rules on internal 
and external pluralism.  
In Italy these standards are enshrined in law . In accordance with the initial regulation of the  Italian 
dual public and private broadcasting system, adopted in the 1980s and 1990s, article 4 of the current 
TUSMA10 provides general principles that audiovisual and radio services must follow to offer quality 
content to viewers (e.g. objectivity, impartiality, openness to diverse opinions and political ideas, fight 
against disinformation). The Italian legal framework also sets prescriptive ex-ante and ex-post rules to 
ensure internal pluralism. In order to obtain their national authorisations, commercial broadcasters 
must not only cater content which informs and entertains the public in an accurate and balanced way, 
protects minors and upholds cultural diversity , but also provide balanced coverage to all political 
parties. In fact, since the 1990s, the Par Condicio Law sets stringent rules to ensure political parties 
are allotted fair and balanced oppor tunities to reach the public  and prevent misinformation  on 
political matters and politically sensitive issues. To ensure compliance,  Mediaset has developed 
guidance for its broadcasting structures, including for news directors, clearly outlining the r ules 
concerning equal access of political entities to television and the implementation measures issued by 
the NRA AGCOM.

For over thirty years  now, Mediaset has taken extremely seriously the  direct responsibility it bears 
over the news services. To this end, the national regulatory framework is at the basis o f the internal

This commitment to high-quality information is also confirmed by Mediaset’s decision to create, as

In Spain, the Ley General de la Comunicación Audiovisual  (LGCA)12 includes provisions to ensure

importance of these media in shaping public opinion. For instance, Article 5 of the LGCA provides that 
pluralism shall be promo ted by encouraging the existence of varied  public and commercial media 
which reflect the ideological and political pluralism and the cultural and linguistic diversity in society. 
Additionally, the LGCA states that self-regulation shall be promoted in order to adopt codes of conduct 
on internal pluralism for audiovisual media service providers. The Spanish regulatory body - Comisión 
Nacional de los Mercados y la Competencia (CNMC)  - is in charge of ensuring transparency and 
pluralism in the audiovisual sector and the independence and impartiality of the public media.  
The independence of individual editorial decisions in Mediaset España newsrooms is fully guaranteed 
by means of two internal Codes. Since 2019 the company has a “Code of Ethics” 13, mandatory for all 
employees, which clearly states that journalists working for the company must carry out their work 
with objectivity and independence in the information they provide. The Code of Ethics sets an internal 
procedure to report an infringement of any of the  principles laid down in the Code14 and exercisable 
through an application form 15. In 2022, the i ndependence of the newsroom has been further 
reinforced with the approval of the company’s own Newsrooms Ethics Code16. The Code stresses that 
editorial decisions must not be influenced by political or commercial pressures, economic or personal 
interests and that journalists must not accept gifts or privileges that could lead to a loss of their ability 
to provide objective and independent information in relation to any company or institution appearing 
in their videos. The infringement of th e Code is also subject to the complaint procedure outlined 
above.

Furthermore, in Italy , rules on external pluralism  are set in Article 51 of Legislative Decree 208/21 
amended in accordance with the findings of the Court of Justice of the EU outlined in its Ruling of 3 
September 2020 (C-719/18 - Vivendi). This is an innovative and dynamic concept t hat provides for a 
specific, case -by-case assessment of the potentially harmful situations through well -identified 
parameters, moving "from a system of ex -ante thresholds rigidly determined, to a system that 
provides for adequate concrete verifications (through market analyses conducted by the NRA AGCOM) 
of possible violations of pluralism, setting aside a purely "automatic" application of concentration 
thresholds"18. 
In concrete terms, the law prohibits the creation of a position of significant market power detrimental 
to pluralism and confers to AGCOM the prerogative to monitor and to control progress and evolution 
of the integrated communications system (SIC) and of each market thereof, also with reference to the 
revenues generated in Italy by entities established abroad. In order to assess whether a company or a 
group of companies is in a situation of significant market power which may be detrimental to 
pluralism, AGCOM has to take into account  not only revenues but also: (i) the level of static and 
dynamic competition within the system , (ii) barriers to entry, (iii) convergence between sectors and 
markets, (iv) synergies arising from activities carried out in different but contiguous markets, (v) 
vertical and conglomerate integration, (vi) availability an d control of data, (vii) direct or indirect 
control of necessary and scarce resources (such as broadcasting frequencies), (viii) the economic 
efficiency of the enterprise, also in terms of economies of scale, range and network, (ix) the audience 
share of r adio and television programmes, including news programmes, cinematographic works, 
editorial and online products and services.  Shall an investigation  identify positions of significant 
market power detrimental to pluralism , AGCOM must intervene  to ensure they are promptly  dealt 
with. In addition , operations likely leading to dominance must be  duly inhibited and their effects 
overturned. Art. 51 applies to traditional media (i.e. press, radio, audiovisual media services), to online 
activities hav ing an impact on pluralism  and to  any online activity generating revenues on the 
advertising market.  
At present , internal and external pluralism have only been defined at national level, creating a 
fragmented media landscape across the EU. This approach conflicts with the cross-border and global 
dimension of digital players active in the media market. While, at first sight, the digit isation of 
information and the intermediation of online platforms seemed to strengthen media pluralism, the 
selection of news  based on data tracking and on algorithmic decisions has locked citizens in eco -
chambers preventing access to diverse sources of information. This process, carried out by a handful 
of powerful online platforms operating in the digital space  in the absence of an updated framework 
for digital services - recently introduced with the Digital Services Act Package - has been detrimental

Chapter III - Framework for regulatory cooperation and a well-functioning internal market for media 
services 
Media pluralism and cultural div ersity are a cornerstone of Europe’s identity. However, the 
prerogatives left to Member States have led to divergent approaches.  While global players with the 
scale and strength to access very different markets have been allowed to expand cross border, 
European media groups face restrictions and are prevented from acquiring the necessary scale to 
compete on fair terms with global streaming services and online platforms.  
Supporting a well-functioning internal market for media services is, therefore, of the essence. To this 
end, Chapter III of the Proposal aims at fostering coherence and coordination among Member States, 
while allowing national prerogatives and subsidiarity to be maintained. 
For instance, replacing ERGA with the European Board of Media Services is an important step towards 
a strengthened collaboration among national authorities. While respecting the subsidiarity principle 
provided in the TFEU, the fact that the Board bolsters the exchange of best practices and strengthens 
cooperation among NRAs is positive as it will trigger coherent s national media pluralism frameworks 
and, ultimately, facilitate cross-border service provision. 
In this context, the EC can play a steering role within the Board. While upholding the independence of 
the Board, as it has no voting rights, the EC can facilitate a consistent approach to media pluralism 
throughout the EU. In fact, as outlined in Recital 24 “it is essential that the Commission and the Board 
work and cooperate closely” as the EC could assist the Board in several ways: from the application of 
the AVMSD to the interplay between the EMFA and the Digital Services Act  Package. Therefore, the 
EC can play an honest broker role within the Board of Media Services. Exclud ing the EC from the 
process counters the very aim of strengthening the internal market. 
• Chapter III, Section 4 - Provision of media services in a digital environment 
This section sets out important provisions that complement the horizontal framework devised by the 
Digital Services Act Package and by the sector-specific provisions in the AVMSD. 
At present, platforms arbitrarily decide what can be on their service, providing limited information 
about their policies to users and media services. Although the entry into force of the Digital Services 
Act will limit the platforms’ discretion and ensure their T&Cs are transparent, content that is selected 
by editorially responsible actors is still moderated and demoted by  services bearing no editorial 
responsibility19. 
 
This can severely affect media pluralism and raises competition concerns in the online advertising 
market, as platforms may remove content of competing AV outlets  in a multi -sided market . To 
preserve the integrity of our service, the visibility of our content and ensure fair competition in the 
online advertising market, content lawfully uploaded by regulated media service providers should not 
be arbitrarily removed by providers of intermediary services.  
 
Article 17 of the EMFA proposal mitigates these imbalances as it sets a transparent framework for the 
removal or suspension of media content on VLOPs. Clear processes and possibly guidance from the EC 
on this subject are necessary instruments to ensure citiz ens continue to have access to diverse  and 
balanced information online. While this is a positive first step, merely informing media service

Article 19 is another crucial provision  to safeguard pluralism:  it ensures European broadcasters  
continue to be easily accessible to viewers on digital interfaces and devices.  The penetration of 
connected TVs is raising major concerns in terms of sustainability of European media and media 
pluralism. In fact, these devices are closed ecosystems built around differen t layers of content and 
services that are pre-organised for viewers by TV manufacturers through their operating systems. The 
so called “Google business model”, previously deployed on smartphones and PCs, is now being 
replicated on connected TVs, encompassing very similar exclusionary practices. 
 
In order t o organise content and services on connected TVs, manufacturers rely on commercial 
agreements that are enforced through their operating systems and user interfaces. Service providers 
with higher financial capacities can therefore obtain better prominence and discoverability via 
dedicated buttons on the remote control (e.g., Amazon Prime, Netflix and YouTube buttons) and top 
ranking on TV proprietary interfaces.  Moreover, these practices also prevent viewers  from easily 
retrieving the linear channels and digital services from their national audiovisual media service 
providers, as the latter cannot compete with the financial capacities of global VOD services providers 
and video-sharing platforms. 
Allowing visibility of services on connected TVs to be decided simply via commercial agreements must 
be strongly countered as it  will severely impair the European media service providers’ capacity to 
compete on fair terms. The right of customisation of aud iovisual media offers provided in Article 19 
sets a good basis to prevent this trend. Furthermore, the reference to Article 7a AVMSD creates the 
necessary balance between the right of users to customise the AV interface on their connected device 
and the fundamental right to receive impartial information20. This interplay shall be upheld throughout 
the legislative process. 
The link between Article 19 and Article 15 is also very positive. In fact, only a limited number of 
Member States have issued rules to ensure visibility of general interest services on devices and digital 
interfaces. Once again, the role of the EC may be helpful and should be valued also in this context, as 
the guidelines it may issue on the matter (Article 15.2.a) should ensure a consisten t application of 
Article 7a AVMSD throughout the EU. 
• Chapter III, Section 5 - Requirements for well -functioning media market measures and 
procedures 
Articles 20 to 22 should be read with reference to the legal basis of the EMFA – article 114 TFUE . 
Therefore, both the opinions of the Board on national measures affecting the operation of media 
service providers (Article 20) and the Board opinions on media market concentration (Article 21) 
should aim at improving the functioning of the internal marke t. This is especially true given the fact 
that “differences between national rules are such as to obstruct the fundamental freedoms and thus 
have a direct effect on the functioning of the internal market or cause significant distortions of 
competition”21.  
Once again, the Proposal respects national  subsidiarity. In fact, article 21 does not prevent national 
authorities from taking into account purely national aspects in their assessments  of media market

In the EC proposal, both articles only apply to media market concentrations. Nonetheless, European

involving online players, whose activities have consequences on media pluralism, should be included 
in the scope of articles 21 and 22. 
• Chapter III, Section 6 - Transparent and fair allocation of economic resources 
Reliable and transparent audience measurement is crucial for the independence and sustainability of 
advertising-funded broadcasters and, ultimately, for the diversity of audiovisual media services and 
for a well-functioning media market. 
Important steps have been taken in the Digital Markets Act to address the shortcomings of audience 
measurement on gatekeepers’ services. This said, the EMFA proposal aims at ensuring that solid and 
verifiable audience measurement systems are in place throughout the media and digital ecosystem. 
To this end, the scope of Article 2(14) raises some concerns with reference to the proposed approach. 
In fact, the definition of audience measurement should encompass not only the audien ce 
measurement systems of media services - which are already transparent, verifiable and reliable – but 
especially those developed by online platforms. 
The problems which media service providers face are rightly outlined in Recitals 45 and 46: verifiability 
and transparency should be imposed to all audience measurement systems. This said, provisions on 
audience measurement should not lead to a single audience measurement currency. Broadcasters 
and online platforms are , in fact, very different s ervices subject to  very different rules.  Since 
broadcasters are, indeed, publishers and thus hold editorial responsibility, they face stringent rules to 
provide quality content to viewers . Online platforms , instead, are subject to an incremental 
framework of due diligence obligations in light o f their size and reach , which only regulates how 
content is promoted and presented to online users. It is therefore important to keep the ir audience 
measurement systems separate as the former value duration and viewab ility while the latter have a 
click-based approach. 
This distinction is ever more important given the fact that, at present, some online operators are trying 
to impose their proprietary audience measurement systems on the market 23. This is particularly 
worrying as it would create a common currency which would not reflect the different nature of the 
services measured. 
Therefore, we value the principle-based approach provided in Article 23. Transparency, impartiality, 
inclusiveness, proportionality, non -discrimination and verifiability should be applied to all audience

Conclusion 
Following the above, we reiterate our strong support to the EC proposal. The approach suggested

I. Hikvision and Cybersecurity 
 
Hikvision1, the global leader in security video camera technology, appreciates the opportunity to 
participate in the consultation process on the proposed Cyber Resilience Act. Hikvision applauds 
the EU for its continued global leadership in developing a cybersecurity regulatory framework, 
and we welcome the proposal to create and apply strong requirements to protect end -users, 
end-user data, computing systems and the Internet at large.  
 
Hikvision manufactures products and provides solutions that make a positive difference for 
society — helping safeguard people, property, assets, and combat crime.  The company’s core 
competency of video security technology is utilized in more than 150 countries. 
 
The company established its European headquarters in 2009 in Hoofddorp , near Amsterdam in 
the Netherlands. We employ more than 400 professionals across 20 European countries, and we 
have 14 business units in the region. We have many successful partnerships in Europe, where we 
support customers with their video security needs.   
 
Our vision is to provide customers with secure and trusted technology. We are constantly 
updating our own practices on cybersecurity and encourage more extensive communication 
among stakeholders, focusing on addressing the risks and increasing network  defence 
capabilities. We also regularly provide users with advice and best practices on how to protect 
their equipment against security breaches.  
 
Hikvision has been an industry leader in cybersecurity and our products adhere to some of the 
most rigorous global standards and certifications, including ISO 27001, ISO 9001:2008, CMMI 
Level 5, and AICPA SOC. Hikvision also leads in securing customer data by requiring end-users to 
create passwords at the time of first use for any products, in fact, Hikvision was one of the first 
companies in the video security business to remove default passwords from their products.

II. Hikvision’s position on the Cyber Resilience Act proposal 
 
Cybersecurity4 and data privacy continue to be one of the greatest challenges facing our digital 
world. Technology’s rapid growth creates a challenging env ironment to effectively create and 
apply regulations. Hikvision applauds the EU for its continued global leadership in creating and 
applying strong requirements to protect end -users, end-user data, computing systems and the 
Internet at large. The EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) , supplementing the existing cybersecurity 
regulatory framework, comes at a time when new technologies  – such as 5G and AI – are no 
longer buzz words  but are actively becoming a part of our everyday lives. To steer and control 
technology to focus on security and privacy, the CRA should set standards for digital products to 
adhere to. 
 
There is no doubt that innovation drives the exciting world of technology. T oday, t echnology 
companies and hobbyists are creating the tech of tomorrow. Low-cost computing systems like 
Raspberry Pi and Arduino provide valuable learning tools and the possibility of innovation to 
much of the world’s population. This type of innovation is what drives technology. However, 
when someone invents a widget to solve a problem, they are focused on that problem and not 
necessarily on security and privacy. Standards are needed to ensure that digital products meet 
baseline requirements before they are offered to the public.  Also, we believe security controls 
need to be introduced at the earliest stages in the development lifecycle (shift left  principle). 
Enacting legislation that  democratizes security practices will  help protect systems from 
manufacturer to end-user.   
 
All computing systems have vulnerabilities, we just haven’t found them all yet. However, digital 
products can, and should, have a set of processes around them that provide continued 
cybersecurity and privacy support during the lifecycle of the product.  
 
Scope and definition 
The proposed version of the CRA aims to address cybersecurity challenges of “products with 
digital elements.” Such definition could result in including in the scope of the Regulation products 
such as a digital bedside alarm clock, designed in 1980s with basic digital elements, which is 
presumably is not the focus of the legislation. Instead, we would recommend using a more 
specific description of the scope of the products that would be subject to the CRA’s requirements. 
The legislation is focusing on software products and products that are computerized and 
connected, meaning the products have a central processing unit (CPU) and operating system, 
they run software, and use some form of connectivity , including but not limited to, Wi -Fi, 
Bluetooth, cellular (4G/5G) or traditional wired networking. A phrase/term such as 
‘computerized connected devices (CCD)’, would better represent the intended scope of the CRA.

Impact Assessment 
The goal of the CRA i s good and needed but this must be done with caution. Many products  
currently available on the market  use open-source software , with part of the software being 
continuously supported, and some of it being created by hobbyists. While widespread use of this 
type of code is one of the problems we have today ( for instance Log4j vulnerabilities of 2021), 
requiring support of such open-source code could lead to potential issues, such as: 
1. A reduction in innovation when a hobbyist developer wants to share software but does 
not have the time or financial means to support it over the long term. 
2. Some product developers might want to hide their use of a third-party code.  
 
A requirement for manufacturers of CCDs to disclose a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) would 
theoretically remedy the second issue  by enabling them to build a more secure cybersecurity 
posture, however as the SBOM is mostly produced on an honor system , it would not eliminate 
the threat entirely.

Additionally, companies should be moving toward a Zero Trust 10 architecture. This includes 
vendors and end -users of digital products. While vendors need to embed cybersecurity and 
privacy controls in their products, the network is the first, and most important line of defense. 
The Zero Trust architecture  (i.e., least-privilege access controls, granular micro segmentation , 
and multifactor authentication (MFA)) greatly reduces the risks of an organization becoming the 
victim of a cyberattack. 
 
Policy Options 
We recommend rolling out the requirements for manufacturers of  digital products in the 
following phases: 
 
Phase 1  would include the Commission adopting delegated acts and providing guidance and 
recommendations within 12 months since the entry into force of the Regulation . It would allow 
CCD producers to self-certify against the conformity requirements specified in Annex I.  
 
Phase 2 would require that CCDs be certified against the Commission’s requirements by a third-
party organization or using a cybersecurity tool that is approved by the Commission before the 
products are offered on the open market. This would work similarly to car inspections that are 
required in some regions where cars must be looked over by a n authorized mechanic to ensure 
that they are safe to drive . However, in this situation, the third -party cybersecurity company 
would test the CCD to ensure they do not have known vulnerabilities and that they are complying

Specific recommendations on the text of the proposal 
Annex I 
1.  Security Requirements relating to the properties of products with their digital elements 
(1) “Products with digital elements shall be designed, developed and produced in such a way 
that they ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity based on the risks.” 
• This is a good phrase, but it is not measurable. The phrase “be designed, 
developed and produced” is used multiple times across the proposal. There needs 
to be a set of standards for which to comply to ensure that the requirements can 
be enforceable.  
(2) “Products with digital elements shall be delivered without any known exploitable 
vulnerabilities.” 
• We would recommend expanding the statement to include: “Exploitable meaning 
that vulnerable code may exist in the product as long as mitigating controls are in 
place to make non-exploitable.” 
(…) 
(3) “On the basis of the risk assessment referred to in Article 10(2) and where applicable, 
products with digital elements shall: 
(c) protect the confidentiality of stored, transmitted or otherwise processed data, personal 
or other, such as by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit by state-of-the-art 
mechanisms;” 
• The language of “encrypting relevant data” could result in using very weak 
encryption standard s to comply with the provision. We would therefore 
recommend pointing to an encryption standard. 
 
2.  Vulnerability Handling Requirements 
Section 2 of Annex I provides relevant requirements, however the following elements should also 
be considered: 
1. Include details of the vulnerability with a CVSS severity score. 
2. Disclosure of a vulnerability on company/organization website. 
3. Requirement to create a CVE.  Some companies hide their vulnerabilities on a difficult to 
find website. 
4. Provide customer/end-user alerts and communication on vulnerability where possible.

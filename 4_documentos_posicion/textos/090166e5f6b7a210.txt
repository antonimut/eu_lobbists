Apple Response to the EU Proposal for a Cyber Resilience Act 
Brussels, January 2023 
Apple designs security into every product and service, creating industry-leading security architectures that address the 
unique requirements of mobile, watch, desktop and home. Importantly, the Cyber Resilience Act focuses on increasing 
security for everyone, and Apple is pleased to see it adopt certain concepts we have long championed.   
Apple welcomes the Cyber Resilience Act’s focus on product security because we are committed to offering our uses the 
best data security in the world and we recognize the importance and role of strong product security in strengthening the 
EU’s overall cyber-resilience. Mobile devices are a gateway for European citizens to interact with the world, from making 
financial transactions and using public transport, to exercising, proving vaccination status, and identifying themselves 
digitally. Mobile devices are used to connect with friends and family, access public services and banking, and engage in 
e-Commerce. They also are relied on by users to store highly personal and sensitive data, including photos, videos, 
heath data and financial information. 
The proliferation of data has been accompanied by a dramatic rise in digital attacks. Globally, the total number of data 
breaches have increased fourfold between 2013 and 2021. More specifically, as mobile devices become tools for an 12
ever-increasing array of purposes, they have also progressively become targets for bad actors to exploit — from cyber-
criminals to state sponsored groups. With highly sophisticated methods on the rise, they target mobile devices as a 
means for propagating cyber intrusions. ENISA’s threat intelligence reports show a significant increase in malware 
infections and Nokia’s 2020 Threat Intelligence Report indicated that mobile platforms which do not centralize and 
manage the access of third party software are particularly vulnerable to attacks. 
Cybercrime puts all stakeholders in the mobile ecosystem at risk — and while consumers are often the primary targets, 
malware attacks on mobile devices can harm developers, online advertisers, and businesses that are not even direct 
participants in the mobile app ecosystem. The rise of corporate data breaches reflects a fundamental change in the 
digital landscape. In 2017, one in five interactions between consumers and companies took place online. By 2020, that 
percentage had skyrocketed to more than half. Whilst people clearly benefit from the ability to interact with businesses 3
online, this rapid shift in behavior has created even more opportunities for criminals to target consumers’ personal data. 
Criminals are increasingly using social engineering techniques to go after corporations to steal data or target them in 
ransomware attacks. The economic incentives are significant, with corporate login credentials of administrators selling 
for as much as $120,000 on the dark web. The damage caused by these types of attacks are not just limited to 4
companies themselves, but can be devastating for consumers as well. For example, between 2020 and 2021 
ransomware attacks against healthcare organizations nearly doubled. Many of these cyber intrusions to date have 5
occurred on networks, computers, or whatever technology platform has exposed and easily exploitable vulnerabilities, 
but as foreign adversaries improve their attack capabilities, the risks to mobile devices will grow. 
As such, maintaining security and privacy on mobile devices is of critical importance, and providing a framework that 
allows for a secure mobile eco-system is essential for Europe’s overall cyber-resilience.  
It is also why Apple is committed to developing leading security technologies — designed to safeguard personal

I. Apple’s Experience with Taking a Comprehensive Approach to Mobile Security 
Apple strongly supports the main objectives of the Cyber Resilience Act.  
Every Apple mobile device combines hardware, software and services to achieve greater security. For example, Apple-
designed silicon and security hardware powers critical security features. Software protections work to keep the operating 
system and third-party apps protected and services provide a mechanism for secure and timely software updates, power 
a protected app ecosystem and facilitate secure communications and payments. The result is a secure platform that not 
only protects devices and their data, but the entire ecosystem including what users do locally, on networks and with key 
internet services.  
In keeping with the Act’s first two objectives, our approach to security covers the entire lifecycle of our products and 
services and across our entire platform.  
  
A comprehensive overview of Apple’s approach to platform security can be found in our May 2022 Apple Platform 
Security Guide. 
System Security 
We also support the Act’s second main objective; to ensure a coherent cybersecurity framework, facilitating compliance 
for hardware and software producers.  
We are a hardware and software producer, of course, and so we can offer system security for controlling access to 
system resources in Apple devices without compromising usability. System security encompasses the boot-up process, 
software updates and protection of computer system resources such as CPU, memory, disk, software programs and 
stored data. 
An important part of Apple security is secure boot, which protects the system from malware infection at boot time. 
Secure boot begins in hardware and builds a chain of trust through software, where each step is designed to ensure that 
the next is functioning properly before handing over control. This security model supports not only the default boot of 
Apple devices but also the various modes for recovery and timely updates on Apple devices.  
Subcomponents like Secure Enclave also perform their own secure boot to help ensure they only boot known-good code 
from Apple. The update system is designed to prevent downgrade attacks, so that devices can’t be rolled back to an 
older version of the operating system (which an attacker knows how to compromise) as a method of stealing user data. 
In addition to secure boot, Apple devices include boot and runtime protections so that they maintain their integrity during 
ongoing operation. Apple-designed silicon on iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, Apple TV, HomePod and a Mac with Apple 
silicon provide a common architecture for protecting operating system integrity. 
App Security  
We further promote security through our approach to apps. 
Today, providing for secure use of apps is among the most critical purposes of a security architecture. Even as apps 
provide amazing productivity benefits for users, they also have the potential to negatively impact system security, 
stability and user data if not handled properly. 
To mitigate risks associated with apps, Apple has implemented various layers of protection to help ensure that apps are 
free of known malware and tampering and that app access to user data is carefully mediated. These security controls 
facilitate a stable, secure platform for apps, in turn enabling thousands of developers to securely deliver hundreds of 
thousands, if not millions, of apps for iOS, iPadOS, and macOS — all without impacting system integrity. Users can also 6
access these apps on their Apple devices without fear of viruses, malware, or unauthorized attacks. 
The protections on the iPhone and iPad are especially robust because all apps are obtained from the App Store—and all 
apps are sandboxed—to provide the tightest controls. 
A key part of our approach to app security involves and requires transparency with respect to security properties, 
consistent with the Act’s third main objective. Every app and every update submitted to the App Store is closely reviewed 
by both automated systems and human experts against technical content and design criteria described in the publicly 
available App Store Review Guidelines. Areas of review include ensuring that apps do not unnecessarily request access 7
to sensitive permissions or data and that they do not make misleading or false claims in marketing materials or 
otherwise. The App Review process is critical to device security. 
Sandboxing is another tool that protects user data from unauthorized access by preventing apps from gathering or 
modifying information stored by other apps. More specifically each app has a protected directory for its files that only the 
app itself can access. A third-party app can only access user information it wasn’t given first-party access to by using 
services explicitly provided by iOS and iPadOS, which are designed to prevent unauthorized data access across apps. 
Apple also provides an array of additional protections, including those against phishing, by implementing the  common 
FIDO alliance and World Wide Web Consortium passwordless sign-in standard, which allows websites and apps to offer 
an end-to-end passwordless option.  8
Data Security 
The secure boot chain, system security and app security capabilities all help to verify that only trusted code and apps 
run on a device. Apple devices have additional encryption features to safeguard user data, even when other parts of the 
security infrastructure have been compromised (for example, if a device is lost or is running untrusted code). All of these 
features benefit both users and IT administrators, protecting personal and corporate information and providing methods 
for instant and complete remote wipe in the case of device theft or loss. 
iOS, iPadOS, and Macs with Apple silicon use a file encryption methodology called Data Protection. Data on an Intel-
based Mac is protected with a volume encryption technology called FileVault. In all current Apple devices, cryptographic 
key hierarchies are rooted in the dedicated silicon of the Secure Enclave, and a dedicated AES Engine supports line-
speed encryption and helps ensure that long-lived encryption keys aren’t exposed to the kernel operating system or 
CPU, where they could be compromised. In addition to using Data Protection and FileVault to help prevent unauthorized 
access to data, Apple uses operating system kernels to enforce protection and security. The kernel places apps in a 
sandbox, which restricts what they can do on the system, and a mechanism called Data Vaults restricts which apps are 
allowed to access certain sensitive information. 
Network Security  
In addition to the built-in safeguards Apple uses to protect data stored on Apple devices, there are many measures 
organizations can take to keep information secure as it travels to and from a device. All of these safeguards and 
measures fall under network security. In keeping with the Act’s fourth and over-arching purposes, these measures – and 
all of the security measures we build into our products and services – enable businesses and consumers to use products 
with digital elements securely. 
Because users must be able to access corporate networks from anywhere in the world, it’s important to help ensure that 
they are authorized and that their data is protected during transmission. To accomplish these security objectives, iOS, 
iPadOS, and macOS integrate proven technologies and the latest standards for both Wi-Fi and cellular data network 
connections. That’s why our operating systems use — and provide developer access to — standard networking 
protocols for authenticated, authorized and encrypted communications.

Finally, we understand that cybersecurity and cyber resilience is an ongoing process requiring constant innovation and 
effort -- a journey rather than a destination.

II. Key Areas of the Proposed Cyber Resilience Act That Would Benefit from Further Development 
As discussed above, Apple is fully committed to the objectives the CRA is aiming to achieve, and supports the proposal’s 
ambition to improve the overall security of products sold on the EU market. However, for the proposal to truly deliver and 
become a successful framework, we encourage co-legislators to ensure it reflects the entire threat landscape; that it 
does not unintentionally create new security vulnerabilities; and that it fully reflects the best security practices available 
today. To strengthen the proposal, we’ve identified certain areas we believe should be addressed during the co-decision 
legislative scrutiny process, including those highlighted below. 
Essential Cybersecurity Requirements 
We encourage the co-legislators to build on the essential security requirements listed in Annex I. In particular, as the 
proposal seeks to improve the overall security standards for mobile products, there is merit in looking at the entire threat 
landscape. Whilst vulnerabilities are a large source of security issues, increasingly attackers target users directly to 
compromise the security of their products, systems and data. Attackers use sophisticated phishing or social engineering 
techniques, e.g. clicking on links or downloading unvetted software, as a means to gain access to users’ devices. They 
frequently do this not by exploiting a vulnerability, but by tricking users into revealing passwords and other credentials 
and using them as means to steal data, ransom or attack networks. The risks to enterprises is heightened as employers 
are increasingly adopting Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) policies that encourage employees to use their personal 
devices on corporate networks. This provides bad actors a direct route into corporate networks, which has led to an 
increase in threats targeting mobile devices. Therefore, depending on an assessment of risk, manufacturers should 
design products - or have policies in place - to mitigate against such forms of attacks, including controls or vetting 
processes on how third party code can access key functionalities of a device. 
Manufacturer Vulnerability Disclosures 
Section 1 of Article 11 requires manufacturers to report any “actively exploited vulnerability” to ENISA within 24 hours. 
This requirement poses a serious security risk because it encompasses reporting vulnerabilities that have not been 
patched or remedied. More specifically, requiring the disclosure of information on active vulnerabilities risks information 
leaking, especially if the purpose of reporting to ENISA is that ENISA will disclose the information to others. When 
information leaks, malicious attackers could weaponize the vulnerabilities against users in hours. Users will then be left

Manufacturer Incident Disclosures 
Section 2 of Article 11 requires manufacturers to report “any incident having impact on the security of the product with 
digital elements” within 24 hours. The 24 hour notification period is very short and fails to consider that cyberattacks are 
often complex and require the investigation of experts to confirm and assess the impact of the incident, which must take 
priority. Furthermore obligation  does not consider the significance or the severity of an incident which would lead to the 
disclosure of numerous immaterial incidents, making it harder for cybersecurity practitioners and government authorities 
to focus on critical security issues. We strongly recommend policy makers to reconsider this approach and align with 
other legal frameworks such as the GDPR and NIS 2.

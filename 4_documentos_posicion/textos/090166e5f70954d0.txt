 
NORWEGIAN REFUGEE COUNCIL (NRC) FEEDBACK ON THE 
PROPOSAL FOR A DIRECTIVE ON EU RESTRICTIVE 
MEASURES 
• NRC strongly welcomes the inclusion of Article 3 ( 6) within the proposal, which excludes 
humanitarian aid from the risk of criminalization under the new Directive. The inclusion of 
the humanitarian carveout in the operative paragraphs, and not only in the memorandum 
is particularly commended as it would give direct effect to the exemption. 
• NRC and other humanitarian actors have document ed extensive evidence which shows 
how sanctions can negatively impact our ability to implement principl ed humanitarian 
assistance. Sanctions can restrict humanitarian’s ability to transfer funds to certain 
contexts, impact the types of programmes we can provide, cause operational delays, and 
create challenges in procuring certain goods. They can also impact our abilit y to respond 
based on needs alone, as fear of violating sanctions regimes is one of the reasons that 
donors include requirements for humanitarian partners to vet final beneficiaries of 
humanitarian assistance. 
• Humanitarian exemptions remain the most efficient way to protect principled humanitarian 
assistance from the unintended negative consequences of restrictive measures, as well as 
ensure compliance with international law, including international humanitarian (IHL). The 
inclusion of humanitarian carveout as proposed in Article 3 (6) is in line with several other 
EU legislations, including – but not limited to: 
o Recital 38 of the Directive 2017/541 of 15 March 2017 on Combating Terrorism. 
o Article 6 a) §1 of the Regulation 36/2012 concerning Restrictive Measures in view 
of the Situation in Syria as amended by Council Regulation 2016/2137 of 6 
December 2016. 
• It is crucial for the EU to swiftly and effectively implement the full operationalization of UN 
Security Council (UNSC)  Resolution 2664 from 9 December 2022 ( S/RES/2664(2022)1 
by which the UNSC adopted a standing humanitarian exemption to nearly all its asset 
freeze sanctions regimes.  As a result, impartial humanitarian organisations and their 
partners covered by this exemption can transfer funds, purchase resources, and provide 
services when conducting humanitarian activities to support people in need without facing 
penalties or criminal liability for violating sanctions regimes. 
• While UNSC 2664 is only immediately for UN sanctions regimes, it is important that other 
sanctioning bodies, such as the EU align their autonomous sanctions with the new UN 
humanitarian exemption  – therefore demonstrating unequivocal commitment to  
international law, including IHL, as well as en hancing coherence and consistency with

• In order to ensure that principled humanitarian is protected from possibl e risk of 
criminalization,  the language of the proposed directive ought to be strengthened to ensure 
the conformity of EU sanctions legal frameworks with International Humanitarian Law (IHL) 
and reflect the spirit and the letter of UNSC 2664 – as suggested below: 
 
Recital 9 
Paragraph 9 of the recital should be amended in the following way: 
‘’Moreover, activities which concern the provision of goods and services of daily use for the 
personal use of designated natural persons, such as food and healthcare products and services, 
or of petty cash, where it is clearly limited to fulfilling the basic human needs of such persons and 
their dependent family members are excluded from criminalisation. The failure to report such 
activities are also excluded from criminalisation. In addition, and in line with Art icle 1 of UNSCR 
2664, the delivery of humanitarian aid assistance or activities that support basic human needs to 
persons in need is excluded from criminalisation. Such humanitarian assistance must be provided 
strictly in accordance with international humanitar ian law and can notably consist of food and 
nutrition, shelter, health care, water, and sanitation. Furthermore, in implementing this Directive, 
Member States should take into account that International Humanitarian Law, the law of armed 
conflict, req uires that restrictive measures should not prevent the delivery of humanitarian 
assistance in line with principles of impartiality, humanity, neutrality, and independence.’’ 
Article 3 (6) 
Article 3.6 should be amended in the following way: 
‘’Paragraphs 1, 2 and 3 shall not apply to: 
- the provision of goods or services of daily use for the personal use of designated natural 
persons, such as food and healthcare products and services, or of petty cash, where  it is 
clearly limited to fulfilling the basic human needs of such persons and their dependent 
family members, 
- to the failure to report such activities; 
- humanitarian aid provided for persons in need  assistance or activities that support basic 
human needs.’’ 
Rationale 
• The proposed revision aligns with language of UNSC 2664 which states that ‘the provision, 
processing or payment of funds, other financial assets, or economic resources, or the provision 
of goods and services necessary to ensure the timely delivery of humanitarian assistance or to 
support other activities that support basic human needs (…) are permitted’.  
• UNSCR 2664 covers  activities necessary to ensure  “the timely delivery of humanitarian 
assistance or to support other activities that support basic human needs” but does not define

these terms. There exists no comprehensive list of a ctivities that constitutes ‘humanitarian 
assistance’, as the drafters of the Geneva Conventions considered it difficult to anticipate the 
humanitarian needs that might arise. Despite typically covering a range of activities that seek 
to restore the life and dignity for people in distress – humanitarian activities can differ 
significantly depending on the context. 
• It is essential that any interpretation of the sc ope activities covered by the exemption is 
expansive and reflects the reality of modern humanitarian wor k and is not restrictive or 
limitative. Humanitarian assistance and humanitarian activities involve responding to complex, 
protracted crises, often caused by multiple interlocking factors including natural disasters, 
extreme weather conditions and/or conflict. As such responding to the humanitarian and basic 
needs of a population will require a broad range of interventions, encompassing immediate 
life-saving support  activities to increase  resilience to ongoing shocks , and peacebuilding. 
Furthermore, owing to the extreme pressure put on humanitarian budgets of all major donors, 
including the E U, it is essential that the interpretation of what constitutes humanitarian 
assistance is not limited to the source of the funding. 
• In this r egard, we would point to the definition used by the Office of Foreign Assets Control 
(OFAC) in its humanitarian general licences4 as a good example and suggests that the EC – in 
its further guidance - adopts an inclusive approach to the concept of humanitarian assistance 
and basic needs, ideally modelled on the definition used by OFAC.

              Introduction The Federation of the European Sporting Goods Industry (FESI) strongly welcomes the European Commission’s recent proposals to review the current EU framework on industrial designs1 to help better protect industrial designs across the EU and make them “quicker, cheaper and more predictable”.   The sporting goods industry is an innovative and IPR-intensive industry2 which constitutes 0.4% of all enterprises and 0.3% of all persons employed, turnover and value added of the total EU economy3. Designs are essential in the sporting goods industry as it helps bring style to technology and can encourage people to participate in sport. Sporting goods’ companies also invest a large amount of money and expertise in developing fresh and innovative designs that respond to changing consumer tastes. Registering their designs and obtaining an intellectual property right over them helps companies defend themselves against imitators and counterfeiters who stand to profit simply by copying, at the expense of other companies’ innovation and resource expenditures. For these reasons, FESI supports the Commission’s objective to harmonise, simplify and streamline the procedure for the EU-wide registration through the proposed revision of the Regulation on Community designs (hereafter “The Regulation”) and the Recast of the Directive on the legal protection of designs (hereafter “The Directive”), and would like to make some comments that we think should be taken into consideration in view of the future EU discussions on these two proposals.  1. Positive changes  a. Definitions (Regulation Article 3 – Directive Article 2) FESI welcomes the new broader definitions of “Product” and “Design” which will help take account of the technological innovations and the evolution of the digital world. In particular, FESI strongly supports the fact that “interior environments” and “graphical user interfaces” are now expressly included in the definition of “product” (article 3§2 of the Regulation). The definition of “individual character” is also more flexible with the removal of the term “clearly”.    1 Proposal for a Regulation on Community designs (COM(2022)666) AND Proposal for a Directive on the legal protection of designs (recast - COM(2022)667) 2 EPO and EUIPO (2019), IPR-intensive industries and economic performance in the European Union 3 KMU Forschung Austria and VVA (2018), Study for EASME: Improve economic and policy knowledge in the field of sport related industries with particular focus on sporting goods sector

b. Scope of rights conferred by a registered design (Regulation Article 19 – Directive Article 16) FESI strongly welcomes the fact that rights holders will now be entitled to enforce design rights against goods that have not been released into free circulation in the EU (goods that are in transit, or being stored at a warehouse, or transshipped in a port (article 19§3 of the Regulation)). Additionally, it is also positive to note that the new proposals include a protection against unauthorized 3D printing, which will help right holders to more effectively address the increasing challenges brought by the development of 3D printing technologies (article 19 §1 (d) of the Regulation).  c. Grounds for invalidity (Regulation Article 25 – Directive Article 14) Regarding the situations where an EU design may be declared invalid, FESI supports that prior user rights are now strengthened for good faith entities. Taking the example of sporting goods companies, this new provision would be very beneficial if a third-party bad actor files a registration on a leaked product of our industry. d. Principle of cumulation (Regulation Article 96(2) – Directive Article 23) First of all, FESI welcomes the clarification made regarding the interlinks between design and copyright protection, and the fact that it is now possible to have both copyright and design registration on the same product. These are distinct bodies of IP rights and it makes sense to allow for both forms of protection. e. Multiple applications (Regulation Article 37 – Directive Article 27) FESI welcomes the abolition of the so-called “unity of class” principle, which now gives applicants the ability to combine more than one design in the same application. Such mechanism will reduce administrative burden and save time and money for applicants. Additionally, FESI would recommend introducing a “Continuation of application” option, which would help claim benefit of earlier filing date and cross-references to other applications. To complete this mechanism, FESI would recommend introducing the option of an “Appendix”. The Appendix can include any number and type of drawings (e.g., 3D drawings, photographs, etc.), as well as any description. However, the Appendix will not be treated as part of the disclosure, and its main use can be to add to the application information that was inadvertently omitted. The drawings in the Appendix would provide support for filing continuation applications on the alternative designs. The drawings in the Appendix can sometimes be used to support necessary amendments to the formal drawings of your original application.   2. Elements of concern  a. Fees (Regulation Annex I) While FESI acknowledges the objective of simplifying the fees’ system to make it more accessible to individual designers and SMEs, we fear that the proposed changes to the fee structure for filings and renewals might eventually result in unjustified higher costs for large entities. For example, moving to a simplified structure of a flat fee per design would cost more on average for large entities to procure EU design registrations. Additionally, the total duration of protection of a registered EU design can be up to 25 years and requires renewal fees to be paid every 5 years. However, since the EUIPO plans to materially increase renewals at years 15 and 20 and considering that some companies currently automatically renew their design registrations in Europe, the new fee structure would result in those companies paying significantly more to

Design protection through registration only (Directive Article 3) The actual Designs Directive4 allows Member States to provide design protection also in unregistered form. In the current review proposal5, the Commission proposes to remove that discretion by limiting design protection to registered protection only. FESI is concerned about this removal and would like to recommend keeping the “Unregistred design protection”. Indeed, the current provision grants rights owners a protection period of three years from the date when the design was first made available to the public in the territory of the European Union, without any requirement of registration or payment fee. If the design has been published and rights owners don’t meet the “grace period” requirements, because the business is moving too fast, they can still benefit from the Unregistered Community Design (UCD) right which they can use against infringers in case the designs are copied. Such a protection is highly useful for rightsholders when they need protection for a short time (eg. only for 3 years).  c. Repair Clause/Spare Parts (Regulation Article 20(a) – Directive Article 19) While actual spare parts and repair of footwear or apparel is not a significant risk that we see presently in the sporting goods’ sector, we fear that infringers could however take advantage of the new clause and defeat the purpose of the design statute by shipping in unassembled parts under the guise of “spare parts” and then assemble counterfeit or knock-off goods in-country. This could defeat the purpose of partial design protection, which is currently a robust way to enforce against infringers who choose to copy core innovative and protectable design elements of certain products, but not always the entire product. This could be even more problematic for certain categories of sporting goods, such as helmets, bikes, gym equipment or eyewear for example, with high risks for counterfeit spare parts to fail to comply with safety standards and endanger the health and safety of European consumers.  To avoid this, we would therefore recommend clarifying the provision to make sure it only applies to crash parts/parts that are required to be replaced for functionality of the overall product or to be limited to automobiles/motorcycles as we understand it was the initial purpose as stated in the Commission’s inception impact assessment6.  d. Limitation of the rights conferred by an EU design (Regulation Article 20 – Directive Article 18) Regarding the proposed extension of the list of limitations enshrined in article 20 of the Regulation, it seems that the notions of “experimental purpose” (§1 a) and “comment, critique and parody” (§1 e), have been added in an attempt to imitate the US law on copyright in order to create a fair use exception in the design context. As noted above, design rights and copyrights (and even trademark rights) are distinct forms of IP protection that follow different requirements, which are themselves embedded into various EU laws and which courts analyze differently in making determinations of validity and infringement.  For this reason, we do not see a benefit to additional efforts to harmonise or draw parallels between these forms of protection.  In addition, it is important to highlight that the US law in this copyright area in particular is quite complex and often difficult to apply even in a copyright context. Design notice (Regulation Article 26 (a) – Directive Article 24) On the introduction of the “design notice”, FESI doesn’t think the provision is necessary as currently most rightsholders do not even use the “TM” or “R” symbols because of possible backfire risk. While we acknowledge the fact that the provision remains optional and not mandatory, we are concerned that this situation could lead to an unclear "notice vs. no notice" situation, which could further complicate enforcement procedures. Therefore, for reasons of legal clarity and practicality, we believe that this provision should be removed from the proposals. If it were to be maintained, we would recommend keeping it optional, as currently proposed by the Commission, so that rightsholders are not legally forced to use it.   f. New Locarno classification FESI believes that in parallel to the revision process of the EU Design framework, and in line with the objective to update and adapt design protection to the new digital environment, a new description of "Digital Representation of a Product" / "NFT" should be added to the Locarno Class no 14-04 (GUI)7 as there is currently no classification as such in all the Locarno classes.

In October 2022, the European Commission published a proposal for the revision of the Urban Wastewater
Treatment Directive. The revision contributes to the European Zero Pollution Ambition and the objectives
from the Green Deal. Vewin sees the revision as a good step towards a desired better integration of various
European directives such as the Water Framework Directive (WFD), the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED),
and the Drinking Water Directive (DWR).
Micro-pollutants enter sewer systems and, after partial treatment at wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs),
discharge to surface water. Consequently, concentrations of a cocktail of pharmaceutical residues, PFAS and
other emerging substances are almost everywhere in our surface waters. Hence, these substances impact
drinking water resources and can threaten the production of drinking water. The impact of the pollution is
now also visible in groundwater sources from which drinking water is made because pollution in surface water
eventually reaches groundwater.
Vewin has long advocated for a better approach to pharmaceuticals, more monitoring, and stricter rules for
indirect industrial discharges to sewer systems, particularly when discharges from WWTPs take place in
vulnerable areas that are directly or indirectly a source of drinking water. Conceding that several points are
reflected in the Commission's revision proposal, Vewin sees the present proposal as an improvement on the
current directive.
Quaternary purification
The Commission proposes that WWTPs should add quaternary (4th stage) treatment to purify
pharmaceuticals and cosmetic products from effluent. Vewin endorses the premise of the Commission's
proposal that the 'polluter pays'. The Dutch drinking water sector is positive about this additional treatment
step because it means a better protection of drinking water sources against micro-pollutants. Vewin advocates
the following:
EU Revision Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive
5 december 2022
Ensure that WWTPs discharging to surface waters used as a source of drinking water are prioritized for
quaternary treatment installation and updating, and that this treatment is installed by 31-12-2035.
Currently, the Commission proposes a minimum percentage of removal of 80% for quaternary treatment.
However, a minimum percentage does not lead to desired results. When the concentration of a particular
substance in water is high, an 80% relative standard is not enough to reduce harmful effects on the
environment and drinking water resources. Therefore, absolute standards are more effective.

The Commission proposes in Article 14 that all non-domestic discharges to sewer systems must be licensed
and reviewed when they pose a threat to drinking water resources. In addition, member states must take as
many measures as possible to stop these discharges of substances when they pose a threat to the drinking
water supply. Vewin is positive about this article. However, in light of coordinating the Urban Wastewater
Treatment Directive, the WFD and the DWR, Vewin sees some areas for improvement:
Ensure that permits of discharges to sewage systems can be revoked, rather than revised, when the
receiving waters are used as a source of drinking water;
Involve WWTP operators in the permitting process for indirect discharges to WWTPs;
Guarantee that these permits will not negatively affect drinking water sources;
Ensure that Substances of Very High Concern and Priority Substances are included as substances
where permits of discharges to sewage systems may be revoked;
Add a new article prohibiting all non-domestic discharges of PFAS to sewer systems.
Pharmaceutical residues, pesticides and substances released from (non-)household discharges are a major
risk to the sources of the public drinking water supply. Dutch drinking water companies monitor hundreds of
these substances in their sources and incur high costs for monitoring and treatment. The comprehensive
monitoring plans described in the revision provide more insight and guidance to reduce the emissions of
these substances to the environment and sources of drinking water.
The revision suggests that monitoring results should be shared with the appropriate public health
authorities. Because drinking water companies are too often surprised by foreign substances at drinking
water intake points, Vewin proposes the following:
In order to gain more insight into the harmful substances in the environment, it is important to include the
Annex of the revised List of Priority Substances Directive in Article 20(3).
Ensure that information from surveillance and monitoring is publicly available and shared directly with
drinking water companies.
Include the substances in the Annex of the revised Priority Substances Directive in the list of
substances that member states must monitor at urban wastewater treatment plants.

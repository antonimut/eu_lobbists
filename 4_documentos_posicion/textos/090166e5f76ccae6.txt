Why the EU should add biobased plastics content targets in the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Regulation  
 
Almost 40% of European plastics consumption comes from packaging, but currently, more than 
98% of the European plastics production derives from fossil fuels. 
Braskem therefore calls on the European Parliament and Member States to include biobased 
content targets for plastics, alongside recycled content mandates, in the Packaging and Packaging 
Waste Regulation. It is essential that these biomass-based plastics are drop-in solutions, meaning 
they fit into traditional plastic packaging value chains and are fully recyclable within existing 
recycling streams1. Adding such targets would actively promote the use of, and investments into, 
sustainable renewable sources.  
This would support the plastic packaging sector ’s shift away from its dependence on virgin fossil 
resources, a vital step towards achieving climate neutrality. The targets should be compliant with 
stringent sustainability criteria - including the protection of biodiversity and ecosystems, negating 
inadvertent effects of land use change, and protecting human rights – to ensure the development 
of biobased materials are sustainable in the long term, as also stated in the EU policy framework 
on biobased, biodegradable and compostable plastics.  
The EU has set as its objective to become a climate neutral circular economy and the European 
Commission has set an ambitious objective to have at least 20% of the carbon used in the chemical 
and plastic products come from sustainable non-fossil sources by 20302. We firmly believe that 
incentivising biobased products that demonstrate a reduced carbon footprint, considering their 
carbon storage potential when compared to fossil-based equivalents, is an important solution to 
help achieve a carbon neutral circular economy (please see Annex I for the benefits of bio -based 
plastics). 
Therefore, Braskem calls for a 10% stand -alone target for biobased plastic content in the 
Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation , complementing the existing recycled content and 
reuse targets, to be set for 2030, while more ambitious targets should be required for 20403.  
 
The rationale behind setting biobased content targets 
 
1. Biobased plastic targets will have  a positive climate impact by reducing the carbon 
footprint of packaging, due to their carbon removal potential 4,5. 
2. Biobased raw materials are needed to complement recycled feedstocks by covering 
unavoidable material losses in production, collecting and recycling6,7.  
3. Biobased feedstocks ( e.g. biofuels, such as bioethanol and bionaphtha), provide an 
immediate alternative to the use of fossil resources , and drop-in biobased plastics 
manufacture at reduced infrastructure  costs since downstream investment in to 
polymerization and conversion aren’t needed.

Annex I – Benefits of biobased plastics 
 
Responsibly sourced  biobased plastics with a lower environmental footprint that can be proven 
through LCA analysis, present significant advantages that we highlight here. 
1. Biobased plastics play a key role in the transition to a circular economy, ensuring the 
material stays in the loop without compromising its quality. 
o Biobased plastics makes reference to the feedstock  of the plastics, i.e. biomass,  and 
should not be confused with biodegradable or compostable plastics. 
o Biobased plastics have the same properties and utility as existing fossil-based plastics, 
as they have identical chemical structures.  
o As an example, Braskem’s drop in biobased plastics in its I'm Green™ portfolio are fully 
recyclable via the mechanical route 8, making them low -carbon, high -quality and 
circular.  
 
2. Enabling sustainable value chains, while contributing to rural development by stimulating 
local bio-economies. 
o Biobased feedstock can present a significant sustainability advantage throughout the 
value chain. 
o Biobased plastics stimulate the creation of jobs, often in rural areas, especially by 
increasing the role of primary producers in local bio-economies. 
o To be accepted, biobased feedstock must be cultivated and processed following the 
principles of sustainable development, meaning preserving high biodiversity areas, 
avoiding deforestation, keeping fertilisers and pesticides use to a minimum - thus 
contributing to the preservation of biodiversity  - optimising biomass for food and 
renewable energy in parallel9. 
 
3. A shift to renewable feedstock would reduce the dependency on fossil fuels and support the 
transition to a carbon neutral economy.  
o Renewable materials can be produced globally, and their production can be tailored 
regionally. Therefore, this ensures indep endence from finite, unevenly distributed 
resources, while creating a more resilient industry with a variety of supply chains.  
o Biobased plastics’ carbon footprint can be significantly lower than fossil-based plastics, 
given the carbon removal capacity during its life cycle. Data has shown that in many 
cases, the carbon footprint could even be negative, as more carbon is captured than 
emitted. 
o In fact, if 10% of plastics switch to biobased feedstock, the consumption of 4,4Mt of 
fossil feedstock and the emission of up to 6,2Mt of CO 2-eq would be avoided , whilst 
using only 0,4% of the land available for agriculture10

4. Targets for biobased content would support the transition to a circular and resource efficient 
economy.  
o As mentioned, drop-in biobased plastics are fully recyclable, thus contributing to the 
EU’s objective for all packaging to be recyclable. 
o Biobased feedstocks are usually produced alongside food products like sugar and 
maize. This enables the agricultural sector to produce more with less. As an example, 
sugar mills are evolving to biorefineries that produce sugar, ethanol, electricity, biogas 
and compost that helps fertilizing their own plantations, maximising the feedstocks ’ 
potential and improving the resilience of the sector. 
o As mentioned, a lmost 40% of the 52,4Mt of virgin plastics consumption in Europe 
comes from packaging, but currently, more than 97% of this  (50,1Mt) derives from 
fossil fuels. This scenario could be significantly changed if the EU were to follow the 
cascading principle 11 and acknowledge that  biofuels add more value to society if 
treated as a valuable renewable feedstock for the materials market.

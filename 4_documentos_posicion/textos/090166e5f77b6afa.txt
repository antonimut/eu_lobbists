In December 2022, the European Commission (DG GROW) finally released its long -anticipated 
proposal for Euro 7 Emissions Regulation . For the first time, the proposal combines regulation s 
for both cars and vans, and heavy-duty vehicles, into a single regulatory proposal. In its Inception 
Impact Assessment, and the Impact Assessment of the Proposal, the Commission identified the 
following issues to be addressed by the proposed regulation: 
• Reduce complexity of the current standards, curb  administrative costs, and facilitate efficient 
implementation. 
• Update list of pollutants, and limits, to reflect current concerns over air-quality 
• Ensure emissions limits are complied with throughout the life of the vehicle, under all conditions 
of use.  
• Deliver reductions in air pollutants beyond what can be achieved by the 100% CO 2 reduction 
target for cars and vans, and increasing shares of zero and low emissions, and Euro VI E heavy-
duty vehicles. 
Westport Fuel Systems (Westport) is pleased to offer the following opinion points relating to the 
Commission Proposal for Euro 7: 
 
1. Implementation Dates and Implementing Acts 
The proposed implementation dates are: 
• Passenger Cars & Vans - July 1 2025 
• Heavy Duty Vehicles - July 1 2027 
These dates represent very ambitious timelines for technology and product readiness, and in 
order for these implementation dates to remain at all feasible the proposal must proceed through 
co-decision without material change. 
Westport can support the ambition, particularly given the pending 100% CO2 reduction target for 
new cars & vans in 2035. The introduction of Euro 7 alongside the proposed phase out of non-
zero emission light duty vehicles puts a significant business risk against the investments needed 
to comply with Euro 7. This is a serious consideration for higher volume petrol and diesel products 
(including hybrids), and an even greater business challenge for lower mar ket volume products 
such as gaseous fuelled vehicles.

However, the feasibility of maintaining these implementation dates is heavily contingent on the 
rapid development of all Implementing Acts (IAs) needed to support the Commission proposal. 
There are numerous IAs outstanding that are required to fully define all the testing, measurement, 
and compliance requirements for Euro 7. The Advisory Group on Vehicle Emissions Standards 
(AGVES) has a work plan to develop and confirm these details, and completion of t his work, on 
time, is a paramount need. 
Within the Commission proposal there are several elements that are beyond the control of the 
OEMs and Tier 1 system suppliers, for example the supporting digital infrastructure that is needed 
to complete the continuous On Board Monitoring (OBM) elements. These elements, coupled with 
the requirement to verify new subsystems, such as tyres,  or development of yet to be proven 
sensors, create a degree of uncertainty that an OEM could comply with all the anticipated 
requirements on time. There should be consideration to allow OEMs to type approve new vehicles, 
at Euro 7 emissions levels, earlier than the proposed implementation dates if these secondary 
elements are the cause of any potential type approval delays. 
 
2. Emission Limits 
Through extensive industry and academia collabor ation, coordinated through AGVES  and the 
CLOVE consortium, the Commission has proposed comprehensive new pollutant limits for both 
Light Duty and Heavy Duty vehicles.  
It is pertinent to point out that while some initial public reactions have claimed that the limits are 
too lenient, in reality the limits proposed are substantially more stringent than Euro 6/VI. The 
proposed limits must be assessed within the context of significantly extended test conditions, the 
absence of conformity factors, extended durability and lifetime requirements, all coupled with 
continuous monitoring. 
Westport provided the CLOVE consortium a number o f data-centric reports, presentations and 
analyses, highlighting the limit of t echnology for some pollutants specific to gaseous fuelled 
vehicles, in particular heavy-duty vehicles. All the analyses provided illustrated the performance, 
and trade -offs under test conditions aligned to Euro 6/VI , particularly trade -offs affecting 
efficiency and CO 2 emissions. The emissions limits proposed by the Commission, especially for 
CH4 and NMOG, when coupled with the widely expanded compliance conditions of Euro 7 are at 
the extreme of what can be conceived for gaseous fuelled vehicles. The trade-offs identified at 
Euro 6/VI will likely be exacerbated under the Euro 7 test regimes and limits. It is imperative for 
the industry that legislators resist the pressure to tighten the proposed limits, and that the limits 
stand as proposed through co-decision.

3. HDV CH4 Limits 
Westport would respectfully ask again for the consideration of revised HDV CH4 limits within Euro 
7. 
 
Under the current limits in Euro VI (CH4 500mg/kWh), tailpipe CH4 emissions from current state-
of-the-art natural gas engines imply ve ry high combustion efficiencies, in excess of 99.8%.  
Continuing to reduce methane emissions remains a goal of the gas vehicle industry, as part of an 
overarching drive to reduce the total GHG profile of gas trucks.  
 
Achieving the CH4 targets proposed in Euro 7 will likely require some undesired tra de-offs with 
thermal efficiency, CO2 and total tailpipe GHG,  an outcome that is contradictory to the goal of 
reducing total GHG emissions. 
 
During engagement with AGVES and CLOVE, we proposed (with data support) the option of 
adding CH4 and N2O into a combined GHG limit, in order to provide flexibility for advanced gas 
engines to meet both limits without compromising CO2 and overall GHG. Such an approach would 
allow technology developers and vehicle manufacturers to optimally trade-off CO2 and CH4, taking 
consideration for total GHG, fuel efficiency and fuel costs.  Such a proposal could be envisaged

4. Linkage to Respective CO2 Regulations 
For passenger cars and vans, Euro 7 must be viewed through the same lens as the CO2 regulations 
that call for a 100% CO 2 reduction by 2035. As mentioned in point 1, the implication is one of 
decreasing volumes, through to a phase out, of new vehicles at Euro 7 emissions within 10 years 
of the propo sed implementation date of 2025, bringing significant consequences for R&D and 
product development priorities. 
The revised CO2 regulations for HDVs have yet to be proposed. Within the Impact Assessment, a 
HDV powertrain technology road map is presented as the basis for evaluating the effectiveness 
of the Euro 7 proposal: 
 
This roadmap implies a considerable market share for non-ZEV technologies (diesel conventional, 
hybrid, and gas) beyond 2040. For consistency of regulations, and cost effectiveness of 
regulations, it is important that the anticipated proposal for HDV CO 2 regulations is aligned with 
the assumptions used in developing the Euro 7 proposal. 
 
5. Cost Effectiveness 
Within the Impact Assessment , benefit-cost ratio analysi s has been presented for each of the 
light duty and heavy-duty market segments, resulting in very cost -effective conclusions in both 
cases. However, it appears that much of the “benefit” of the Eur o 7 proposal is actually derived 
from two factors: 
• The obsolescence/ageing out of older, pre-Euro 6/VI vehicles 
• The phase in of zero emission vehicles, driven by the vehicle CO2 regulations.

As such, any economic evaluation of Euro 7 should apply caution that the socio-economic benefits 
of Euro 7 have not been overstated, and the costs underestimated (accounting for the ZEV 
component).  
 
6. Type Approval for Hydrogen Dual Fuel Engines 
There is an urgent need for Type Approval routes for low carbon, hydrogen dual-fuel internal 
combustion engines. The Commission has moved forward with proposals to UNECE for the 
necessary regulatory revisions (primarily R49) to enable type approval for mono -fuel hydrogen 
engines. This now needs to be complemented with simultaneous efforts to encapsulate dual-fuel 
hydrogen engines within the Type Approval regulatory frameworks, and within the scope of Euro 
VI and Euro 7 HDV emissions regulations. 
 
In conclusion, after a number of delays, industry now needs certainty 
over the scope of Euro 7. The proposed emission limits, and the extent of 
use conditions over which compliance must be controlled, mean that Euro 
7 as proposed by the Commission represents a significant challenge for 
the OEMs and technology suppliers. The emphasis should be on adding 
the clarity  and final definitions via the Implementing A cts, and 
proceeding through co -decision as quickly as possible without the 
temptation to add further stringency and complexity beyond the 
challenging targets set by the Commission proposal.

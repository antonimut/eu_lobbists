ACEA comments to the public consultation on the Commission proposal for Euro 7 and the 
supporting documents 
February 2023 
 
The European manufacturers of cars, vans and heavy-duty vehicles, represented by ACEA, are 
fully committed to providing the right vehicles to swiftly move Europe’s mobility and transport sector 
to climate neutrality and to minimise the impact of our sector on air pollution. 
ACEA’s members support the aim to improve air quality in Europe. In line with the European 
Commission’s Green Deal ambition, the focus of our industry is on decarbonisation. By swiftly 
moving Europe’s transport and mobility sector to climate neutrality and zero-emission vehicles, 
exhaust pollutant emissions will disappear fast, positively benefiting air quality. Therefore, all efforts 
must be focused on decarbonisation, an accelerated uptake of zero-emission vehicles and the 
rapid phase-out of pre-Euro 6/VI vehicles. The new CO2 regulation for cars and vans and its 
intended phase-out of internal combustion engines will automatically lead to the elimination of the 
vast share of remaining exhaust pollutant emissions. The expected review of the CO2 regulation for 
heavy-duty vehicles, due for publication on 14 February 2023, will similarly aim to accelerate the 
move to zero-emission vehicles.  
Therefore, the Euro 7 proposal must not slow down the efforts of our industry to climate neutrality. 
Unfortunately, this Euro 7 proposal would impose a regulation that will divert valuable resources 
from the development and engineering of new zero-emission back to further development of 
vehicles with internal-combustion engines, together with a need for significant new testing facilities. 
At the same time, the benefit of this Euro 7 proposal towards reducing levels of key air pollutants 
such as exhaust NOx and particles is marginal, at best. 
European manufacturers are leading the transformation and transitioning fast to electrification to 
meet future CO2 targets - this will deliver zero-emission vehicles across all on-road sectors. The 
transition needs time, but it has started, and it is irreversible. Industry cannot afford to go back on 
its investment plans. 
For cars and vans, the investment needed to achieve this Euro 7 proposal to design, develop and 
produce vehicles also includes the building and commissioning of major new test facilities. The 
shorter the lead-time, to Euro 7, the higher the vehicle cost increase because of short-term limited 
supplier capacities. With this Euro 7 proposal, cheaper models of cars and vans will either face a 
steep increase in price or be phased out, leading to less choice for customers who will hold onto 
their older vehicles for longer. This would negatively impact air quality and should be carefully 
considered. 
The proposed date of this Euro 7 proposal for cars and vans is unrealistic and impossible to 
achieve as it does not provide sufficient lead-time to develop, engineer, test and type-approve all 
vehicle models and variants. Considering the Commission also proposes to update Euro 7 over 
several years, manufacturers will not be able to sell vehicles, leading to production stoppage, 
factory closures and job losses. 
For heavy-duty vehicles, this Euro 7 proposal moves from a sound Euro VI powertrain type-
approval approach to a completely new approach, which has no global similarity. The very 
stringent Euro 7 exhaust pollutant requirements forces the industry to shift capacity and investment 
for the transition to zero-emission vehicles away to a fading technology. The feasibility of achieving 
the proposed very stringent exhaust pollutant limits under the proposed boundary conditions is 
unknown at this time. This proposal is not a proportional evolution from Euro VI. It is a major 
revolution that will have severe consequences for Europe’s heavy-duty vehicle industry. 
The investment needed to achieve the Euro 7 proposal to design, develop and produce vehicles 
also includes the building and commissioning of major new test facilities have to be recovered 
by sales of new vehicles with internal combustion engines for a period of time far shorter than 
previous regulations have provided, and as sales volumes will undoubtably shrink. With this Euro 7 
proposal, heavy-duty vehicles will face steep increases in price or certain vehicles will be phased

Euro VI1 for heavy-duty vehicles is a very effective regulation for the reduction of exhaust pollutant 
emissions. Recent studies2 show that the proposed new Euro 7 regulation would deliver only 
marginal exhaust pollutant reduction benefits by 2030 and 2035, compared to natural fleet renewal 
with the latest Euro VI heavy-duty vehicles and the uptake of zero-emission vehicles. Accelerated 
fleet renewal will the latest Euro VI heavy-duty vehicles will deliver even bigger air quality benefits. 
The Euro 7 proposal does not support Europe’s manufacturers of trucks and buses in establishing 
and defending their leading role in the transition to climate neutrality. Arbitrarily choosing to stop 
having a separate heavy-duty engine and vehicle exhaust pollutant regulation like Euro VI (with a 
trivial justification of ‘simplification’), risks the EU losing its role in setting standards that many world 
markets follow (e.g. UNECE standards), affecting the enormous benefits brought by global 
‘harmonisation’, with the EU setting the pace. 
ACEA has positively engaged on Euro 7, and we made an ambitious proposal for Euro 7 for heavy-
duty vehicles in mid-20213. We see the Commission’s Euro 7 proposal for heavy-duty vehicles 
critically and we call for significant amendments of substance. We remain ready to engage with 
regulators to help frame a proportional Euro 7 regulation that does not divert from the efforts of our 
industry to decarbonise in these still challenging times. 
Issues in the Euro 7 proposal and supporting impact assessment that require comment: 
The Euro 7 impact assessment is flawed - it fails to take account of the environmental 
benefit of the latest Euro 6 and Euro VI steps: 
The impact assessment refers in many places to 2018 data and uses 2018 as the baseline year to 
determine the additional benefit of a Euro 7/VII regulation in terms of vehicle sector specific 
pollutant reductions. 
This falsely inflates the benefit of Euro 7/VII by ignoring the fact that major exhaust pollutant 
emission reducing steps in the current Euro 6d, and Euro VI regulation framework have applied to 
all new vehicles since 2018. 
If 2020 had been used as the baseline, it would be clear that Euro 7 for cars and vans has only 
marginal benefit by 2035 compared to on-going fleet renewal with the latest Euro 6 cars and vans 
and at the same time as the transition to zero emission cars and vans is accelerating. Slides 
presented by the Commission (e.g. at UNECE GRPE in January 2023) acknowledge the fact that 
the impact of the proposed Euro 7 for cars and vans is marginal. 
By no means does this justify increasing the severity of Euro 7 for cars and vans. The proposal is 
not a simple extension of Euro 6; the package is a very severe one (see comments below).

The Euro 7 impact assessment is flawed - it makes illogical assumptions on the rate of 
electrification of heavy-duty vehicles and fails to consider more beneficial options than 
another regulation on new vehicles: 
The impact assessment takes no account of the proposal the Commission is due to publish on 14 
February 2023 on future heavy-duty vehicle CO2 targets. The assumptions in the Euro 7 impact 
assessment for the rate of heavy-duty vehicle transition to zero emission vehicles - see impact 
assessment Part 1, Figure 7(b) - are rejected by ACEA because they are nowhere near what 
would be needed to achieve future heavy-duty vehicle CO2 fleet targets. Manufacturers would 
have to pay huge CO2 non-compliance penalties, as well as the massive investment in Euro VII 
and decarbonisation. 
In our opinion, the impact assessment for heavy-duty Euro VII must be done again, at the least 
when the CO2 proposal is adopted, or better when the co-legislators agree the heavy-duty vehicle 
CO2 proposal. This was done for cars and vans, and it is prejudicial to Europe’s heavy-duty vehicle 
industry if the impact of the heavy-duty CO2 proposal is not properly accounted for. In short, the 
flaws of the impact assessment falsely inflate the claimed benefit of Euro 7 for heavy-duty vehicles. 
New Euro 7 vehicles will not appear overnight and would take several years to make any impact in 
the EU fleet - at the same time as the fleet is electrified. Quickly improving air quality will be 
achieved by replacing the older vehicle fleet having the highest emissions. The impact assessment 
fails to consider an option of replacing older vehicles with the latest clean Euro 6/VI vehicles, in 
parallel to the arrival of more zero emission vehicles. Independent studies show this will have a 
much greater benefit than a Euro 7/VII regulation focusing only on new vehicles4. 
Combining light- and heavy-duty vehicles into one regulation is a mistake: 
At the start of Euro 7 stakeholder discussions, industry requested to maintain separate and distinct 
regulations for light-duty vehicles (cars and vans) and heavy-duty vehicles that would continue the 
well-founded appreciation of the very different approaches for the two vehicle segments. Such an 
approach would also maintain the important bridge that previous Euro regulations have brought to 
international regulations, where elements of Euro 6 and Euro VI are now helpfully included in major 
regional regulations (e.g. UNECE 1958 Regulations and China 6 and latest US EPA emission 
regulations). 
Unfortunately, the Commission has chosen to disregard advice from stakeholders that deal with 
the pollutant emission regulations on a day-by-day basis, and combine all vehicle classes into one 
proposal, justified by the aim of simplification. ACEA sees no simplification from this proposal, only 
additional burden for no obvious benefit and the claim in the impact assessment that type-approval 
costs will be massively reduced is false. Proposing the EU takes a different pathway for vehicle 
pollutant emission regulations at this stage of the transition to zero emission vehicles makes no 
sense. 
As of today, many regions follow regulations based on the Euro pollutant emission regulations. 
That is the case with the UNECE regulations to which around 64 Contracting Parties apply R83 
(car and van pollutant emissions) and around 60 apply R49 (truck pollutant emissions). As well as 
EU member states, other major markets using UNECE regulations are Russia, Japan, South 
Korea, and many other African and Asian countries. 
History has led to the Euro regulations being the basis of UNECE regulations under the 1958 
Agreement, but the Euro 7/VII proposal now changes the regulatory framework in a way in which 
other regions may likely see no need to follow, especially in terms of the high cost and low 
additional environmental benefit compared to Euro 6/VI, which are already in the UNECE 
regulations mentioned above. 
Euro 7/VII will bring no benefit of harmonisation because, even as Euro 7/VII was being proposed, 
North America adopted the EPA27 (model year 2027) rule for heavy-duty vehicles which actually 
includes some of the good parts of the on-road testing conditions from Euro VI (but EPA27 remains

The proposed Euro 7 date of 1 July 2025 for cars and vans is impossible to achieve: 
Irrespective of the substantial development work, type approval and testing capacity to address all 
production of cars and vans makes Euro 7 from 1 July 2025 impossible. The proposed date does 
not provide anywhere near sufficient lead time for all necessary technical development of internal 
combustion engine powertrain and new brake technologies. 
In many respects, this unrealistic date is already posing a distraction from crucial investment and 
engineering development in electrification. If maintained, manufacturers will not be able to sell 
vehicles, leading to production stoppage, factory closure and job losses. 
The Euro 7 proposal for cars and vans is not a simple extension of Euro 6, as the Commission 
claims. The proposed Euro 7 exhaust pollutant limits are the strictest of the Euro 6 petrol or diesel 
limits applied as fuel-neutral limits and they are tightened significantly because the undoubted 
variability of the on-road measurement instruments (accounted for in Euro 6) is now ignored, so 
manufacturers must set their engineering targets lower than in Euro 6. Additionally, the Euro 7 
proposal would significantly extend the on-road test conditions compared to Euro 6. All in all, the 
Euro 7 proposal is a significant strengthening beyond Euro 6 for cars and vans. 
The proposed Euro 7 date of 1 July 2030 for small volume manufactures is impossible to 
achieve: 
ACEA appreciates that the Euro 7 proposal recognizes the spread and specific nature of the 
business models of small volume manufacturers and the intention to take those into account. 
Recital (19) clearly states how emissions from vehicles sold by small volume manufacturers 
constitute an insignificant part of emissions in the Union, validating the negligible environmental 
role of those manufacturers. 
Therefore, ACEA requests that the Euro 7 Regulation should not be applicable to small volume 
manufacturers so they can concentrate their investments on technologies able to reduce CO2 
emissions. Recognizing the need to define a clear route for all the automotive industry players, we 
anyway understand the aim to address all new vehicles registered after a certain date. 
Under the proposed definition of small volume manufacturer, coherent with the one provided by the 
CO2 Regulation (i.e. a manufacturer whose registrations are fewer than 10000 new motor vehicles 
per calendar year in the Union), we advocate for a sustainable Euro 7 entry into force for small 
volume manufacturers as of 1 July 2035. 
Heavier vans need specific conditions, not treated like cars: 
Emission limits: 
Heavier vans cannot fulfil the same exhaust pollutant limits as cars due to the higher van weights 
and rolling resistance. This has been well understood over previous Euro steps. 
The Euro 7 proposal introduces a new category of less stringent exhaust pollutant limits applicable 
to vans with a power to weight ratio less than 35kW/t based on mass in running order. To be a real 
proposal the mass basis must be changed to gross vehicle mass or technically permissible 
maximum laden mass (ie. power based on a loaded van, not an empty van). If not there would be 
no vans in the European market that could possibly use these less stringent exhaust pollutant 
limits. Van manufacturers will therefore have no advantage from these particular limits unless 
vehicle performance is downgraded in relation to weight capacity. It is highly likely that customers 
would not purchase such downgraded vans.

In consequence, for Euro 7, practically all vans will need to meet the same exhaust pollutant limits 
as cars and that increase in burden is increased by the on-road (RDE) test conditions which give 
no latitude for vans compared to cars. 
ACEA therefore strongly recommended to retain the Euro 6 framework for vans with specific limits 
for the heavier vans (N1 Class III vehicles) that reflect distinctly higher driving resistance due to 
heavier loaded weights and frontal area of vans. 
At the very least, less stringent fuel-neutral exhaust pollutant limits for heavier vans must be based 
on the Euro 6 N1 class III emission limits. 
Application dates: 
Van product cycle are longer than cars so a common application date in Euro 7 would shorten the 
product cycle for Euro 6 vans. 
For heavier vans the extra step of one year for new approvals and one year for all registrations, 
after the dates for cars and which has always been in the Euro regulations, is needed for van 
manufacturers and the approval authorities. 
Van models built on similar platforms can bridge wide weight ranges across light-duty and 
heavy-duty regulations: 
In order to better optimise the handling of van and special vehicle platforms that extend across 
light- and heavy-duty weight ranges, Euro 7 should facilitate the type-approval of a heavier van in 
the same way as a lighter van by simply extending the proposed cut-off at 4 tonnes to 5 tonnes. 
The proposed Euro 7 date of 1 July 2027 for heavy-duty vehicles is practically impossible at 
this time: 
The proposed Euro 7 date of 1 July 2027 for heavy-duty vehicles is practically impossible at this 
time without knowledge of what actually must be done. The Euro 7 proposal is very complex, and 
the feasibility of the Euro 7 proposal for heavy-duty vehicles is not known at this time, ie. the main 
act plus all technical details, annexes, implementation guidelines etc. 
Over-arching view on lead-time need: 
For new type-approvals (new types), a minimum of three years lead time is needed for industry. 
This is not three years from when the Commission made its Euro 7 proposal, and it is not three 
years from when the co-legislators have decided the main act. Any implementing or delegated 
measures the Commission will bring later can add to the severity and burden. When industry 
knows all that, then a minimum of three years must apply. 
ACEA refers to the Commission’s own commitment to give sufficient industry lead-time to meet 
new regulations in GEAR2030 and CARS2020. 
For manufacturers and approval authorities to manage all type-approval work, one extra year after 
the new-types date must be given for all registrations (all types). This has been a well-established 
provision in all previous Euro regulations. 
The proposed on-road test conditions are not balanced or proportional: 
Cars and vans: 
The Euro 6 regulation, as a complete package, is state of the art because it delivers vehicles with 
extremely low exhaust pollutant emissions where it counts – on the road covering the vast majority 
of statistically possible on-road driving events and conditions. Despite this, the Euro 7 proposal 
aims to extend coverage to the last few percent, adding complexity and requiring new hardware 
without any perceivable benefit. All customers will drive in the areas covered by Euro 6, but all 
Euro 7 customers will pay for the significant engineering of new vehicles to address statistically 
irrelevant driving conditions. 
The Euro 7 proposal to impose extended testing under biased/worst-case conditions has to be 
prohibited because addressing these statistically irrelevant conditions will have practically no 
impact on air quality. Biased driving/worst case conditions will force additional technology onto all 
Euro 7 vehicles, from the smallest car to the largest van. As a result, the smaller low budget cars in

Europe would be threatened by a four-digit Euro cost increase, which could not be sustained and 
will lead to such models being withdrawn. 
The Euro 7 proposal fails to address “biased driving” or misuse, critical points to protect 
manufacturers from deliberate attempts to fail well-engineered compliant vehicles, but regulators 
also need a framework for regulatory certainty. Rarer driving conditions could be considered but 
not in a punitive way, for example, cars towing several tonnes of payload under dynamic driving 
cannot be seen as normal driving. 
ACEA is ready to explore a proportional extension of the Euro 6 on-road driving test conditions. 
Heavy-duty vehicles: 
The current Euro VI regulation, as a complete package, is state of the art because it delivers 
engines with extremely low exhaust pollutant emissions. Compliance of engines installed in 
vehicles is conducted on the road through in-service conformity assessment using portable 
emission measuring equipment (ISC-PEMS). The ISC-PEMS program has been refined over 
several steps in Euro VI and the on-road boundary conditions cover the range of different 
vocational and transportation heavy-duty vehicles. 
The Euro 7 proposal now seeks to move that well-functioning approach in Euro VI to real driving 
emissions (RDE), extending what exists in Euro 6 for cars and vans to heavy-duty vehicles under 
the justification of simplification. 
In several respects, ISC-PEMS may be similar to RDE but critically the proposed Euro 7 exhaust 
pollutant limits are a huge reduction of around 87% for NOx compared to Euro VI and over 90% for 
PN compared to Euro VI. In addition, under RDE conditions the undoubted variability of the on-
road measurement instruments (accounted for in Euro VI) is now included in the limit values. In 
consequence, exhaust pollutant engineering targets would need to be practically zero. The 
measurement of such levels will require on-road measurement system (PEMS) capability that is 
not known today, and it is not clear if such a major improvement in PEMS is coming to allow 
manufacturers to consider Euro 7 development work. 
The Euro VII proposal aims to extend on-road coverage to the very edge of possible driving 
boundaries, adding complexity and requiring new hardware without any perceivable benefit. Cold 
start emissions may be an issue for heavy-duty vehicles used in urban duty cycles requiring 
additional hardware solutions, but those urban vehicles may simply move earlier to electrification. 
However, the impact of cold start emissions for long-haul heavy-duty vehicles is less relevant. 
Conclusion for on-road test methods: 
Euro 7 should enforce on-road exhaust emission testing in statistically relevant conditions. The 
expansion to cover ‘any’ driving is prohibitive. 
Including non-exhaust particle emissions due to brake wear is correct – but only when the 
test procedure is comprehensive and accurate and the baseline for brake wear is 
understood: 
The proposed Euro 7 date for cars and vans of 1 July 2025 is not feasible considering the status of 
the test, the reliability of the measurement and the delivery of technical solutions to all new 
vehicles with ICE and/or ZEV. The proposed Euro 7 date for heavy-duty vehicles of 1 July 2027 is 
also not feasible considering the test method is an unknown. 
The Commission’s proposal for a revision of ambient air quality standards5 places a major focus on 
particle emissions. Exhaust particulate emissions from the latest new vehicles are now at very low 
levels with all new vehicles equipped with the latest particle filter technology. ACEA therefore 
agrees that road transport particle emissions from non-exhaust sources such as brakes will 
overtake exhaust particle emissions, so should now be regulated for all new vehicles, with or 
without internal combustion engines. 
However, the proposed whole vehicle brake wear limits for cars and vans should be based on an 
assessment of the issue using a properly developed test procedure addressing all types of

traditional friction brake systems and new regenerative braking systems of electrically chargeable 
vehicles. This test procedure is being addressed in UNECE, but it is still far away from being 
proven, especially the still wide scatter of results seen in inter-laboratory tests. The proposal is 
based only on a subjective review of available literature and fails to consider that it is the entire 
brake system that needs to be changed for all new vehicles, not just brake pad material. 
The current method has been developed primarily for cars. It must be recognised that vans have 
design constraints, especially the rear axle under the cargo bay, such that possible technical 
solutions for cars may not be suitable for vans. 
When the new test methodology is ready (it is a brake test on a bench of a single brake system), it 
should be the basis for a new inter-laboratory program to determine appropriate brake wear limits 
for cars and vans that accounts for all technical means of stopping vehicle motion, and the 
procedure for taking a single brake wear test into a whole vehicle brake wear limit over (at least) 4 
wheels (front axle and rear axle). A new vehicle monitoring and reporting phase can be considered 
to gather the data needed to determine new brake wear limits. 
Measures to address non-exhaust brake wear particle emissions for all new heavy-duty vehicles is 
supported, but only when all necessary and relevant test procedures are first ready to set the 
baseline for brake wear particle emissions. The test procedures being developed for cars and vans 
are not a suitable starting point for heavy-duty vehicles, which have several (non-friction, non-
wear) means of slowing motion of heavy-duty vehicles. 
For heavy-duty vehicles, further thought is required concerning what to be measured? Just the 
heavy-duty tractor unit or the complete heavy-duty vehicle including the semi-trailer? The fact that 
heavy-duty tractor units pull semi-trailers which can be 20 (or more) years old appears to limit 
options for complete heavy-duty vehicle brake wear limits. 
Including non-exhaust particle emissions due to tyre abrasion is correct – but address this 
to the tyre industry when the science of tyre abrasion is understood: 
The science of particles due to tyre abrasion are recognised by ACEA, but such measures must be 
addressed directly to the tyre industry, not in the Euro 7 proposal. The tyre industry is best placed 
to manage the critical interdependencies regarding, for example, tyre wet grip, noise, rolling 
resistance and wear. 
ACEA is surprised the Commission propose to continue with stepwise changes in Euro 7, 
in the last years of the internal combustion engine: 
The Commission’s intention to make a number of implementing or delegated regulations, leading 
to further steps in Euro 7, must be slimmed down to the absolute minimum. The multiple 
development and type-approval steps of Euro VI over the past eight years was a big problem for 
manufacturers, approval authorities and technical services and transport operators. It must not be 
repeated because it takes away development capacity and capital investment for electrification and 
steers it to a fading technology. 
The number of Euro 7 heavy-duty vehicles with internal combustion engines will decrease rapidly 
with an accelerated market uptake of zero-emission vehicles. There will be no environmental 
benefit to be gained by subjecting decreasing numbers of vehicles with internal combustion 
engines to further development steps. 
Euro 7 heavy-duty vehicles with internal combustion engines have marginal impact on improving 
air quality as their sales numbers diminish. The greatest impact on air quality is being delivered by 
accelerating fleet renewal of the old vehicle fleet with the latest Euro VI vehicles. Pre-Euro VI 
vehicles should be replaced by the latest Euro VI vehicles, or by zero-emission vehicles. 
On-Board Monitoring (OBM) as a tool to monitor vehicle compliance 24/7 is not a proven 
technology for industrial application. Anti-tampering and over the air data transmission 
(OTA) for a vehicle lifetime is impractical: 
Beyond type-approval, Euro VI already has in place successful measures to ensure vehicle 
compliance, for example conformity of production (COP), in-service conformity (ISC), on-board 
diagnostics (OBD) and fleet surveillance through additional on road (ISC-PEMS) tests. 
 
 8 
However, Euro 7 proposes to require on-board monitoring (OBM) of emissions at all times and for 
the lifetime of a vehicle. Such an aim depends on new exhaust sensors and these sensors are 
either not available or have limited capability and lifetime and their cost is unknown. 
The Commission aims to propose the measures later, so what is required from OBM is very much 
unclear. However, industry cannot accept further development into OBM after the initial Euro 7 
introduction because the phase-out of vehicles with internal combustion engines will be moving 
forwards. 
Measures to improve vehicle tamper-protection can be implemented but to do that and keep pace 
with new tampering methods that may appear over the lifetime of a vehicle which were not evident 
when a vehicle was designed is impossible. The same applies to over the air data transmission 
because vehicles introduced today cannot be expected to communicate via new data transmission 
standards that may appear several years after first registration. 
These need for these proposals requires further thought because it is another issue where the 
Commission will come with details later as the phase-out of the internal combustion engine has  
probably already been decided in the discussions on the Commission proposal for future heavy-
duty CO2 targets. 
Bodybuilders and users of approved engines are impacted by Article 9: 
Reconsider the Euro 7/VII proposal (ie. Article 9) because, as written, the well-functioning 
bodybuilding industry (with many SME’s) will be critically impacted by Euro 7 if their product 
portfolios must be restricted to remain within the exhaust pollutant data relating to the chassis cab 
or an engine (as a separate technical unit) to be installed in a heavy-duty vehicle. 
Need provisions to address Euro 6/VI vehicles in stock when Euro 7 would apply: 
End-of-series derogations should be facilitated through clear encouragement by the Commission to 
the member states. 
Stakeholder consultation: 
ACEA members were active participants to the AGVES stakeholder meetings from mid-2019 until 
April 2021, when engagement on the development of Euro 7 was abruptly stopped. 
The Commission has already started work on the implementing and delegated acts it believes are 
needed to complete Euro 7, despite the fact that the co-legislators have not given their opinions on 
which powers they will pass to the Commission. This seems rather presumptuous of the 
Commission and forces stakeholders to invest time and effort into issues that may not be needed. 
ACEA would suggest the co-legislators give their opinions and, on that basis, the Commission can 
start work according to a well-defined mandate, with a clear timescale to deliver the requested 
secondary legislations in good time before the mandatory dates of Euro 7 apply. Here, ACEA 
requests at least three years lead-time from when the Commission has delivered the secondary 
legislations. If the Commission fails to deliver on time, the mandatory dates should be 
compensated for the delay.

Commission Q&A on Euro 76: 
We welcome the fact that the Commission has recognised the Euro 7 proposal needs substantial 
clarification through the Q&A it recently published on the AGVES web-page, but industry needs 
correct and proportional legislation in law and not a Q&A that has no legal basis. Therefore, 
clarifications must be translated into a substantial set of amendments which the co-legislators 
should address. 
The amendments proposed by ACEA will, in many respects, deliver those clarifications into 
regulatory text but clarification alone is not sufficient to make this proposed Euro 7 regulation 
feasible and proportional. This is why ACEA is requesting a substantial number of amendments to 
the Euro 7 proposal. 
Fundamentally, we urge the Commission to present an amended text to the co-legislators that 
takes up the issues addressed in the Q&A, and the many others the Q&A has not yet addressed. 
In parallel, we urge the co-legislators to integrate the amendments proposed by ACEA that aims to 
make the proposal feasible and within the constraints of the transition to zero-emission vehicles 
which industry is committed to delivering.

Euromines Input Paper 
Structural changes to the Market Design 
In the context of the on-going Commission work on a new energy market design, Euromines would 
like to address several challenges and provide solution proposals of what is needed to overcome 
structural issues shown by the unprecedented crisis of 2022 and lay the groundwork to avoid future 
crises. We highly welcome the announced reform of the European electricity market, the decoupling 
of gas and electricity prices and the urgency and priorization to bring electricity prices in line with the 
targets of industrial decarbonization. 
One aim of the reform must be reducing pressure on industrial consumers through a holistic approach 
focused on increasing capacity, especially stable, non-intermittent baseload capacity, to avoid 
disruptions of supply chains due to skyrocketing energy costs. We are facing unprecedented 
challenges for Europe’s society and economy and agree with the assessment that the current price-
setting for consumers – developed under different circumstances – is not fit for purpose and requires 
a change paired with short-term relief measures.  
Resilience of strategic value chains for metals and minerals is indispensable for the technologies to 
achieve the Green Deal objectives and ensure prosperity– this will require both diversification and 
using the EU’s mining potential (cf. Annex I). This paper sets out ideas for structural changes in the 
energy market design to ensure both investments into decarbonization and increase long-term value 
chain resilience as well as expectations towards short-term measures during the current crisis (Annex 
II mapping of challenges, risks & finance needs).  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Summary of suggested long-term measures (explanations below) 
- Medium-long term: structural security and investment certainty 
o Keep the investment signal intact: calling for demand reduction for electricity goes against 
REPowerEU and takes away the guidepost for industrial decarbonization via electrification and 
would be detrimental to our industry who is in the midst of the transition.  
o Decarbonization, investment signals and future competitiveness of our industry is based on a shift 
to electricity. Savings in one eventually lead to more consumption in the other – reducing demand 
in both at the same time is not feasible.  
o Avoid continued capping for both gas and electricity with redistribution as this will lead to massive 
distortions for industry that like mining depends on direct/indirect electrification to decarbonize. 
o Achieving a design that reflects actual production costs of the energy mix at every point, a 
reasonable profit margin plus a collectively applied responsibility for all generators to share 
balancing costs would allow for a more realistic price level.  
- To ensure all of the above we suggest: 
o Soften the effect of the marginal pricing system by a new settlement price through a hybrid pay-
as-bid and pay-as-clear pricing model (shock absorber) 
▪ By setting the market clearing price for non-fossil resources at the non-fossil production 
with the highest price level 
▪ Electricity producers relying on fossil fuels adhere to a pay-as-bid-model.

Medium to long-term resilience adjustments; merging decarbonization with competitiveness  
The crisis should serve as a lessons learned and incentive to structural changes necessary to allow 
Europe’s industries to continuously progress climate neutrality by making sure Europe has the “right” 
type of electricity – variable and baseload – at affordable prices. 
With the proposed acceleration of the decarbonization through electrification and the much-stressed 
“doubling-down” on the twin transition, we welcome the reconsideration of the market design. A 
reformed market design needs to create the production capacity necessary for the decarbonization 
targets alongside incentives to increase interconnection and physical infrastructure as the current 
price setting mechanism is not driving the needed investments in capacity but rather increases 
subsidies. Furthermore, given the reliance of new capacities on feed-in tariffs and PPAs, the set-up 
right now limits the liquidity in long-term markets and future capacity deployment. 
Electricity-specific considerations 
The rigidity of the market design and the rent-capturing due to inframarginal reveneus put the 
electricity markets into a conundrum where abundant, cheap energy and incentives to decarbonize 
become increasingly exclusive. This issue needs to be addressed not just in a crisis-mechanism but 
in a long-term structural change as otherwise it will be exacerbated with increased renewable output 
• “Pay as produced” does not account balancing costs while windfall profits stay with producers 
• RES-producers calculate with inframarginal profits without providing a particular merit to price-
setting and grid-stabilization/balancing 
• The main issue remains with baseload:  
• Electricity is not affordable to 1) fulfill the needs for baseload-power to fully decarbonize 
operations running 24/7 and 2) cover the expected huge increase in demand given that 
electricity (direct or indirect) is the commodity of choice for industry to decarbonize. This 
makes decarbonization under the current system extremely difficult as gas/marginal power 
plant will continue set the price – and there is simply not enough gas in the system nor 
enough electricity generation capacity available.   
• Companies that decarbonize will therefore face increased exposure to spot prices 
• PPAs are not changing investment signals but are rather influenced by it. While this is 
good for producers, the energy intensive industry is rather inclined to avoid investing in the 
EU. 
• The lack of baseload electricity combined with marginal price setting prevents a trigger for 
prices to go achieve a competitive level that merges industrial competitiveness with 
decarbonization through electrification.  
 
Baseload adequacy seems to be the only trigger to reduce energy prices – however the current 
design has as consequence that:  
• The electricity market transformed from a buyers’ to a sellers’ market, with heavy subsidies 
and a mismatch in responsibilities to ensure system working by balancing the grid 
• A new revenue stream appeared: base capacity providers will continue sticking to their assets 
as long as possible as this revenue stream remains highly profitable

Euromines suggests the following action points in addition to ensure that our industry can operate and 
invest with a long-term time horizon, by ensuring energy prices reflect the competitive cost of fossil-
free electricity:  
1) Capping, demand reduction and redistribution require caveats to not exacerbate the 
issue:  
• Demand reduction: industry chose electricity to decarbonize, now putting demand 
reduction for electricity on top (as opposed to a reduction objective to move away from 
fossil to decarbonized energy sources) will bring us into a conundrum. Unless the aim is 
(as spelled out) paying consumers to not use electricity – i.e. to shut down.  
• This leads to an investment crunch as neither gas nor electricity as an energy source for 
industrial processes are viable as both should come with demand reduction. Moving away 
from gas to direct/indirect electrification will not lead to electricity savings.   
• Such a proposal therefore requires caveats as to avoid unrealistic energy reduction 
demands and limitations for industry to avoid shutting off as there is no guidepost anymore 
where to go and enterprises that pursue this transition must not be put at a disadvantage.  
• It needs to be kept in mind that gas for electricity only makes up 10-20% of consumption, 
the rest is for heating and industrial applications/feedstock. This must be reflected 
especially for those who made or are in the midst of transitioning like the mining industry.  
• Coherence issue: capping, demand reduction and redistribution would go against the 
REPowerEU objectives that provided a clear guidepost for intensifying electrification 
efforts. Any such system would lead to big divergences among member states and would 
require safety-caveats for industry, particularly the mining industry, to avoid penalizing 
those that are already in the transition and will require more electricity for their 
decarbonization efforts.  
 
2) Indirect cost compensation: Euromines represents 42 different metals and minerals, that 
are either price-takers, thus traded according to globally set reference prices, or in direct 
competition with producers in the EU neighborhood that benefit from much lower energy 
prices. The way forward for decarbonization will be via electricity – the main commodity the 
energy intensive industry chose either in direct or indirect applications. With the rising intensity 
of electricity in processes, the issue of indirect CO2-costs is becoming more prominent in 
order to stay competitive. We urge in the context of the current price-hikes, to make use of the 
flexibility clause in the ETS State Aid Guidelines to ensure mining becoming eligible for 
indirect cost compensation. Otherwise – as industry zoomed in on electricity as commodity of 
choice to decarbonize and in absence of structural changes to the market design – Europe will 
find itself again in the same situation.  
 
3) Future-proofing Power Purchase Agreements: For any stable production running 24/7 like 
mining, baseload is vital – and with increased share of renewables, the issue of balancing

4) Shared responsibility for grid stabilization: Sharing windfall profits with consumers in a 
permanent structure is possible as auctioning happens on an hourly basis. The data is 
therefore available to distribute the profits between RES producer and consumers based on 
the energy mix they consume. Phasing out subsidies and feed-in tariffs will help with 
distributing balancing responsibility.  
 
5) Enhanced inclusion of DSOs in the roll-out of renewables: DSOs are responsible for 
admitting connection points of renewable energy operations, yet the more volatility they allow, 
the higher is the cost-burden they face. To increase renewables, investments in DSO-grids are 
necessary, but with prices set by public authorities, this possibility is limited. Some of the 
(future) windfall profits from renewable generation should go into extending of DSO grids to 
overcome limitations in grid capacity. 
 
6) A new shock-absorbing settlement price via a combined pay-as-bid and pay-as-clear 
model. Building upon point 13 of ACER’s “Final Assessment of the EU Wholesale Electricity 
Market Design”, that describes the US “Peaker Net Margin” (Texas) and the Australian 
“Cumulative Pricing Threshold” we suggest a change in the pricing design following along the 
principles of these mechanisms.  
As today’s electricity prices are based on the marginal clearing price set by the most 
expensive (mostly fossil based) electricity producing power plant, competitive prices for 
electricity are out of reach even though cost competitive renewables penetrate the system 
more than ever. While this marginal price system creates enormous windfall profits for fossil-
free electricity producers, it adds simply costs to end consumers without maximizing societal 
welfare and incentivizing better technology.  
 
In light of  
- the increased penetration of fossil free electricity 
- the need to preserve the price signal  
- the importance to incentivize industrial decarbonization combined with competitiveness 
- the superior efficiency of market signals as opposed to re-distribution efforts

The advantages of this model ensure a better market functioning as to:  
- Decoupling of gas and electricity prices 
- Stabilized and transparent price formation  
- A still high profit level for renewable electricity producers while fossil electricity producers 
are paid their bidding price to provide baseload and power in times of demand  
- Avoiding excessive windfall profits would allow industrial consumers to undertake 
investments necessary to decarbonize 
- It would not change the electricity price signal or touch upon the functioning of the EU ETS 
and thus ensure continued conservation and demand flexibility incentives 
- It maintains current bidding practices, thus ensures a reasonable profit for fossil-free 
electricity producers and incentives to build new fossil-free electricity capacities 
The implementation does not require profound changes 
- Price calculation would not change significantly  
- The marginal price system would stay intact but protects consumers from excessively high 
prices 
- It is technology-neutral which prevents distortions of the Forward Market  
 
Conclusion: a combination of the points raised above 
Any review of the electricity market must not lead to a reduction in the incentives for investment in 
fossil-free electricity production and industrial electricity consumption – both direct and indirect – for 
decarbonization. The effects of taxes on and re-distribution of so-called "windfall profits" and savings 
obligations must not become a permanent mechanism as decarbonization, investment signals and 
future competitiveness of our industry is based on a shift to electricity. Savings in one eventually lead 
to more consumption in the other – reducing demand in both at the same time is not feasible.  
In light of the increased focus on an open strategic autonomy for critical raw materials and 
direct/indirect electrification as the mining industry’s commodity of choice for decarbonization, the 
issue of sufficient electricity at affordable prices is becoming more urgent than ever. Thus, a reduction 
of the electricity prices via a new price settlement system, tax reductions and adequate carbon 
leakage protection from indirect CO2-costs transferred through electricity prices for the mining 
industry will be paramount to ensure transparent and stable electricity markets that provide the right 
investment signals for industrial decarbonization through electrification.

Annex I -  mining industry exposure and ripple effects 
The EU mining industry and mineral sector is one of the most important primary industries of the EU ensuring a 
steady supply of raw materials and thus ensuring the strategic autonomy in minerals and metals critical for  
defence, the clean energy transition, pharmaceuticals and other technologies.  
EU ore production is up to 90% lower in emissions than equivalent production in third country, depending on the 
availability of fossil-free electricity and infrastructure to absorb a large amount of intermittent renewable energy 
as well as the availability of mining enterprises to invest into electrification, digitisation and process 
development.  
Just for the technology needs of the net-zero climate emission target, six times more metals and minerals are 
needed, and many times more for specific metals and minerals that are needed for  clean energy production and 
supply.  
The EU mining sector has the potential to provide a major part of these minerals and metals to ensure the raw 
materials base for clean energy technologies, increase self-sufficiency within the EU and foster innovation for 
the sustainability and digital transition.  
Euromines represents 42 different primary minerals and metals of which several amount to 100% of EU 
production, accounting for 350.000 jobs.  
The energy intensity of our industry and the need for adequate infrastructure and the related raw materials 
needed puts the EU economy in a hen-and-egg situation: fossil-free electricity requires huge new amounts of 
raw materials for infrastructure and capacity, while the production of these raw materials requires huge amounts 
of fossil-free electricity (direct and indirect: demand is estimated to increase 15-fold).  
Such high energy prices will have consequences on the following technologies given the lack of availability : 
- Clean energy technologies: copper, nickel, gold, silver, zinc, iron ore 
- Steel and ferroalloys: iron ore, zinc, nickel  
- Recycling of e-scraps and metals: lead  
- High temperature processing: magnesite  
- Fertilizers, foodstuff, medical products: potash, magnesia, salt 
All of these can be sustainably and responsibly mined in Europe – yet due to the high electricity prices further 
investments, expansion or new projects are impossible to model given the exposure level to the high gas and 
electricity (direct and indirect) prices in energy use. 
Ripple effects will impact all major industries and value chains in the EU, for example:  
- Metals-intensive value chains ranging from electricity infrastructure to batteries  
- Water treatment, food and feed products (magnesium and calcium salts)  
- Potash fertilizers given the natural gas needs (high-efficiency CHP) with no substitution available 
and thus impacting food security of the EU 
- Additives for plastics in electric cables (automotive industry)  
- Hydrochloric acid and caustic soda which used to be readily available  
- Salt for pharmaceutical and medical applications (e.g. purified salt for COVID-19-vaccines), salt for 
chemical industry

Annex II – challenge mapping 
Fossil-free mining and processes will require massive investments in infrastructure and technolog y to:  
• Rapid increase in electrification, automation, and digitisation  
• Phasing out of fossil fuels used for heating and ventilation  
• Replacement of vehicles and work machinery with electric and battery-powered alternatives 
• Developments in new refining processes  
• New processes such as battery development, CCS and CCU, hydrogen production and storage  
• Greater efficiency processes  
• Major technological and system developments and gains and as well as more inter -sector connections  
Challenges are: 
• Stemming the high investment costs in electric vehicles and infrastructure  
• Managing performance uncertainty and unstable electricity and fuel prices  
• Investments into infrastructure to ensure a stable supply of fossil free electricity  
• Investment and OPEX-uncertainty for indirect electrification through hydrogen  
• Availability of hydrogen 
• Stable access to fossil free electricity at competitive cost  
• Development of fossil-free chemicals for explosives 
• Development of fossil-free chemicals and reducing agents for the enrichment and refining processes. 
• Create commercial solutions to utilise and store carbon dioxide  
• Safe and efficient battery systems and related infrastructure Creating more efficient and more 
predictable permit processes  
For some minerals, these investment challenges are compounded by fuel switching requirements to save gas 
as it requires often changes in processes and storage facilities for enterprises with si gnificant higher costs, 
lower energy efficiency and higher specific CO2-footprints per kWh for which no free allowances would be 
handed out – or compensation granted for savings of gas. These additional costs for fuel switching and thus gas 
savings could amount up to several dozen Million euros, for which no funding exists while risk increases due to 
forward contracts in case of significant gas price drops.  
These high investment costs for the transition of our sector will be in the range of several hundred billion Euros. 
These investments will not materialize if OPEX certainty is not given in terms of stable and low prices for fossi l-
free energy sources.

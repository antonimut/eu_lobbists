Equinor response to the European Commission’s Electricity Market Design consultation 
Equinor welcomes the opportunity to respond to the European Commission’s consultation on the 
reform of the EU’s electricity market design. 
 
The recent price developments in the European energy market, driven primarily by sudden 
disruptions of natural gas supply from Russia, have led to an unprecedented discrepancy in supply 
and demand. This has further highlighted the need to ensure security of supply and to incentivize 
investments and rollout of more renewable power generation, while guaranteeing market stability . 
Europe needs a  regulatory framework that guarantees a functioning internal energy market , 
incentivizes investments in renewable generation investments, power transmission and distribution 
systems that supports further electrification  as well as in low carbon technologies  necessary for 
flexibility services to handle intermittencies and grid stability.  
 
Change to the European electricity market d esign aiming to significa ntly alter  price setting 
mechanisms can come to severely impact investments. Modifications to the current design, including 
many of the aspects covered in this questionnaire, would merit a robust impact assessment, and 
should not be rushed through a legislative pro cess if the goal is to arrive at , and adopt long-term 
solutions that delivers security of supply, lower emissions and safeguards European  
competitiveness.   
 
When looking at wholesale market reform it is important to remember the role that the market has 
played in encouraging competition, innovation, and cost savings, and that while the day-ahead price 
is an important reference, forward markets will also, at least over the medium term, continue to play 
several important functions through price recovery (as when developing fixed priced Power Purchase 
Agreements). 
Crisis response measures affecting the functioning of the European energy markets should as 
general principle, remain temporary, and not made a permanent fixture in the legislation.  
Overall, the areas covered in this questionnaire are highly relevant to address some of the 
challenges mentioned above. Increased use of PPAs and Contracts for Difference (CfDs), coupled 
with increased liquidity in forward markets are important steps towards providing price stability for 
consumers and increasing the build out of new renewable power generation through providing routes 
to market.    
 
While not in direct scope, it is worth noting, other reforms at the Member State level, including around 
planning and grid  development, will also be needed to support the objectives  of the proposed 
reforms.   
 
Given the restricted time limit for this consultation, Equinor has chosen to primarily focus on a 
selection of chapters and sub-topics:   
 
Power Purchase Agreements  
Equinor believes that market based PPAs can play an important role in price hedging and as a long-
term instrument to support investments in the development of new power generation, particularly 
electricity from renewable energy sources. For both producers and consumers market based PPAs 
can facilitate access to finance also from private commercial actors.

Certain barriers remain for a more widespread adoption of PPAs. Public and market-based credit 
guarantees could help both new developers and new consumers in increasing the amount and size 
of counterparties, these sources of support should be possible to combine. While neither CfDs nor 
PPAs should be made mandatory, there are already examples at Member State level where these 
schemes are combined successfully.  
 
Forward Markets  
Liquid wholesale markets are essential to producers and consumers in handling commercial risks. 
The ability to hedge forward production decreases the impact of short -term price spikes on both 
buyers and sellers. Hedging is essential for risk management, and for reducing the impact of price 
volatility. Hedging becomes  less costly when forward markets are liquid, with the resulting cost -
savings benefiting consumers. Nevertheless, it is important to explore the future role of the market, 
in the context of an increasing share of low-cost low carbon generation.  
Contracts for Difference  
Two-way CfDs can be a helpful, even a necessary tool to support investment and development of 
new power generation technologies. Although they may contribute to some mitigation of market price 
volatility, their main function is to attract necessary investments. Contracts, like CfDs, should not be 
made mandatory, but should remain a voluntary instrument, so as to allow for market participants to 
find the best solutions for hedging and financing.  
 
The design of CfDs should reflect the ultimate objectives of energy policy; different technologies will 
have different needs when it comes to, for example, price, duration, and location. Equally it may be 
effective to combine technologies to offer stable, cost efficient and decarbonized power. At the same 
time, there is a need to assess how CfDs can be established in a way that provides the right 
incentives to react to market signals, while at the same time providing the necessary risk reduction. 
Designs of CfDs should give due attention to potential distortions on short -term incentives and 
dispatch optimality, and the proper functioning of the market.  
 
Accelerating the Deployment of Renewables  
In order to accelerate the deployment of renewables, a transmission access guarantee (TAG) could 
be a helpful tool to promote offshore hybrid renewable projects. There will be a need for clarity and 
transparency from system operators and regulatory authorities on transmission charges and tariffs, 
both for the offshore hybrid projects and other new onshore generators. It must also be assumed 
that a TAG will not alone suffice as support for an offshore renewable project, which would likely also 
necessitate a CfD.   
 
In addition, there is a need for more efficient and transparent permitting procedures , coupled with 
harmonized and properly implemented rules in all Member States.  
 
Limiting revenues of inframarginal generators  
The temporary inframarginal revenue limits were introduced as a response to the high wholesale 
energy market prices, and they should remain temporary and not written into permanent legislation. 
Their introduction has led to divergent implementation across Members States, with the resulting 
patchwork of measures likely to create  market distortions, reduce investment signals in certain 
countries over others, and erode efficient dispatch signals. There is a risk that such policies, should 
they continue, and when taken together with other temporary measures, may harm investment just 
at the time the REPowerEU calls for increased low carbon development. If such measures were to 
be prolonged, the Commission must ensure their implementation is harmonized across all Member 
States.

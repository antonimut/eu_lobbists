Summary  
On 26 October 2022, the EU Commission presented a proposal to revise the EU Ambient Air Quality 
Directives.  
The EU Ambient Air Quality Directives define common methods for monitoring, assessing and provid-
ing information on ambient air quality in the European Union and set objectives for air quality to avoid, 
prevent or reduce harmful effects on human health and the environment as a whole. EU air quality 
standards have been set for the following 13 air pollutants: Sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide and nitro-
gen oxide, particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5), ozone, benzene, lead, carbon monoxide, arsenic, cad-
mium, nickel and benzo(a)pyrene. 
As part of the European Green Deal, the EU Commission is planning to revise these standards to bring 
them more into line with the recommendat ions of the World Health Organization (WHO). The revision 
focuses on the EU Directive on Ambient Air Quality and Cleaner Air for Europe (2008/50/EC) and the 
EU Directive on Arsenic, Cadmium, Mercury, Nickel and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons in Ambient 
Air (2004/107/EC). 
In our view, a revision of the European Ambient Air Quality Directives is not necessary at present. In 
its report on the review of these directives, the Commission concludes that the current legislation pro-
vides the necessary tools to meet existing challenges. The directives have been effective in improving 
air quality and achieving high air quality standards.  
If the Commission, Council and EP pursue the legislative project further, we would appreciate if 
 
▪ the significantly tightened limit values will only be applied after an appropriate transition period 
from 2040 at the earliest, 
▪ the challenges of the industry's transformation towards climate neutrality are taken into  
account,  
▪ additional provisions on ac cess to justice , compensation and penalties are not introduced  
(not against the state either).

Key demands: 
1. Postponement of the process on the amendment of the Ambient Air Quality Directive 
No urgent need for action 
There is no urgent need for action to amend the existing and very successful EU Ambient Air Quality 
Directives now. At the same time, the EU is pursuing a large number of legislative projects that already 
contribute directly and indirectly to improving air quality. These include climate protection regulations 
that will lead to a significant reduction in the use of fossil fuels and thus also to a decrease in particulate 
matter and nitrogen oxide in the air. Other regulations with a positive influence on air quality are, for 
example, in the road transport sector the electrification of the European vehicle fleets for passenger 
cars and light commercial vehicles and the associated de facto "ban" on the registration of new vehicles 
with combustion engines from 2035, the planned amendment of the CO2 fleet limits for heavy commer-
cial vehicles and regulations with regard to the emission performance of vehicles (keyword e.g. Euro 
7 standard) as well as in the building sector the German restrictions on pure oil or gas heating systems. 
Ultimately, the requirements for the energy efficiency of buildings will also have a positive influence on 
air quality over the next few years.  
Insufficient trade-off between health protection and other EU objectives 
In addition, the proposed directive fundamentally lacks a balanced consideration of the interests in 
protecting health as far as possible with other objectives of the European Union. EU objectives such 
as ensuring affordable mobility, creating and maintaining well -paid jobs, preserving the competitive-
ness of the European economy including cross -sectoral value chains, ensuring sufficient financial 
strength of regions and maintaining efficient infrastructure are disregarded. The proportionality princi-
ple that applies to legislative measures is also not taken into account. Instead, long -term and very 
vague goals ("toxic free environment", "zero pollution objective") are pursued unilaterally and primarily 
on the environmental side, which is only examined in the abstract, without looking at the whole picture. 
Lack of knowledge on particulate matter (PM 2.5) – First set up measurement programmes  
It is necessary to first improve the knowledge about the ambient air quality in the European Member 
States - especially for the parameter particulate matter PM 2.5 – and only in a second step , if neces-
sary, to set measures and limit values on the basis of valid data. From BDI´s point of view limit values 
should not be set without reliable knowledge of the ambient air quality and without being able to really 
assess the effects on Member States. Therefore, the consultation on the directive should be sus-
pended so that the Member States can first carry out adequate measurement programmes to improve 
the knowledge on ambient air quality. Sufficient measurement results on the actual pollution of Euro-
pean regions – especially by particulate matter PM 2.5 related to the daily and  annual load – do not 
exist so far. 
Insufficient consultation time due to expiry of the legislative period 
Moreover, there is only little time left until the end of the legislative period of the European Parliament. 
Thus, it must be feared that there will not be sufficient capacity and time in the Council and Parliament 
for careful consultation on the Commission's complex proposal.

2. Application of new limit values only after an appropriate transition period in 2040 at the ear-
liest 
The new and significantly lowered limit values should only apply after an appropriate transition period 
from 2040 at the earliest, as compliance with the values by 2030 is unrealistic due to the actual ambient 
air quality levels in Europe. This would also harmonize better with the EU's other regulatory plans, 
especially in the areas of climate protection and transport. European society can only cope with the 
considerable impacts of the proposed limit values on industry – especially on numerous medium-sized 
enterprises - as well as on commerce, mobility and housing with long and sufficient transition periods 
(Article 1). 
 
3. The challenges of the transformation of industry should be considered 
The new, stricter air quality limits may lead to the delay or prevention of projects in areas , where air 
quality limit values are exceeded – even if the projects serve climate neutrality and transformation. 
This must be prevented. Instruments must be found to adequately take into account the industry's 
interest in a rapid transformation. This is because a successful transformation of industry is the pre-
requisite for effective plant-related emission reductions and corresponding air pollution control. There-
fore, transformation projects should be given special consideration when drawing up air quality plans 
(cf. Article 19). 
 
4. No additional provisions on access to justice, compensation and penalties (not agains t the 
state either) 
Additional provisions on access to justice, compensation and penalties are either unnecessary or even 
counterproductive, as they mean considerable legal uncertainties for the actors concerned (planning 
authorities, municipalities, proje ct developers) and thus impede rather than accelerate the desired 
transformation (cf. Articles 27-29).  
For example, Article 28, which introduces a new compensation scheme for damage to health, could 
result in compensation claims against the state of unimagined proportions.

In detail: 
Article 1 – Objectives of the Directive are too ambitious  
Article 1 No. 1 – Improvement of air quality instead of a zero-emission target as an objective 
of the Directive 
We propose that the existing Article 1, which defines the objectives of the Directive, only be editorially 
adapted but no new objectives be introduced. 
The EU Commission proposes to anchor the zero-pollution objective as the goal of the directive. The 
objective of the previous Ambient Air Quality Directive was to improve ambient air quality. In our opin-
ion, this objective is preferable.  
The goal of a zero-pollution environment is undeniably desirable in the abstract. But is it realistic? In 
our European society,  where industry and mobility play an important role,  it is unlikely that a zero -
pollution environment can be achieved with proportionate effort. Rather, a risk-based approach needs 
to be at the center of the political discussion. It should be examined what can actually be achieved in 
the member states. A zero-pollution environment will not be achievable by European Member States.  
Similarly, a toxic-free environment is not achievable even by scientific standards. When the objectives 
of the directive state that a toxic-free environment should be achieved, the term itself is completely 
unclear and cannot be clearly classified by the member states in the implementation. 
Article 1 No. 2 – Application of the limit values only after an appropriate transitional period at 
the earliest from 2040  
All limit values of Annex 1 of the Directive should be complied with from 2040 at the earliest and not 
already from 2030. Article 1 No. 2 should be amended accordingly. Otherwise, unacceptable interven-
tions in the economy, mobility and housing would be the consequence in order to raise air quality to 
the required even higher level. 
Even compliance with the new air quality limit values as early as 2040 would be very ambitious and 
could only be achieved with considerable investment . In the EU Commission's proposal, the existing 
limit values are halved or lowered even further. The following table shows this in an overview:

In addition, there is only limited knowledge about the actual pollution levels. F or example, PM 2.5 is

Although the Commission's proposal provides for a considerable increase in the number of measuring

We are against an approach of setting limit values without really being able t o assess the impact on 
Member States, regions or municipalities.  
The considerable gap between the existing ambient air quality  and the ambitious new limit values 
requires a long transition period until the new limit values apply. Compliance with the limit values for 
NOx and PM 2.5 will only be possible through implementation measures in other policy areas, espe-
cially climate protection (ramp-up of electromobility, hydrogen transformation, etc.). However, their ef-
fects will not have an impact until well into the 2030s. 
The implementation deadlines for the Ambient Air Quality Directive must therefore be oriented towards 
the implementation of the aforementioned measures. In 2030,  it is not yet possible to foresee what 
successes will have been achieved with regard to air quality, for example, through the "Fit for 55 pack-
age" in other sectors such as transport, buildings and the restriction of combustion engines.

Article 3 – Extension of review period of air quality values to ten years  
Article 3 No. 1 – Extension of review period to at least ten years 
The periodic review period of air quality values by the Commission of five years from 2028 provided 
for in Article 3 is too short and should be increased to at least ten years.  
A prerequisite for achieving the ambitious limit values targeted in the Commission's proposal are cross-
sectoral (transformation) strategies that cannot be implemented overnight. These include, in particular, 
the phasing out of combustion engines and the advancement of electromobility in the transport sector 
as well as the industrial transformation to hydrogen technology. The limit values are already at a level 
that can no longer be influenced by individual technical measures and an implementation of the r e-
quirements from BAT for plants that fall under the IED. 
The start of the first review cycle in 2028 should also be reconsidered. If the revised Ambient Air Quality 
Directive enters into force in 2024 and the Member States have a two -year implementation period, it 
would be too early to review the values again in 2028.   
Article 3 No. 2 Paragraph 2 – Policy decision instead of reference to WHO guidelines 
No. 2 paragraph 2 seems to establish a (de facto) dynamic reference to the WHO guidelines with the 
wording "ensuring alignment with the WHO Air Quality Guidelines", which is to be rejected. The EU 
has no regulatory access to the WHO regulations and it is not foreseeable what the content of these 
regulations will be in the future. The EU should be able to deci de on limit values itself - especially 
against the background of the various political goals pursued with the Green Deal and possible conflicts 
of objectives. 
Article 3 No. 2 Paragraph 3 – Proportionality assessment in the review 
In Article 3 No. 2 (3), a proportionality assessment should be established for the aspects to be consid-
ered by the EU Commission in the review ("proportionality of measures"). It would also be important to 
add that the EU Commission should also consider aspects such as the effects of air quality measures 
on mobility, infrastructure and the economic performance of the Member States in the review. This is 
necessary because the WHO guidelines merely reflect scientific findings without claiming to assess 
their practicability in practice.

Article 7-10 – Extension of air quality monitoring 
Article 7-10 – Lack of knowledge on particulate matter (PM 2.5) – first establish measurement 
programmes 
It is necessary to first improve the knowledge about the ambient air quality  in the Member States – 
especially for the parameter particulate matter PM 2.5 – and only then to set measures and limit values.  
 
BDI is opposed to an approach of setting limit values now without really being able to assess the effects 
on member states, regions or municipalities. Therefore, the consultations on the directive should be 
suspended so that the member states can first carry out measurement programmes to improve the 
findings on air quality – especially for the parameter particulate matter PM 2.5. 
 
The parameter PM 2.5 is only measured at relatively few measuring stations in Germany. The Com-
mission is also introducing a new 24 -hour limit value for which there is hardly any measurement data 
available. The annual value for PM 2.5 is exceeded very frequ ently throughout Germany. In this con-
text, the measured maximum annual limit values are also significant, because they illustrate that there 
are many areas where achieving the new limit values proposed by the Commission in such a short 
time is not realisti c. The proposed limit values  can only be achieved – if at all – with serious and 
disproportionate interventions in economic and civil activities (keywords: Industry, trade, road traffic, 
heating of buildings, agriculture). 
 
The proposed regulations in Articles 7-10 will lead to more parameters being measured at more meas-
uring points in the Member States. In principle, this is to be welcomed. However, this will probably lead 
to an increased number of exceedance areas. In these areas – which are not yet known  – there will 
then be restrictions on economic and civil society activities – also and especially in the mobility sector 
– and this will happen very soon, as the limit values are to apply from 2030.   
 
Article 7 No. 2 – Assessment of areas at 10-year intervals  
Consideration should be given to extending the time limit for the assessment procedure in Article 7 
from five to ten years.  
Reviewing the classification of all zones and agglomerations in the Member States against the assess-
ment thresholds set out in the Directive in a 5-year cycle is not practically possible in view of the expe-
rience gained so far. A review every ten years, on the other hand, is more feasible and can indeed 
provide reliable and usable findings. 
Article 7 –Formation of suitable averages between different measuring points 
The monitoring system should be extended to include a method that makes it possible to form suita-
ble averages between different measuring points. Extreme or unusual deviations shou ld be able to 
be neglected in this way.

Article 12 – Clarification that limit values of Article 12 only apply from 2040 at the earliest 
Article 12 should only apply from the year in which the limit values are to be complied with according 
to Article 1 (at present, the COM proposal regulates the application of the limit values from 2030; 
realistically, as explained above, the limit values should be applied in 2040 at the earliest). 
According to Article 1 No. 2 of the Commission proposal, the limit values of the Directive are to be 
complied with from 2030. Art icle 12 No. 1, however, in fact leads to compliance with the values from 
Annex 1 with immediate effect. This does not fit together. This creates an unclear legal situation in the 
period up to 2030.  
According to the current wording, it is not clear what Member States have to consider in this transitional 
period when approving projects that may directly or indirectly emit air pollutants (transport routes, ports, 
industrial projects, power plants). In fact, the limit values thus also apply immediately in the non -ex-
ceedance areas, which makes the Member States' ability to act and the implementation of projects 
considerably more difficult.  
Article 12 No. 1 and No. 2 – Maintain target values 
The target values for metals (arsenic, cadmium and nickel) in Article 12 No. 1 and No. 2 and Annex I 
should be retained. The Commission proposal i ntends to change the existing target values into limit 
values. 
Ozone continues to be a target value in the Commission proposal; this should also apply to the metals 
mentioned. The metals should be deleted accordingly in No. 1 and inserted in No. 2. Similar to ozone, 
no limit values but target values have been set for these metals in the current Ambient Air Quality  
Directive, as they have complex characteristics that have to be taken into account when assessing 
compliance with the WHO values. Natural sources are relevant, as well as external factors such as 
unstable meteorological conditions (dry weather, thermal inversion) or the local geographical location, 
which must be taken into account for certain measuring stations.  
Article 12 No. 4 – Policy decision instead of reference to WHO guidelines 
The de facto legal and dynamic reference to the WHO Air Quality Guideli nes should not be inserted. 
The EU has no regulatory access to the WHO rulebook and no one can foresee what the content of 
this rulebook will be in the future.   
Article 12 No. 4 – No legal uncertainty through unclear terms and objectives 
Article 12 No. 4 should not be changed, if possible, in order not to create legal uncertainty. 
According to Article 12 No. 4, Member States shall endeavour to achieve and maintain the best ambient 
air quality and a high level of environmental and human health protection, taking into account the WHO 
guidelines. It is unclear how the term "endeavour to achieve" will ultimately be interpreted by the courts 
and what "the best ambient air quality" is or how  high the "high level of environmental and human 
health protection" must be.

The deletion of the proportionality clause in No. 3 should be reversed. 
Article 16 – Same regulation for contributions from natural sources and contri-
butions from indirect human activities 
Emissions indirectly attributable to human activities should be regulated equally as  emissions from 
natural sources, especially if renaturation or recultivation, which is in the interest of environmental pro-
tection and climate change mitigation, is pursued. 
On closer examination, the boundary between a contribution from a natural source and a contribution 
from indirect human activity cannot be clearly drawn. In particular, interventions in the landscape 
through agriculture, quarrying, gravel extraction, open-cast mining, recultivation, construction activities 
with exposure of areas and embankments, renaturation and creation of embankments as well as sand 
beaches at artificial ponds and lakes cannot be attributed to direct human activities if dust is removed 
by the wind.

For such transformation projects (e.  g. hydrogen transformation in industry), no procedure under  
No. 2 should be required if the relevant emitters agree on installation-specific plans with the authorities

within or outside of air quality plans pursuant to Article 19. These specific plans should allow exceed-

Article 18 No. 1 – Exception for SO2  
Article 18 No. 1 according to which the deadline for compliance with the limit values for particulate 
matter (PM 10 and PM 2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) can be postponed, should also apply to SO2. A 
postponement should be granted if the limit values are exceeded locally limited due to site -specific 
conditions. External factors such as climatic and meteorological conditions and local geographical cir-
cumstances also influence compliance with SO2 air quality standards. These factors should be consid-
ered as well when developing and implementing measures to improve air quality. There are techno-
logical limits to reducing SO2 emissions, so continued high levels of investment will lead to lower im-
provements than in previous years. When setting stricter air quality standards, technical and economic 
feasibility should be considered, as well as the time needed for industry to adapt.  
Article 19 – Air quality plans 
Article 19 No. 1 – Inclusion of the principle of proportionality  
The principle of proportionality should be explicitly included in Article 19 No. 1.  
Disproportionate measures such as reducing emissions that are technically difficult or impossible to 
achieve should not be taken.  
By including the principle of proportionality, consideration could be given to special circumstances in 
individual cases. For example, on construction sites, the problem of exposure of particulate matter can 
be controlled to a certain extent but may not be completely prevented. On construction sites, particulate 
matter is released both by the construction activity itself and by the use of machinery. The peculiarity 
is that construction sites are mostly set up only temporarily and are not stationary installations.  The 
existing limit values are already difficult for the construction sector to meet. This is especially critical 
since the construction industry plays a central role in the provision of public services through infrastruc-
ture and housing construction. To make matters worse, stricter limits would make it more difficult to 
recycle building materials on site, for example conc rete that is crushed in the first step. This would 
have a negative effect on the construction sector's efforts in the circular economy.   
Article 19 No. 1 – Consideration of polluters with significant contributions 
Article 19 should stipulate that measures in air quality plans  may only be directed at those polluters 
who also make a significant contribution to the exceedance of limit values. 
Article 19 No. 4 – No preparation of air quality plans before the limit values come into force  
Article 19 No. 4 should be deleted. It stipulates that air quality plans must be drawn up in zones in 
which the limit values (applicable from 2030) are exceeded two years after the directive enters into 
force (i. e. presumably in 2026) by 31.12.2029. 
The Commission's proposed regulation will result in an obligation to draw up air quality plans immedi-
ately after the Directive enters into force. This is because the known measurement results in Germany

show – as shown in detail above – that the limit values are exceeded nationwide. This means that the

Article 19 No. 5 – Plans for industrial transformation  
The new, stricter air quality limits may lead to the delay or prevention of projects in areas , where air 
quality limit values are exceeded – even if the projects serve climate neutrality and transformation. 
This must be prevented. Instruments must be found  to adequately take into account the industry's 
interest in a rapid transformation. This is because a successful transformation of industry is the pre-
requisite for effective plant-related emission reductions and corresponding air pollution control. There-
fore, transformation projects should be given special consideration when drawing up air quality plans. 
In No. 5 it should be newly regulated that operators of installations according to Directive 2010/75/EU 
(IED) in areas of exceedance should be given the op portunity to agree on a plan with the competent 
authority, within or outside of an air quality plan, which ensures that within five to ten years the exceed-
ance is avoided or at least significantly reduced. It should be made clear in the Directive that measures 
under the Ambient Air Quality Directive beyond the plan are excluded.  
In order not to jeopardize investments in the EU's industrial sites that are necessary for climate protec-
tion (e. g. in hydrogen transformation) and beyond, special plans for installations subject to the Indus-
trial Emissions Directive should be made possible that exclude further measures under the Ambient 
Air Quality Directive. Such a plan must be linkable to a transformation plan in the sense of Article 27d 
of the IED.  
Article 19 – EU Commission guidelines  
The EU Commission should propose in a guidance document measures to mitigate background pollu-
tion that are suitable for different types of areas and can be implemented at a proportionate cost.   
Article 22 – Information to the public 
In Article 22 No. 2, the comparison with the WHO recommendations should be deleted. The Commis-
sion proposes that an hourly updated, publicly available air quality index be produced by the Member 
States. This should also refer to the WHO recommendations on air quality. As already stated above, 
we reject de facto references to these WHO values, as they can neither be influenced by the European 
Union nor have any legal validity in the EU. 
Article 27 – Regulation on access to justice not necessary 
The newly proposed Article 27 should be deleted. The provision is unnecessary because comprehen-
sive legal action is already possible in the EU and in the Member States against missing or insufficient 
measures for air quality planning. This was once again explicitly confirmed by the European Court of 
Justice in its most recent judgement (judgement of 22.12.2022, case C-61/21).

The stipulation of an almost unconditional right of action for NGOs in No. 1, last paragraph, is inappro-
priate. At the very least, the non-governmental organization with the right to sue should work to improve 
air quality or health protection.  
Article 28 – Delete compensation for injury to health 
Article 28, which introduces a new compensation scheme for damage to health, should be deleted.  
Compensation claims of unimagined magnitude would be the consequence.  
In its judgement of 22.12.2022 (Case C -61/2), the Court of Justice expressly clarified that there is  
currently no legal basis under European law for an individual right of action for damages by an EU 
citizen due to possible damage to health caused by the exceeding of air quality limit values.  
This right to compensation should also not be introduced by the amendment of the Ambient Air Quality 
Directive. The consequences of the regulation proposed by the Commission would be dramatic.  
▪ The introduction of such a claim for compensation would lead to considerable uncertainty for 
the authorities responsible for implementing the air quality plans and have the effect that par-
ticularly far-reaching and thus potentially disproportionate measures are taken as a precau-
tionary measure to avoid the risk of compensation. 
▪ Since compliance with t he limit values for NOx and PM 2 .5 proposed by the Commission is 
extremely unlikely in 2030 and millions of EU citizens probably live in areas where the limit 
values are exceeded, a large number  of lawsuits of immense proportions is foreseeable – 
especially since class actions are also to be made possible (Art. 28 No. 2). The consequences 
for legal peace in the member states, for the courts, authorities and the treasury of the member 
states are not foreseeable. 
▪ The possibility under Art. 28 No. 2 that NGOs can also file class actions will foreseeably in-
crease the number of lawsuits once again.  
Article 29 – No penalties against natural and legal persons 
The amendments in Article 29 should be deleted completely.  
The regulations with which natural and legal persons can be charged with quite considerable penalties 
are rejected. The Directive is primarily directed at the Member States and their air pollution control 
policy and planning. Individuals  and companies that emit air pollutants are only indirectly affected by 
the provisions of the Directive. A European law provision that requires Member States to nevertheless 
enact criminal provisions against natural and legal persons under private law in their national law i s 
therefore disproportionate.  
The existing sanctions regime is sufficient. There are no indications that natural or legal persons are 
significantly responsible for the failure to meet air quality objectives. Rather, the revision of the directive 
is intended to place more responsibility on the competent authorities in the Member States. Extended 
sanction regulations are therefore neither necessary nor justified.

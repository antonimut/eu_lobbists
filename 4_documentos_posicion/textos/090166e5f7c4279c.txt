2 Public consultation on the European electricity market reform 
Summary of the reply to the European Commission's 
public consultation on the design of the electricity market 
February 2023 
The transition to climate neutrality  requires a regulatory framework that makes 
electrification the most natural choice for industry  and households and passes on the 
benefits of renewable electricity generation to all consumers. Consumers must be at the 
center of European electricity market reform. Yet, the reform must keep separate how 
Europe meets the long -term goal of a carbon -neutral electricity sector at the lowest 
cost to society and the short-term policy response to high electricity prices.  
The current energy crisis stemming from the war in Ukraine has put the European 
economy under severe stress. The EU's new energy security strategy, REPowerEU, points 
to the deployment of renewables as a key instrument to address unusually high energy 
prices and support European households and businesses. The EU wants wind power to 
account for 43% of European electricity by 2030, up from 15% today. This means 
doubling the rate of wind deployment from 15 GW per year to 30 GW per year. In Spain, 
the current ta rget is to reach 50 GW of installed wind power capacity by 2030, which 
means that 2.5 GW per year needs to be installed, which means tripling the installed 
capacity in 2021 (850 MW). 
In this context, national governments have intervened in energy markets to alleviate the 
burdens on end consumers . They did so without a joint policy response to the energy 
crisis, resulting in fragmented and uncoordinated interventions. This has undermined 
the very foundations of the internal energy market, leading to revenue uncertainty and, 
ultimately, very significant negative impacts on renewable energy investments.  
As things stand, Europe is not building enough new wind farms and investments are 
declining:  
• Wind turbine orders are down 47% year-on-year. 
• In 2022 there were almost no final investment decisions in offshore wind.  
• In the first 11 months of 2022, final investment decisions accounted for only 12 GW. 
In the case of Spain, orders have been closed for 712 MW, when 2,500 MW need to 
be installed annually according to the National Plan (PNIEC). 
 
 
 
3 Public consultation on the European electricity market reform 
Some of the policy options presented in the Commission's public consultation document 
are challenging and could lead to even greater structural uncertainty for investors at a 
time when we need to accelerate the deployment of renewables as a matter of urgency 
to boost energy security. While we welcome the European Commission's ideas on the 
forthcoming Green Deal Industrial Plan, the Integrated National Energy and Climate 
Plans (in particular Spain's PNIEC) and on permi tting renewables to help accelerate 
deployment, all of this is academic in the absence of clear investment signals. 
A good Market Design reform is essential to reverse the current worrying trends and 
must: 
• Guarantee the freedom of all players to opt for the various tools or mechanisms, 
without limitations or obligations. The success of the new model will depend on 
providing attractive environments for investors by making all the possibilities of 
operating in the market coexist: short, long, with public CFD, with private PPA, etc. 
Each country has its own trajectory and approach, and the various tools have been 
penetrating differently in each country.  
• Guarantee Legal Certainty , ensuring that in no case will there be retroactive 
effects on the contracts in force, nor any impact on the sector's agents that could 
jeopardize the profitability of their investments. In this sense, it is necessary to 
confirm the certainty of investment in renewables and cement Articles 4 and 6 of 
the 2018 Renewable Energy Directive on the visibility of volumes and the design 
of support systems.  
• Ensure long-term regulatory stability of the operating rules that end up being 
agreed. Make clear the difference between the current conjunctural measures 
adopted by both the EU and Spain, wh ich should have an expiration date, versus 
long-term structural measures. 
• Include a Transition Plan from the current model while the new market design is 
being drafted. Therefore, requiring a testing process with sandbox environments 
in order to simulate t he effects, identify market failures, build confidence in the 
stakeholders and gather lessons learned for the proposed changes regarding the 
existing model. 
• In relation to renewable technologies - which must gradually replace fossil fuels - 
the market design must give signals that make investment in renewable energy 
more appealing compared to investing in technologies in the phase-out stage of 
the mixes , by offering more attractive profitability schemes. Nor should one 
renewable technology be favored over another unless it is for technical reasons of 
contribution to the system or added value to the local economy.

Q1. Do you consider the use of PPAs as an efficient way to mitigate the impact of short-
term markets on the price of electricity paid by the consumer, including industrial 
consumers? 
Yes. It is essential to maintain both PPAs and CFDs as voluntary options for develop ers 
seeking financing for their renewable installation projects so that there is a competitive 
market. 
PPAs are a model that brings value to both the generator and the consumer: 
• The generator gets price visibility and revenue certainty, favoring project financing. 
• For the consumer, it provides a value solution adapted to their needs, visibility and 
stability of their future electricity costs, as well as a renewable and competitive 
supply. 
Q2. Please describe the barriers that currently prevent the conclusion of PPAs. 
PPAs have developed strongly in recent years in some countries such as Spain, so this 
model can serve as a reference for other countries. However, even in countries such as 
Spain, closing PPA contracts is currently an unduly complex process due to the wide 
range of factors involved in the conclusion of such a PPA.  The lack of liquidity in the 
PPA market, regulatory instability (since, although changes are implemented in the long 
term, they also have an effect on PPAs in the short and medium term ), the lack of 
standardization of contracts , and the d ifficulty of complying with the guarantees to 
cover counterparty risks  required by financiers , are the main obstacles faced by 
promoters.

Q4. What technologies should be excluded and why? 
Manageable energy sources provide the necessary price signals that indicate the optimal

Q5. What are the main risks of requiring new publicly supported inframarginal capacity 
to be procured on the basis of two-way contracts for difference or similar arrangements 
for example as regards of the impact in the short -term markets, competition between 
different technologies, or the development of market based PPAs? 
Mandating that infra-marginal technologies be subscribed to CFDs involves a wide array 
of risks that can lead to inefficiencies: 
 Slow the implementation rate of renewable energy : By only offering a single 
financing choice for renewable projects, any renewable projects requiring a 
different type of remuneration mechanism due to their own characteristics 
would be left aside. It should be voluntary so that those developers who consider 
CfDs as the ideal form of financing have access to it, but under no circumstances 
should it restrict investment in renewable energy for projects in need of other 
types of remuneration schemes.  
 This type of market intervention increases the perception of regulatory risk , 
which makes project financing more expensive. 
 Low price signals and distortion of the electricity market  by h indering the 
development and implementation of storage technologies such as hydrogen and 
batteries, which right now do not have sufficient profitability margins. Making 
use of the moments of low prices can be an effective way to boost the 
development of th ese technologies. In addition, if the price signal in the day -
ahead market is disturbed, the optimal dispatch would also be altered.

 Risk of reducing technological innovation , since a fixed price for energy 
discourages the upgrading and improvement of facilities, as well as investments

 Risk of inefficient design in Contract for Differences: 
o Having an overly low reserve price , so that developers may decide not to 
attend the auctions and delay the investment . See the case of auctions in

o Preferential treatment of some technologies over others: if the strike price

Q7. How can it be ensured that any costs or pay -out generated by two -way CfDs in 
high-price periods are channelled back to electricity consumers ? Should a default 
approach apply, for example, should these revenues or costs be allocated to consumers 
proportionally to their electricity consumption? 
CfDs must be voluntary for both generation and demand, otherwise the allocation of 
costs or payments generated by CfDs can distort the PPA market by reducing the supply 
of energy in the market while having to bear the CfD-regulated settlement. 
• In a low market price scenario , a consumer would receive a charge (direct or 
indirect) on their bill in addition to the cost of energy agreed in their PPA; 
therefore, it could cause their PPA supply to be uncompetitive. 
• In a high market price scenario, a consumer would receive a discount (direct or 
indirect) on his bill independent of the cost of his energy agreed in his PPA; 
therefore, he would have a discount that he "does not need" since he is not 
exposed to the market but has a fixed price. 
In order to avoid these distortions, it is proposed to organize secondary auctions of the 
energy subject to a CfD so that any retailer can acquire the energy at a fixed price and 
term. In this way, renewable energy reaches all consumers, including t hose who have 
more difficulty (size, risk profile) without the renewable promoter being affected, since 
the promoter maintains its contract with the regulator. 
Q8. What should be the duration of a two-way CfD for new generation and why? Should 
this differ depending on the technology type? 
It should depend on the CAPEX of the project, and it should be based on differences 
by type of technology. For onshore wind, a minimum 15-year period is proposed.

It is essential that there is some scope for purely commerc ial investments. This is 
necessary for electricity producers to meet their obligations under the PPAs. Producers

2.2. Limiting revenues from infra-marginal generators 
Q1. Do you consider that some form of revenue limitation of inframarginal generators 
should be maintained? 
Market interventions generate legal uncertainty and deter investment in new 
renewable installations. 
Therefore, we do not consider that a revenue cap on infra-marginal generators should 
be maintained. The application of the EU emergency measures on electricity market 
interventions and revenue caps on infra -marginal generators should end in June 2023 
and should be kept out of the EU market design reform. 
As ACER clearly indicated in its final assessment of the EU wholesale electricity market 
design (April 2022), interventions in the functioning of the electricity market in the 
current circumstances should be viewed with caution , and it would be preferable to 
address the root cause of the problem (current high gas prices) instead of the electricity 
market framework itself.

1. Introduction and Rationale 
 
The EU is set to significantly, and rapidly, accelerate the deployment of its solar and wind capacity 
through the Fit for 55 package, and even more in the context of the current energy crisis. However, 
not enough attention has been given to energy storage, which is a fundamental enabler of European 
energy resilience and the energy transition. 
 
In its REPowerEU strategy, the EU expressed the ambition to deploy over 1,100 GW AC of wind and 
solar capacity by 2030.1 Variable renewable capacity provided by wind and solar is set to become the 
backbone of the future energy system, with its share poised to increase considerably as the continent 
strives to reach carbon neutrality within this half century. 
 
Crucially, storage is a solution to the urgent need, as highl ighted in REPowerEU, to replace natural 
gas in its flexibility and baseload role in the short (2023) and medium (2025) term. A rapid deployment 
of renewable sources coupled with storage will decrease Europe’s fossil fuel dependence and 
increase the efficiency of its energy system, including through the reduction of energy price volatility.  
 
A net-zero energy system in Europe will require a wide range of different storage technologies. It is 
important to differentiate between long -duration storage technolog ies, such as hydro -pumped 
storage or thermal energy storage, and shorter duration technologies, in particular battery storage.2 
The two have different roles to play in enabling a high penetration of variable renewables on the grid. 
Long-duration storage can address all term needs, providing flexible solutions and firmness for short, 
medium and long duration level, in particular during those times of the year when variable renewable 
generation is lower; short-duration storage rather focuses on daily flexibility and grid stability needs 
to stabilise grids and match wind and solar generation profiles with end-user electricity demand.  
 
Decarbonising the power sector is the first action to take in order to enable a smooth transition of 
the energy system. The el ectrification of the heating and transport sectors will be more effective if 
green electricity is used. It is critical to deploy technologies that allow for an efficient use of renewable 
electricity at any time.  
 
With this White Paper, SolarPower Europe, representing the entire solar energy value chain, offers a 
contribution to the discourse around the EU action needed to achieve its ambitious Green Deal 
agenda and address the ongoing energy crisis. This White Paper focuses on the storage needs in the 
EU e lectricity sector and the solutions that support variable renewable energy deployment and 
European energy security.

2. Policy Actions 
 
To increase electricity storage capacity in the EU, we urge policymakers to: 
 
1. Set non-binding EU electricity storage targets for 2030, sending clear signals to investors; 
2. Develop EU -wide and national electricity storage strategies,  defining roadmaps to reach 
these targets.  
 
Setting a comprehensive strategy for electricity storage, including clear targets and an enabling  
regulatory framework, would give long-term visibility and certainty to investors, as well guidance to 
national and local authorities. Clear objectives and actions on electricity storage would also drive 
positive policy change in relevant EU and national legislation, for instance in State Aid guidelines. The 
importance of a clear and stable regulatory framework cannot be underestimated. Certainty will 
mobilise investments in storage assets. It is necessary to address any existing uncertainty in the 
regulatory and market framework to simplify project processes. 
 
The importance of setting storage targets has already been acknowledged by some co -legislators in 
the ongoing discussions on the RED II recast. The amendment on Article 3 adopted by the ITRE 
Committee of the European Parliament states that “ In order to facilitate further penetration of 
renewable electricity and to increase the flexibility and balancing services, Member States shall set an 
indicative target for storage technologies”.3

3. Electricity Storage Deployment Policies 
 
 
3.1 EU Policy Actions  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
➢ Launch an EU Electricity Storage Strategy to 2030 
 
We call on the EU Commission to develop and launch an EU Electricity Storage Strategy toward 2030, 
replicating the scope and ambition of the EU Hydrogen Strategy. 
 
The EU Electricity Storage Strategy should contain the following elements: 
 
• It should include a clear indicative EU target to be reached by 2030 and include a roadmap 
to achieve such target, including intermediate capacity targets and monitor the deployment 
of electricity storage capacity to perform corrective actions if needed; this target should be 
further defined in the NECP by Member States based on the potential, features, capabilities 
and needs of each region/country. 
• It should help design a regulatory framework that accelerates electricity storage  
deployment and should give guidance to member states on the actions they should carry out. 
• It should identify and anticipate current and future problems  of the system  and take 
corrective actions, including through the provision of adequate price signals. 
• It should contain an analysis of investment needs and concrete actions to facilitate access to 
relevant funding.  
• It should explore the synergies with the EU Strategy for Batteries  for the growth of 
stationary storage manufacturing capacity in the EU.  
• It should explore and promote the potential of fast development of long duration storage 
capacity in the EU. 
• It should address research and innovation and supply chain challenges and needs. 
 
 
➢ Set a non-binding electricity storage target of at least 200 GW 
 
The electricity storage target should be framed as an aspirational target to stimulate the industry and 
set investment signals; it should also help energy regulating authorities and system operators 
understand the potential volumes of electricity storage deployment through 2030 and beyond. We 
directly observe the positive impact of renewable energy targets on the uptake of renewable energy 
technologies – an EU-wide electricity storage target would speed up technological deployment in the 
storage sector.

The targeted electricity storage capacity should be b ased on system needs. It should provide 
sufficient storage to cover 100% of the electricity demand with renewables in periods of the year 
when renewable electricity generation is higher (spring, summer and autumn) and significantly 
reduce gas dependence fo r electricity generation in winter. It should aim to minimise renewable 
curtailment and shift the load to the hours of peak demand in a cost-efficient manner.  
 
The target should be based on the 2030 variable renewable capacity expressed in REPowerEU: 600 
GWAC solar and 510 GWAC wind. Together, they total 1,110 GWAC of variable renewable capacity by 
2030.  
 
We can frame the target by looking at how national authorities have included flexibility requirements 
in hybrid renewable energy source (RES) tenders 4 in Europe and globally. The experience gathered 
from hybrid RES tenders indicates that the storage power requirement is typically 20 -25% of 
renewable capacity at grid level. At the national level, the Italian TSO Terna recently carried out a first 
assessment on storage needs for 2030.5 The storage capacity needs are estimated to be at around 15 
GW, which is about 15% of the 101.5 GW of total variable renewable capacity, and about 21% of the 
newly installed variable RES capacity. 
 
Looking at the broader pic ture, SolarPower Europe’s 100% Renewable Europe study with LUT 
University, which models carbon-neutral and RES-based energy system pathways in Europe through 
2050, indicates storage power ratios in the range of 15% compared to variable renewable capacity.6 
 
Based on these considerations, we recommend setting a non-binding 200 GW storage power target 
for the EU electricity sector by 2030 , which corresponds to roughly 18% of the variable renewable 
capacity targeted in REPowerEU by the same year. The target should be included in the EU Electricity 
Storage Strategy for 2030. A 200 GW target is a no-regret milestone for a cost-efficient energy system 
based on renewable electricity. This target should be further detailed in NECPs by member states 
based on the pot ential, features, capabilities and needs of each region/country. A 200 GW storage 
target by 2030 is aligned with the overall capacity requirements in the existing literature, considering 
current EU wind and solar targets have been significantly raised with REPowerEU.7  
 
Setting storage duration targets is more complex, since this dimension is significantly influenced by 
the intended usage of the storage device and thus depend on the characteristics of the energy 
system in each member state. Storage duration needs can be quite different depending on a number 
of factors.8

Against this background, it is important not to impose a priori duration requirements on storage 
assets, whose business case might be negatively impacted by strict duration obligations (longer 
duration is usually more CAPEX intensive while providing a dditional flexibility and firmness to the 
system). This will make it easier for investors to build a diverse range of assets to meet market 
requirements.  
 
Given that today there is no clear visibility on EU -wide future storage duration needs, we signal th e 
urgency of carrying out a deep technical evaluation of future EU energy system needs  in different 
areas and applications. We recommend that storage duration targets are set at the national level, 
based on member states’ characteristics and flexibility needs (see Section 3.2). Further, the EU should 
revise its long-term energy modelling scenarios, reflecting the new level of variable RES ambition in 
its modelling tool and the corresponding energy system needs.  
 
The main obstacle for storage – and particularly long duration storage – is making projects profitable: 
energy storage reduces total system costs, but individual projects are unable to capture enough 
revenues in today’s markets. New mechanisms will have to be developed to compensate the value 
of energy storage duration. 
 
 
➢ Make Efficient use of EU Funds 
 
In its REPowerEU strategy, the European Commission proposed that €10 billion should be allocated 
to accelerate storage deployment. 9 This budget should be used to support electricity storage 
deployment in the short and medium term and contribute to the achievement of the storage targets.  
 
In addition to this, Europe should make use of existing EU funds, such as the Innovation Fund and the 
Modernisation Fund, to accelerate the deployment of electricity storage technologies. To improve 
the role of storage in the context of the Innovation Fund, it is recommended to consider innovation 
in terms of business-case innovation. In other words, the ‘innovation’ value of a project bid wouldn’t 
just be evaluated with respect to the specific technology, but could consider the way the technology 
is used. In addition, the calculation of GHG emission reduction should be amended to better reflect 
the benefits provided by storage technologies. The GHG emission reduction calculation should be 
based on GHG emissions avoided in the local grid, including through the accounting of avoided 
curtailment.

➢ Allow co-located storage to charge from the grid  
 
The urgent need to reduce our reliance on natural gas opens the question of the provision of grid 
flexibility services too. Today, these services are mainly carried out by gas-based resources. However, 
storage facilities are capable of providing such services with immediate response.  
 
A number of important barriers preventing storage from providing grid services, including the issue 
of double charges and the possibility to stack multiple services at the same time, have been addressed 
in the Clean Energy Package.10 However, even with the possibility of providing multiple services and 
without double taxation, a crucial roadblock remains – the possibility for behind-the-meter (BTM) co-
located storage that is c onsidered part of a RES installation benefitting from a support scheme to 
charge from the grid.  
 
At present, a BTM co -located storage that is part of a RES generation unit that receives financial 
support (e.g., under a Feed -in Tariff scheme) is prohibited  to withdraw electricity from the grid. 11 
Thus, under the current rules such BTM co -located storage unit cannot provide grid services that 
involve charging from the grid, such as bi-directional frequency response. This would be justified by 
the need to (a) separate green and grey electricity in the storage device (i.e., grey electricity cannot 
be subsidised under a RES support scheme), (b) avoid the risk of distorting the settlement of the RES 
support scheme (e.g., risk of underestimating the market revenues corresponding to the RES energy 
and thus overestimating the regulated premium), and (c) ensure the maximum utilisation of the RES 
installation (i.e., avoid the risk of the additional revenues from the storage operation undermining 
the incentive to produce as much RES energy as possible). 
 
In principle, all these issues could be avoided by opting for an independent co -located storage, 
whereby the co-located storage and the RES generation share the evacuation line but are different 
installations in terms of  connection, metering equipment and schedule with the DSO/TSO. This 
essentially means two different units for the system, which can each benefit from different forms of 
State Aid.12 
 
In any case, it could be possible to allow a BTM co -located storage that is part of a RES generation 
unit to withdraw energy from the grid, thus optimising its value for the system and improving its 
business case. This would require regulatory arrangements tackling the risks previously described:

3.2 Member State Policy Actions 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
➢ Promote storage through hybrid RES auctions 
 
Hybrid renewable auctions are public tenders supporting the deployment of renewables paired with 
storage. These tenders are designed in a way that enhances the flexibility performance of one or 
several variable renewable energy sources through the coupling of storage capacity with renewable 
capacity. Thanks to their design, hybrid tenders can lead to the construction of hybrid RES plants 
whereby more than one renewable generating technology – for example, wind and solar – is present 
at the same time. Hybrid  RES plants have a number of advantages, including cable pooling, which 
allows for a more efficient use of grid connection exploiting the synergies of complementary 
generation profiles.

When planning hybrid tenders, member states should consider the following: 
- Allow stacking of multiple services.  Make sure that storage is able to fully function on all 
electricity and ancillary services markets. This allows the plant to access different revenue 
streams, and, in turn, reduces the overall cost because it allows for lower auction results. 
- Set the possibility to include storage in the renewable bids as a bonus feature . The tender 
could consider the inclusion of storage capacity (locally or in a different location) to provide 
flexibility to the system as a bonus in the evaluation of the proposals.  
- Include location requirements to maximise benefits . Choosing the right location will allow 
to address grid congestion issues. 
- Ensure streamlined procedures for new and retrofitted storage . Administrative barriers to 
the installation of new storage systems or to  the hybridisation of existing renewable plants 
should be lifted. As an example, retrofitting storage should not change the administrative 
procedures for project approval.

➢ Promote storage through other financial support schemes 
 
Across the EU and beyond, there are several good practices at the national level on how to support 
storage through programmes other than RES auctions. There is a broad toolset of instruments that 
includes CAPEX and OPEX remuneration mechanisms for storage (e.g., Italy, Greece, California), 
capacity markets (e.g., UK, France 13, Belgium, Italy), and other support schemes that specifically 
target RES technologies and provide indirect incentives to storage (e.g., Spain). An overview of 
selected existing schemes is provided below. 
 
Remuneration mechanism for storage and Capacity Market in Italy 
In Italy, a new remuner ation scheme for storage capacity is under development. 14 The scheme, 
undergoing a consultation by the Italian NRA, 15 will provide long -term remuneration to storage 
capacity that is necessary to deploy in the centre -south of the country in order to meet RE S targets 
for 2030. The mechanism targets new storage capacity, whose amount is pre -established at each 
procurement round. New projects enter the scheme by submitting the contracted price they ask to 
receive in order to build their project. Following the s ubmission of bids, an auction is held and the 
most cost-effective projects up to the volume cap are awarded contracts. An important support to 
storage investments in Italy has come also from Capacity Market auctions (1.1 GW de-rated capacity 
awarded for 2024 delivery). In the Capacity Market, projects typically have up to 4 years to be built 
and receive capacity payments for up to 15 years. The assets are allowed to trade freely on the 
market, but have an obligation to respond over capacity requirements. 
 
Storage Contracts for Difference in Greece 
The EU Commission recently approved, under EU State Aid rules, a Greek measure to support the 
construction and operation of 900 MW storage facilities in the electricity system.16 The measure will 
be partly funded by the RRF, following the Commission's positive assessment of the Greek Recovery 
and Resilience Plan and its adoption by the EU Council. The award of the contracts to the selected 
projects should take place before the end of 2023 and the storage facilities should be completed by 
the end of 2025. The funding mechanisms includes a yearly revenue under a Contract for Difference 
scheme, under which storage developers are benchmarked against a revenue forecast by the 
regulator, as well as the performance of the b idder peer group. The mechanism thereby provides 
annual operational support payments to mitigate market risk exposure. At the same time, a claw -
back mechanism reduces unnecessary subsidy support while still ensuring the incentive for the 
investor to maximise market revenues.

Capacity market in the United Kingdom 
In the UK, the capacity market consists of an annual scheme with a 4-year delivery lead time, whereby 
capacity is awarded with a fixed price contract and a duration that ranges from 1 to 15 year s. The 
scheme takes into account the forecasted system peak demand, plus a buffer, and combines this 
requirement with the de -rated capacity of all pre -qualified new build, current generators and 
demand-side response in a decreasing -price auction. Under thi s system, contracts are awarded for 
the MW required to meet the demand and all units receiving a contract get the same price for their 
de-rated capacity. Delivery is checked through asset testing and verification, while penalties arise 
where units do not deliver when required, or cannot prove their market readiness. 
  
Connection capacity scheme in Spain 
Connection capacity auctions held in Spain provide an indirect incentive to storage uptake. In order 
to secure connection, renewable energy projects must get a high score in a point-based assessment. 
Around 20% of the points are related to the project having storage sized up to 5% of its capacity. A 
higher capacity is allowed, but grants no extra points. Due to this design, the mechanism does not 
incentivise developers to maximise the value of the storage asset. Although this structure does not 
directly incentivise storage through financial support, it effectively links storage development to 
securing grid capacity. Similar incentives to storage deployment are present in RRF grants.  
 
Use of Modernisation Fund for RES and storage in the Czech Republic 
In the Czech Republic, CAPEX subsidies to RES projects are available through the Programme for the 
provision of support from the Modernisation Fund. 17 Projects are selected via a multi -criteria point 
system, based on cost -efficiency and technical aspects. Co -location of storage in solar and wind 
projects provides extra points, which are determined based on storage capacity in MWh over RES 
capacity in MWp. Within th is scheme, storage capacity can apply to up to 11,000 CZK/kWh (448 
EUR/kWh). In accordance with the GBER regulation, however, storage facilities supported through 
this scheme cannot be used for the provision of ancillary services for the first 5 years. The  storage 
capacity can only be used for peak shaving purposes. This aspect severely limits the profitability of 
the investment in co -located renewables and storage. Without access to this revenue stream, 
developers rather opt for renewable projects without storage, which provide a higher return on 
investment.  
 
 
➢ Set Clean Flexibility Plans at national level  
 
Member states should develop Clean Flexibility Plans at the national level to evaluate flexibility needs 
and establish a support framework for short - and long-term flexibility. Setting up a well-functioning 
support framework includes improving the rules on the provision of system services such as firmness, 
frequency services and redispatching/congestion management.  
 
Following a nation -wide technical evaluation of current and future energy system needs, Clean 
Flexibility Plans should also identify the right mix of storage solutions needed to address the country’s 
flexibility needs. Clean Flexibility Plans should set national storage targets ensuring that the energy 
system is well equipped to meet energy demand with green electricity most times of the year.

Clean Flexibility Plans should also look at means to simplify and clarify administrative, environmental

In addition, the assessment of flexibility needs and the actions taken to address such needs should 
be reported in member states’ NECPs, under the energy security chapter. 
 
 
➢ Include the status of transposing Clean Energy Package storage provisions in NECPs  
 
In the revision of the NECPs planned for 2023, member states should illustrate the uptake in national 
legislation of the storage provisions from the Clean Energy Package, including on the defin ition of 
storage, the removal of double charges, the possibility of stacking different services, the storage 
ownership rules for system operators, and so on. If such provisions have not yet been implemented, 
the NECP should include a list of planned corrective actions to address this.

Long-term contracts  
The Commission’s consultation considers three different types of long-term contracts: power 
purchase agreements (PPAs), two-sided contracts for difference (CfDs) and forward market 
contracts. Specifically, the Commission aims to assess how long-term contracts can be used 
to reduce price volatility and thereby address concerns about affordability, while maintaining 
the integrity of short-term markets in order to promote the efficient use of resources. 
 
This paper sets out Statkraft’s experience of how these three types of long-term contract 
operate and interplay with each other, and with the short-term market. We would like to 
emphasize three key aspects regarding long-term contracts. 
1. A functioning forward market is the basis for risk mitigation in a growing PPA market 
2. Price variability is essential to release flexibility from producers and consumers and 
incentivise energy savings 
3. Contracts for Difference should not displace forward markets and commercial PPAs

Long-term contracts affect market participants’ ability to manage their risks 
Different types of long-term contracts will have a direct effect on the ability of producers and 
consumers to manage their risks. Future prices are uncertain and many market participants 
will want to shield their exposure to high and low prices.  
 
Long-term contracts are important for investment decisions both for new generation capacity, 
for new industrial consumption and for installations increasing end-users' flexibility and 
efficiency. In order for any market actor to go into a long-term contract, you wish to have a 
reference price so that you can evaluate the risks and opportunities the contract may bring. 
This reference price may be a market based long-term price or a internal long-term price 
forecast. For both instances, you need to have a trust in the regulatory framework regarding 
the long-term price development.   
 
Power producers and consumers use different contract types to handle their risks, and 
forward markets play a central role. New regulations regarding other long-term contracts 
arrangements have an effect on the use of forward markets, and in total reduce rather than 
increase the possibilities for market participants to handle their risks. This aspect is important 
to consider while designing the framework for different types of long-term contracts.

Reducing barriers for using forward markets will increase investments  
Market participants of different sizes and needs use forward markets to find the exact 
combination of contracts that suit their needs. For instance, power suppliers use the forward 
markets to purchase multiple contracts so that they can deliver prices to households and 
smaller businesses with a suitable risk exposure. With the exchange acting as counterparty, 
the other side of the contract may well be a large power producer.   
 
Forward markets provide standardized contracts, which enable the development of a 
transparent future price. This provides a key reference point for PPA participants as it 
enables them to compare their bilateral agreements with a market-based price. Forward 
markets are also a flexible tool to handle risk. For example, a power producer will rarely go 
into a PPA for their full volume – as they will often want to keep some merchant risk. In the 
time period of the PPA, the producer still may want to fix the price for a larger part of its 
produced volume. Going into another long-term PPA may fix prices for too long, but the 
forward market provides the right type of contract for this producer. At the time of investment 
decision, knowing that there are both PPA and forward market opportunities, in itself thus 
reduces investor risk.  
 
For forward markets to work well, liquidity is essential. In later years, the liquidity in the 
Nordic forward markets have been reduced. One reason for this is that after the financial 
crisis, bank guarantees can no longer be used as collateral. Statkraft recommends that bank 
guarantees should again be allowed, as the last decade has seen financial regulations 
aiming at a stronger resilience in the financial sector. All our recommendations for forward 
markets are featured in Figure 2 below. 
 
PPAs use the forward market for reference and are an important risk mitigating tool 
for large industry and power producers 
PPAs can be tailor-made to the specification of the consumer and producer. For example, it 
may be attractive for some industrial consumers for their agreed power price to follow the 
price of the commodity they themselves are producing. These tailor-made features 
differentiate them from the standardized products traded in forward markets. However, while 
PPAs provide an important product, they are not suited to all parties. For instance, 
households and smaller businesses neither consume large enough volumes, nor do they 
normally want to fix their prices, for the duration of most PPAs.  
 
Since PPAs are tailor made, it is essential that there is a flexibility to form them according to 
the needs of the customer. Statkraft recommends that elements of PPA contracts should not 
be standardized through regulations. All our recommendations for PPAs are featured in 
Figure 2 below.  
 
Two-sided CfDs should be designed to so that they form part of a diverse mix of long-
term contracts performing different but complementary functions   
In a CfD the producer’s counterparty is a government entity. As such, it is a tool that can 
deliver risk relief where the market is unable to provide this and enable investment that might 
not otherwise have been possible. At the same time, when the government takes over a 
significant proportion of the producer’s risk, there can be less need for the producer to use 
other risk mitigating instruments, like forward markets or PPAs.  
 
That being said, while the government takes over some risk, the upside of a CfD being two-
sided is that it may also reap rewards if wholesale prices go above the agreed strike-price.  
 
Relying entirely on CfDs to promote investment in renewables would be a mistake. It would 
require governments to take on considerable risk and would require a single entity to perform

Long-term and short-term markets must work together  
While the consultation discusses different types of long-term contracts the possible effect of 
long-term markets on short-term wholesale prices is not discussed in detail. We believe this 
effect is essential to consider when making guidelines for long-term contracts, since making 
the right design choices can ensure that these effects are avoided.  
 
Different types of long-term contracts can affect which technologies are developed. In 
addition, their design may affect short-term consumption and dispatch decisions for 
technologies with energy storage possibilities. Therefore, getting the design right, so that 
short-term decisions are unaffected by the design of long-term contracts is fundamental for 
the efficiency of the system.  
 
Firstly, measures altering the long-term price signals will have an impact on investments that 
will determine future generation and load capacity that will ultimately affect short-term 
wholesale prices. For instance, if new permanent measures disincentivise investments in 
demand response, consumers will be less flexible than they would have been and short-term 
prices will be higher as a consequence.  
 
Secondly, measures that alter long-term price signals can affect dispatch and consumption 
decisions. For example, if long-term price signals entail a fixed price for an unlimited volume 
of electricity, the incentive on consumers to respond to changes in short-term supply and 
demand will be reduced. This may inadvertently fuel high electricity prices as pressure 
remains on supply. See Figure 3 for an example of how fixing prices may reduce demand-
side flexibility.

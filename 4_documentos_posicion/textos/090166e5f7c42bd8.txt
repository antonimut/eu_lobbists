Key Messages 
1. Markets have properly signalled supply constraints during the energy crisis. They must 
therefore be preserved and strengthened: 
• Enabling continuous intraday markets will contribute to better balanc ing the 
electricity system by enabling an efficient dispatch of energy sources.  
• Liquidity in forward markets should be increased as they offer market partici pants 
hedging opportunities against short-term price uncertainties.  
2. Investments into renewable and low-carbon electricity sources are required to strengthen 
the resilience of the energy sector and mitigate the impact of external supply shocks on 
short-term energy prices: 
• The role of guarantees of origin  (GOs), facilitating the uptake of renewable power 
purchase agreements (PPAs), ought to be better acknowledged in the review of the 
EU Electricity Market Design. 
• One-way and two-way contracts for difference (CfDs) can also support the uptake 
of renewable and low -carbon sources but should never become the sole pricing 
instrument for existing or new generation capacity. 
• Demand-side incentives should be implemented to accelerate the uptake of 
renewable and low-carbon sources. 
3. Flexible and dispatchable sources, as well as grid reinforcements, are critical to ensuring 
grid stability, thereby enabling a higher penetration of renewables: 
• Revenue stacking should be facilitated and definitions for flexibili ty and ancillary 
products be clarified to accelerate the uptake of energy storage and demand 
response solutions. 
• Investments in physical grid reinforcements are urgently needed to limit volatility on 
short term-markets. 
• Capacity remuneration mechanisms are urgently required to solve  system 
adequacy issues going forward and should be put in place asap. 
4. Providing a stable and consistent policy framework ensures investor confidence: 
• Emergency measures, such as the revenue cap for inframarginal power producers 
should thus be phased out immediately once the emergency situation period has 
concluded, as such measures delay renewables investment. 
• Due consideration should be paid to the interaction between market -based 
instruments and subsidies. 
5. Enhancing transparency in energy markets through a targeted reform of REMIT will foster 
market efficiency: 
• Disclosure thresholds for inside information for power and gas should be introduced 
to ensure legal clarity and certainty and facilitate the firms’ compliance with the 
REMIT inside information disclosure regime. 
• Transparency should be further increased by requiring TSOs to publish Inside 
Information on flow reductions or balancing actions , as soon as the information 
becomes available.

Introduction 
The creation of the internal energy market is widely considered as one of the successes of the 
European Union (EU), bringing costs down and enhancing cross-border exchanges. However, 
the recent energy crisis has seen a surge of criticisms on the functioning of the internal energy 
market and the pricing of  electricity. As a result, the European Commission  (EC) is 
investigating options to review the EU Electricity Market Design  (EMD) in order ‘to make the 
market more resilient and to reduce the impact of gas prices on electricity bills while supporting 
the energy transition’. 
Whilst we do welcome measures to strengthen security of supply and mitigate the impact of 
external supply shocks  on energy p rices, we must warn against falling back into regulated 
markets and prolonging emergency measures indefinitely. Markets have properly signalled 
supply constraints  during the energy crisis  thus clearly demonstrating the need for more 
investments into renewable and low-carbon power generation, energy efficiency and flexibility. 
They must therefore be preserved.  
In light of the EU consultation on the review of the EMD, we wish to highlight the following: 
1. Incentivising investments in renewable and low -carbon sources will help mitigate 
external supply shocks 
Renewable and low -carbon sources are key to achieving a sustainable and secure energy 
system. Facilitating their deployment will enable the EU to be more resilient in case of external 
supply shocks. Ensuring t ransparent and efficient permitting process es and s trengthening 
carbon pricing will support their uptake but additional instruments such as power purchase 
agreements (PPAs) along with guarantees of origin (GOs) and contracts for difference (CfDs) 
are needed to increase investment confidence. 
a. Power Purchase Agreements and Guarantees of Origin 
Power purchase agreements are an efficient way to incentivise investments in new generation 
capacity. They simultaneously enable offtakers to be less dependent o n short-term price  
spikes even though a residual exposure to short-term markets always remains. 
The uptake of PPAs has however been hampered due to: 
• A limited demand for PPAs, often restricted to large consumers. 
• Relatively stable market prices in the pre -COVID era, which did not provide strong 
incentives to conclude long-term contracts.    
• A lack of long -term cross-border hedging instruments (Y -5, Y-10) and limited cross -
zonal capacity. 
• The insuffient creditworthiness of offtakers. 
• Relatively volatile EPC costs, creating additional risks for market actors. 
• A lack of harmonised approaches to pric ing the residual long-term exposure to short-
term intraday/imbalances. 
• Shortcomings in contracts, creating issues in terms of enforceability. 
Recent interference with the introduction of revenue caps for inframarginal generators have 
led to a high degree of uncertainty and have negatively affected the market for PPAs.

Various instruments can help to further support the uptake of PPAs, including having more 
proportional costs for securities / clearing in the longer -term markets , demand  pooling for 
smaller final customers, and simplifying the structure of PPA contracts and the associated risk 
transfers contained within. 
More importantly, further facilitating and harmoni sing the issuance of GOs at EU level will 
support the uptake of PPAs from a demand -side perspective. The importance of GOs needs 
to be better acknowledged in the review of the EU Electricity Market Design. 
b. Contracts for difference 
Contracts for difference are an effective instrument to incentivise investments in new capacity 
as they increase investor confidence by providing a stable minimum revenue. 
All technologies required to meet the EU 2050 climate goals could be eligible for contracts for 
difference or similar arrangements. These would include a.o. renewable and low -carbon 
electricity, hydrogen but also enabling technologies , such as C arbon Capture and Storage 
(CCS). 
There should be no standard duration for CfDs as the duration should be agreed between the 
relevant parties involved.  The duration should be long enough to incentivi se investments in 
capital-intensive technologies. For certain renewable technologies  or CCS, duration of up to 
15-20 years could be envisaged. On the contrary, shorter contracts could be implemented for 
newer, less proven technologies, to provide faster payback at the expense of higher 
technological risk. Once a CfD expires, generation assets should be able to come back to  
market or be offered to enter into a new CfD, if deemed relevant. 
To ensure that CfDs deliver the required benefits at national level , Member States should 
remain free to decide on the design of CfDs: one-way or two-way CfDs, technology-neutral or 
technology-specific CfDs. They should also have the possibility to choose other instruments to 
support the deployment of renewable and low-carbon technologies. By no means should there 
be an obligation for new publicly supported inframarginal capacity to be procured on the basis 
of two-way CfDs as this w ould mean a material change in market mechanisms, significantly 
distorting all market segments from forward to balancing markets. 
Whilst important for investment confidence , the impact of CfDs on spot markets would likely 
be more limited and even detrimental as: 
• The volumes contracted under such contracts can be (and often are) renegotiated in 
the spot market.  
• Price spikes for consumers are generally linked to grid scarcity or congestion situations, 
which could be further exacerbated by the rapid roll-out of variable renewable capacity 
under CfDs.  
It is also important to acknowledge the disadvantages CfDs can have. Beneficiaries of a CfD 
may for instance lose interest in the market price, leading to a so called “produce and forget” 
attitude. Furthermore, a CfD does not eliminate the price risk, it merely transfers it to a different 
party.

2. Further promoting flexible and dispatchable  solutions, as well as grid 
reinforcements increases security of supply  
Whilst renewable energy sources increase Europe’s energy independence, their intermittency 
can hinder grid stability and increase price spikes. Investments into flexible and dispatchable 
sources, but also grid reinforcements , are therefore needed to enable their integration in the 
energy system.  
a. Energy storage and demand-side response  
Energy storag e technologies, including pumped hydro storage  and run-of-river-plants with 
storage capabilities , and demand -side response (DSR) provide flexibility at different time -
scales, from seconds/minutes to hours, weeks, and even months.  
However, their uptake remains limited, mainly due to unclear remuneration schemes. Enabling 
revenue stacking and providing clearer definitions for flexibility and ancillary products, including 
inertia, could help mitigate this issue.  Following an impact assessment, new balancing and 
ancillary services, also of longer tenures, could also be introduced to ensure grid stability at all 
times. The development  of flexibility markets at DSO level  will equally  provide additional 
revenue streams. Most importantly however, energy storage  technologies should be owned 
and operated  by market operators only to ensure their optimal use and avoid undue 
competition. 
The following measures would further incentivise the deployment of energy storage and DSR:  
• Further harmonising power markets and making more cross-border capacity available 
for short-term markets.  
• Maintaining marginal pricing, which provides incentives to mitigate volatility.   
• Encouraging incentive schemes for co -locating energy storage with  existing or new 
generation assets. 
• Ensuring that Member States abolish final consumer charges for pumped storage 
plants, where they apply. This would contribute to better acknowledging hydropower’s 
societal benefits. 
• Accelerating permitting processes, which are often too lengthy and cumbersome. 
• Standardising demand response and aggregation communication protocols. 
• Fostering innovation in energy storage solutions and implementing incentive schemes 
for innovative energy storage technologies, including for sector coupling with storage. 
• Enabling net balancing for prosumers. 
Time is of the essence  as the development of new energy storages takes time : at least 18 
months to achieve commercial operation due to permitting and procurement cycles.

b. Dispatchable generation 
Dispatchable sources ensure security of supply at all times . They are required to mitigate 
scarcity and congestion situations, linked to an increased penetration of renewable electricity 
in the system, and therefore limit short-term price spikes. 
However, recent studies show that the required dispatchable capacity will be missing in many 
Member States in the coming years. Germany, for instance, is expected to have a power 
generation gap of 15 GW in 2031 , even under optimistic assumptions, according to the 
‘Marktdesign für einen sicheren, wirtschaftlichen und dekarbonisierten Strommarkt’ study from 
enervis energy advisors GmbH, released on 6 December 2022. 
Europe urgently needs a more harmonised approach to attract  investment into new  
dispatchable generation capacities to secure supply at times with low  renewable electricity 
production. Capacity remuneration mechanisms (CRMs) should therefore be introduced, as 
soon as possible , in the EU to ensure that sufficient capacity is made available across all 
Member States. Abated gas-fired power plants – with CCS – could benefit from a system 
rewarding availability  and the UK’s  Dispatchable Power Agreement could be used as a 
reference here. It is also important that CRMs are open to all flexibility sources, including 
energy storage technologies such as hydroelectric storage and DSR. 
c. Strengthening electricity networks  
Short-term price  spikes primarily reflect situations of grid imbalances. The integration of 
intermittent renewable energy sources in our systems has added to the stress on networks 
and therefore on markets. The Nordic virtual hub, for instance, collapsed after 2020 due to grid 
bottlenecks. While virtual hubs can be considered as a quick fix to existing grid bottlenecks, 
they are not sustainable in the long term, as shown by the Nordic example, and divide liquidity. 
Instead, grid reinforcements are urgently required to further support the deployment of these 
sources while limiting volatility on short term -markets. In addition to physical grid 
reinforcements (i.e. building new lines)  and as explained above, particular attention ought to 
be paid to market -based, cost -efficient solutions , such as energy storage and DSR to 
strengthen electricity networks. Changing the remuneration system of network operators from 
a CAPEX -based to a  system based on both CAPEX and  OPEX– considering flexibility 
provision as an OPEX – would encourage TSOs to procure these solutions on the market.   
The introduction of a new long-term flexibility product could also be envisaged. TSOs or even 
renewable producers could contract this new product, thereby enabling investment in flexibility 
solutions by securing part of the business case. Currently, flexibility products cannot be hedged 
and investing into flexibility assets is therefore a very high bet on future market developments. 
It would be particularly interesting for renewable operators to procure this flexibili ty as a 
response to the system instability they can create. This could be a way to level the playing field 
with conventional generators that are exposed to imbalance prices.  
In light of grid constraints throughout Europe, locational signals could also be considered on 
the occasion of the periodic reviews of the EU bidding zones and in full coordination with 
market participants. Nodal pricing and a further splitting of bidding zones should, however, be 
disregarded as they raises liquidity challenges and create s substantial operational issues. 
Focus should rather be on ensuring stability and robustness of price signals , as well as 
enhancing market liquidity and hedging possibilities.

3. Short-term and long -term market signals remain the best way to mitigate price 
spikes and keep the electricity system in balance 
Enabling continuous intraday markets and increasing liquidity in forward markets will contribute 
to the deployment of renewables. Above all, s hort and long-term market signals remain the 
best way to mitigate price spikes and keep the electricity system in balance. 
a. Day-ahead and intraday markets 
The day-ahead and intraday markets play an essential role in underlying supply and demand 
fundamentals, thereby signalling scarcity and congestion situations and contributing to 
balancing the electricity system . Through marginal pricing, they are also critical to wards 
ensuring efficient dispatch of renewable sources, such as wind and s olar, including across 
borders, the output of which can be forecast most accurately just prior to production. Provided 
that TSOs have the relevant systems and processes in place, moving the intraday cross-zonal 
gate closure time closer to real time could even further support the integration of renewables. 
Short-term markets must however be further harmoni sed and simplified across Europe to 
enhance liquidity and energy flows across grid areas. Enabling market operators to share their 
liquidity for local markets that close after the cross -border intraday market would  extend the 
efficiency benefits derived from cross -zonal market coupling to intra -zonal trade by enabling 
competition between different market operators operating an intraday market within the same 
bidding zone/scheduling area. In contrast, however, ACER’s decision on the methodology for 
pricing intraday cross -zonal capacity (IDCZCP) threatens intraday auctions, possibly 
endangering liquidity while adding significant operational complexity. We thus call for an end 
to this initiative. 
Finally, the role of day-ahead and intraday markets should be seen as a complement to other 
types of agreements. Indeed, all types of bilateral agreements, from over the counter (OTC) 
trades to power purchase agreements (PPAs) and off -take agreements, are n ecessary to 
tackle different economic strategies and factor in portfolio effects.  
b. Forward markets 
Forward electricity markets  also complement short -term markets by offer ing market 
participants hedging opportunities against day-ahead and intraday price uncertainties.  
Whilst these markets support investment in new capacity  by providing long -term visibility , 
forward markets generally lack liquidity. Unpredictable political, legal and regulatory measures 
but also overly high c ollateral requirements prevent the conclusion of long -term contracts. 
More importantly, liquidity issues are mainly due to a limited demand for this type of contracts, 
related to stable and low prices before the energy crisis. Incentivising hedging from the supply-
side is therefore critical to solving liquidity issues.  
The following instruments could also enhance the development of forward markets: 
• Issuing transmission rights as yearly products beyond year ahead.   
• Ensuring full firmness of transmission rights. 
• Introducing incentives for market making on forward markets. 
• Standardising contracts for renewable energy. 
• Extending the list of collaterals allowed to bank guarantees for instance.

• Guaranteeing stable frameworks to ensur e investor confidence and support forward 
markets. State interventions, especially when unaligned, must be avoided in times of 
crisis. 
4. Stable and consistent policy framework s, as well transparent energy markets , 
increase investor confidence 
a. Protecting market actors against frequent policy changes  
Frequent changes to the EU policy and regulatory framework , as well as, the introduction of 
retroactive measures hinder investments and should therefore be avoided. 
Emergency measures, such as the revenue cap for inframarginal power producers or below 
cost regulated tariffs for SMEs should therefore remain emergency tools, to be phased out 
once the emergency has ceased. More specifically, the revenue cap should not be prolonged 
as it leads to a fragmentation of the electricity market, reduces investor confidence and creates 
operational complexities , as well as , unintended arbitrage opportunities  due to a lack of 
harmonisation at EU level. 
If at all, the measure should be introduced in a uniform manner across Europe and should only 
shave extreme peaks on forward markets  in order to avoid distortion in competition. The 
recently agreed German price cap for gas, which protects customers from extremes while 
leaving the price mechanism widely untouched, could serve as an example for such a cap. 
b. Ensuring consistency in the EU policy framework 
Due consideration should be paid to the interaction between market-based instruments and 
subsidies: 
• Subsidies and/or the prolongation of emergency measures such as the revenue cap 
for inframarginal power producers are detrimental to the growth of PPAs.  
• The introduction of CfDs should go hand in hand with a removal of any revenue cap 
on inframarginal power producers as both instruments follow the same logic. 
c. Enhancing transparency in energy markets  
Finally, it is important to increase transparency in energy markets in order to restore confidence 
and foster market efficiency. A targeted review of the Regulation (EU) 1227/2011 on wholesale 
market integrity and transparency (REMIT) is therefore required. It should primarily aim to set 
disclosure thresholds for inside information for power and gas: 
• Such thresholds would create legal clarity and certainty and facilitate the firms’ 
compliance with the REMIT inside i nformation disclosure regime. Also, it would avoid 
publishing non price relevant information and hence make the disclosure regime and 
in particular the Inside Information Platforms (IIPs) more effective. 
• A 100 MW threshold would be appropriate , as confirmed a.o. by the ‘Report on 
Threshold for Publishing Inside Information ’ published by the Nord Pool Group  on 26 
April 2022. 
• Such confirmed power and gas thresholds – which could be checked on a regular basis 
– should be applicable in all situations except for extraordinary market situations such

ENERGINET RESPONSE TO CONSULTATION OF 
TARGETED ELECTRICITY MARKET REFORM   
 
Introduction 
This paper summarises the main messages from Energinet to the EC consultation on a proposal 
for a targeted electricity market reform. The prices and volatility of th e electricity market expe-
rienced last year is not the result of a market design failure – it is the result of a supply crisis with 
an energy policy failure on natural gas import dependency in the driving seat. This unfortunate 
situation was in the summer and autumn further escalated by outage of nuclear power plants , 
low water levels in the European main rivers  and an incident of unusually little precipitation in 
the Nordic hydro reservoirs, extremely unfortunate yes, but again, a supply shortage. Neverthe-
less, electricity demand was always met. In fact, the internal electricity market has been a tre-
mendous gain for European citizens and reduced the overall costs, while maintaining a high level 
of security of supply. 
 
While Energinet does see a need to future -proof the EU electricity market design, this is with a 
view to cater for the needs of the renewable based  decarbonised electricity system . We are 
already standing at the verge of this in the Nordic countries , and soon the new challenges 
brought about with such fundamental changes to the electricity system –  and energy system as 
a whole - will enter other EU r egions as well. To get the regulatory framework in place for such 
a grand transition will require comprehensive assessments and thorough preparation . 
 
Yet the EC has announced that a proposal for changes to the electricity market design in the 
wake of the supply crisis will be presented in March 2023 aiming at a swift enforcement by win-
ter 2023. As such our general recommendation will be for the Commission to limit the proposal 
to elements which are , if not directly speeding up the path towards delivering of the net zero 
system needs, then as a minimum can be labelled as “no regret” on key policy objectives under 
the umbrella of as cost-effective green transition1. Another relevant consideration is to limit the 
changes to the regulatory framewor k to not create further regulatory uncertainty, than what 
has already been piled up on the sector the past year, with the various emergency interventions 
and more or less radical proposals for structural reform of the EU electricity market .

GENERAL REMARKS  
We are critical to the approach taken in the scope of the consultation. The consultation includes 
a broad range of market design topics and questions, and it is not as “targeted”  towards ad-
dressing the political and social problem of high energy prices, as the Commission has otherwise 
stated several times during  the past 6 months. Depending on the scope of the actual market 
reform proposal put forward by the Commission end of March  2023, we see a risk of policy 
failure that can negatively impact the resilience of the IEM in the long-term, if key market design 
choices are “forced through” in an expedited manner.  
 
We are also critical to the silo-approach facilitated by the mere design of the consultation, which 
pushes stakeholders to respond to a number of detailed questions on various subtopics that are 
more or less independent, whereby the consultation’s ability to aid the Commission in assessing 
the relative importance and envisaged/realistic impact of the various themes based on stake-
holder responses is impaired. Perhaps even more problematic the consultation document ne-
glects the quite crucial inter -dynamics between t wo subtopics presented. In our view this is in 
particular the relationship between 1) a drive for fixed price contracts  and the role of flexibil-
ity/demand side response, which are basically in conflict with each other if poorly designed and 
2) what seems to be an equally dedicated effort to promote  changes to the use and design of  
CfDs, PPAs and Forward markets without reflecting on the fact that they to a large exten t will 
cannibalise on each other. 
 
Finally, we are, however, pleased to see topics and questions included that address important 
issues faced by the EU electricity market in the long-term, if the design of the market is to be fit 
for a net zero energy system, e.g., on the application and design of capacity remuneration mech-
anisms to address capacity adequacy and flexibility, the need for more granular locations signals 
to ensure efficient dispatch in a system dominated by intermittent renewables, and the focus on 
promoting market compatible long-term contracts to facilitate the necessary investments in re-
newables.   
 
So you want the cake and eat it too? 
Also, we see several  misperceptions floating in the debate related  to the tools presented and 
their envisaged and combined impact. 
 
 Fixed prices are  not the same as low prices – neither for consumers , nor for the energy 
system 
The high price volatility and as a result unprecedented high consumer bills over the summer and 
autumn 2022 have put much focus on the possible merit of fixed price contracts. While fixed 
price contracts have their merits in reducing risk, it should also be reali sed that this comes with 
a cost. Fixed prices are not the same as low prices. On the contrary, the cost for consumers may 
be higher in the longer run, in particular if fixed price contracts are concluded in a high -price 
period. Similar, PPAs come with the same additional “insurance” premium to the price the con-
sumer pays, which in many industrial segments has been acceptable when looking to other up-
sides of entering such contracts  in the form of green branding on RES -sourcing and price level 
predictability.  
 
Furthermore, fixed prices remove incentive s of consumers to react to prices, i.e. , moving con-
sumption from hours with high prices where electricity supply may be strained to hours with low 
prices, which could have improved security of supply and “cut off the top” of peak prices. During 
high-price periods the past year in Europe , we have seen that both generators and consumers

are reacting to price signals, which has been key to securing a high level of security of supply and 
thereby avoiding brownouts. In both the short and long term, d emand response will, however, 
not be able to fully replace the use of natural gas. In the future, we will see periods with little or 
no power generation from solar and wind. And in those periods, we need alternative (preferably 
low carbon) dispatchable generation capacity , e.g., biomethane . However, reducing incentives 
for demand response will result in even higher prices in strained situations and increase energy  
costs for society, because we will need even more – probably subsidised – dispatchable genera-
tion capacity. In that case, t he green transition will be less affordable, both for society at large  
and for the individual consumer. 
 
 Special treatment for some always leads to costs for others elsewhere in the system 
In general, adding regulatory components to the market design that allows special treatment for 
certain groups should be avoided. Enlarging the concept of Energy Communities by introducing  
reduced grid tariffs for some prosumers despite separate location of production and consump-
tion will result in cross-subsidisation and be a discriminatory approach, as non-community con-
sumers will have to cover the deficit in grid cost payments. This is not acceptable and often has 
the risk of a social flipside.  
 
The Commission also mention s the concept of Transmission Access Guarantees (TAGs) where 
offshore wind developers connected to a hybrid solution could get a right to a compensation in 
case of reduced transmission capacity by transferring a share of congestion income from the 
TSO to the developer. The primary purpose of this concept is to  reduce risk for developers of 
offshore wind connected to hybrids. What the concept does, however, is basically that it trans-
fers a risk/cost from taxpayers  to electricity consumers, because application of the TAG can re-
duce the required level of potential subsidy from Member States to offshore developers. Instead 
of financing this risk through the subsidy scheme, the risk will be financed through a transfer of 
congestion income, which all else equal will result in higher grid tariffs for consumers . TSOs use 
congestion income to finance investments in grid reinforcements and new interconnectors. With 
fewer congestion rents flowing to TSOs, application of the TAG inherently introduces a risk  of a 
potentially slower build -out of transmission infrastructure , which will impair the integration of 
renewables and thereby delay the green transition.  Furthermore, as such guarantees will only 
benefit developers in dedicated offshore bidding zones, it goes against the EU principle of non -
discrimination compared to other  producers that are connected to the onshore grid , where 
transmission capacity offered to the market is governed by the 70% -rule. Additionally, as has 
also been argued by ENTSO-E, transferring congestion income from TSOs to developers signifies 
cross-subsidisation between transmission and generation assets, which also violates a key EU 
principle. Finally, applying the TAG may reduce the TSOs’ ability to auction long-term transmis-
sion rights, as the congestion income would need to be reserved for the TAGs.  
 
SPECIFIC COMMENTS   
 
CfDs – Use this tool for the right purpose and design it well 
Energinet does not see a widespread or even mandatory application of CfDs as a viable way to 
ensure low electricity prices for consumers. This is simply not the purpose of t his instrument, 
which should be limited in its application to areas where M ember States want to govern public 
support for certain technologies . This could be some renewable  technologies such as offshore 
wind, but for years now, large amounts of renewables have been deployed without public sup-
port rendering CfDs unnecessary. In case some Member States want to force all their RES on to 
CfDs, they could perhaps be  allowed to do that, but it should not be mandatory  at European

level, and the Commission would need to publish strict guidelines to ensure that adverse cross -
border effects of such decisions are avoided.  
 
If applied, CfD-design must include the full range of incentives to react to the needs of the system 
(i.e., financial CfD – fixed volume, f ixed price) and be two -sided2 to ensure the best possible 
combination between risk mitigation for the project developer and efficient use of taxpayers’  
money for duly justified subsidisation. If the CfD design is not financial (but instead directly cou-
pled to actual injection),  it will leave the electricity  system with power generation that is not 
incentivised to be flexible, ultimately increasing costs for balancing the system at the expense of 
consumers, through increases in transmission tariffs. Also, reduced exposure to price signals due 
to badly designed  CfDs will disincentivise much needed sector coupling and RES -integration 
through co-location of renewable electricity generation and electrolysers – a combination which 
will be key to facilitate decarbonisation in hard-to-abate sectors, as well as to take the top off a 
foreseen explosion of infrastructure need.  
 
In whatever form CfDs are introduced, the framework of such contracts should at least focus on: 
• Direct and indirect impacts on market and system integration of renewables,  avoiding 
distorting price signals in day-ahead, intraday, and balancing markets 
• Incentives for generation units to provide system services, including balancing, non -
frequency ancillary services and correctly forecast their production   
• Competition between generation sources resulting in optimal price signals and efficient 
pricing of the CfDs                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          
• Limiting contracts to technologies with long-term revenue risks where investments oth-
erwise would not take place 
• Balancing the need for sufficient investment stability while avoiding overcompensation 
 
Forward markets – You cannot platform yourself to liquidity  
Energinet recognises that liquidity in most of the EU forward markets has been falling over the 
past decade. This is also the case in the Nordic countries where we have a long tradition for an 
organised financial market with a possibility to hedge bidding zone prices (System price com-
bined with EPAD - Electricity Price Area Differences). As forward markets serve an important role 
in the risk mitigation for consumers, short to medium term hedging for conventional production, 
and hedging of residual load for market parties with  PPAs and CfD contracts, it is important to 
understand why liquidity in the forward market is drastically falling.  
 
First, the current structure of products in the EU forward markets are base load and peak l oad. 
These products are perfect for conventional production and consumption. However, wind  pro-
duction is stochastic and is in need for other types of products which better matches the pro-
duction profile. 
 
Second, the forward market is impacted as production search for better hedges in form of PPAs 
and CfDs. These two types of contracts subtract liquidity from the forward market reducing  the 
possibility for consumers to find a good or even a price at all in the forward market to the extent 
they even need it. 
 
Third, as consumers get smart meters, the Danish experience is that more and more consumers 
move to spot contracts where they are settled towards the day -ahead price. This means that

Market surveillance – Regulatory push for comprehensive data accessibility and clearer assign-
ment of roles and responsibilities  
Energinet supports enhanced effort to ensure the transparency and integrity of the electricity 
market and to this end avoid att empts of fraud and manipulation in the market. To ensure the 
best possible starting point for ensuring compliance and increasing consumer trust, we support 
that the roles and responsibilities are further clarified, anchored, and enhanced.  
 
Specifically, we recommend that ACER more comprehensively and wholehearted ly take on the 
task and responsibility of providing assessments of possible market misbehaviour . To champion 
this task, ACER’s full cross -border perspective is needed to avoid gaps between markets and 
jurisdictions. ACER’s work needs to be further facilitated by easier accessibility to all relevant 
data. Therefore, Energinet recommends to regulatorily require that all relevant data – in partic-
ular – from the NEMOs - shall be released and compiled in the ENTSO-E transparency platform. 
Finally, Energinet notes that while ACER has a strong role in making  market assessments, it 
should be left to the national NRAs to take specific action against market parties.  
 
Stronger locational signals – Drive better application of current rules now and prepare for the 
future  
Energinet believes that there are many benefits to stronger signals for the location and dispatch 
of new generation and demand, as well as sustained and transparent price signals for  grid de-
velopment and flexibility resources. More granular locational pricing may represent a significant 
evolution of the electricity market design. Today, the geographical element (bidding zones) of 
market design is static and reactive, subject to lengthy bidding zone review processes. This may 
not fit well with an energy system in rapid development. Instead, if market design is to facilitate 
the transition to net zero as fast and as cost -efficiently as possible, the geographical element 
may need to be more dynamic and able to adapt to changes of the grid as well as the composi-
tion and location of supply and demand.  
 
But reforming rules governing market granularity in EU wholesale markets and other tools to 
strengthen locations signals is arguably out of scope of a fast-track targeted market reform. But 
looking to the mismatch between market and physics and the consequences  and associated 
costs already showing in the system today, Energinet recommends that the Commission takes a 
closer look to the processes governing the current zonal model and facilitate s a more appropri-
ate application of those. Specifically , Energinet suggest s that the Commission in their coming 
proposal clarifies and recommends the procedures under art. 14.7 of the Electricity Market reg-
ulation to promote a faster and more proactive adjustment of bidding zones.  
 
Capacity mechanisms – Review the timelines but safeguard additionality and create guards 
against cross-border distortion  
Energinet generally considers that the topic o f capacity mechanisms is of a complexity that re-
quires a more thorough assessment of options and impacts, than what is realistic within the 
scope of the targeted reform proposal. That being said, we appreciate that there might be a

Excessive Clearing Price Mechanism – a clever way to dampen prices in periods with excessive 
prices 
Energinet generally oppose any artificially set price cap in the market as it distorts incentives and 
hinders efficient dispatch and balancing across market time frames. However, should the Com-
mission wish to include elements in the targeted reform to reduce the market price in periods 
with excessive high prices, we propose the Commission to take a close look at the Excessive 
Clearing Price Mechanism developed by Energinet and earlier presented to the Commission. This 
model caps the excessive inframarginal rent and distributes some of the rent above a set thresh-
old directly to lower electricity prices for the consumers. At the same time, it maintains the ef-
fective dispatch order from the merit order list for renewable energy sources and still allows 
carbon emission rich sources to be dispatched for keeping up the high security of supply needed 
for Europe.

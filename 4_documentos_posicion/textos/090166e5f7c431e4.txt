RE-Source Platform: The new Electricity Market Design must leverage the 
full potential of Corporate renewable energy procurement      
 
 
Introduction  
 
The energy crisis has taken a toll on European businesses and families across Europe. In the Summer 
of 2022 electricity prices peaked at levels 10 times higher than before pre-crisis levels. These very 
high electricity prices largely result from a dramatic ally decreasing supply of gas. They are not the 
result of poor electricity market design.  
 
However, the current crisis has exposed the need for more predictability on energy prices, both for 
businesses and families across Europe. While the market has pl ayed its role in exposing a mismatch 
between supply and demand, the outcome of the market – high energy prices – has become 
politically unsustainable.  This has prompted  uncoordinated and at times counterproductive 
Government interventions on electricity markets.  
 
The current reform of Europe’s electricity market design must address the root cause of the problem, 
dependence on fossil fuels in the electricity system,  and help accelerate the deployment of home -
grown renewables to power the European economy.  
 
Corporate renewable PPAs  and other types of long -term contracts  can play a central role in 
addressing all of these challenges. They allow energy buyers to lock in competitive energy costs and 
provide visibility on revenues for renewables generators. They help accelerate the energy transition 
while meeting an important market need.  
 
There were 6.6 GW of corporate renewable PPAs recorded in Europe in 2022. Over the last 5 years 
Europe has experienced an upward trend in PPAs, with more secto rs (Tech, Pharma, Chemicals, 
Automotive) and more countries involved. But, for the first time since the emergence of PPAs in 
Europe, 2022 did not see year on year growth as compared to 2021.  
 
This is in large part the result of market uncertainty stemming  from Government interventions in      
electricity markets  and regulatory uncertainty , including the adoption of revenue caps for infra-
marginal generation and ad hoc taxes on electricity generation.  
 
The reform of the Electricity Market should resolve this uncertainty by ensuring clarity for investors. 
In particular, it should avoid interventions that would negatively impact the market for  corporate 
renewable PPAs. Rather, accelerating the uptake of corporate renewable energy procurement across

Europe is key to delivering on the EU’s climate and energy security objectives , and the electricity

1. Avoid crowding out PPAs  
 
Government-backed Contracts for Difference can be a necessary  tool to help deploy renewables at 
scale for Member States to reach their energy and climate goals. But Government-backed Contracts 
for Difference must not become the exclusive mechanism to invest in wind and other infra-marginal 
technologies. PPAs also reflect genuine and healthy market dynamics, including corporate off-takers’ 
willingness to commit and essentially finance renewables capacity and renewables developers’ 
successful efforts to secure market-based bankability of projects. 
 
Exclusive use of CfDs  would completely crowd out PPAs and merchant investments which provide 
essential liquidity and optionality for market players to have diversified and resilient portfolios , 
providing greater price predictability and enhancing long-term European industrial competitiveness.                          
Corporate clean energy buyers play an important role helping to de -risk new, additional renewable 
energy generation across Europe. This role can be expanded by safeguarding a role for corporate 
offtakers in contracting with renewable energy projects. 
 
We therefore recommend against Option 4 in the consultation document whereby CfDs would be 
imposed on new and existing projects. Further, options 2 and 3 should be examined with the utmost 
care to ensure that the resulting approach to Contracts for Difference does not remove all space for 
the development of PPAs.  
 
This reform of Electricity Market Design must not lead to the re-regulation of an energy market that 
has been liberalised and properly integrated over the last 20 years. This would drive inefficiencies at 
the expense of consumers. And expose investors to unmanageable regulatory risk leading to a sharp 
decline in private renewables investments.  
 
To ensure that the EU can continue to leverage the demand from corporate buyers for renewables, 
we recommend encouraging the adoption of hybrid renewables support mechanisms which require 
generation from a project to be backed partly by CfD and partly by other means. Drawing inspiration 
from the CfD auctions in the UK where it is possible to bid for support for a share of the total capacity, 
“partial support” CfD auctions could be organized, where a defined share of the total capacity would 
be under a CfD and the rest would be settled into the energy markets and eligible for contracting with 
corporate offtakers (e.g., via PPAs). Such a mechanism would avoid corporate buyers being crowded 
out by CfDs.

Alongside CfDs, PPAs, and other long-term contracts for corporate clean energy sourcing, it is critical 
that there is some room for purely merchant investments. This is essential for electricity producers 
to meet their obligations under PPAs. Generators often need to buy electricity on the spot market to 
meet their obligations to deliver power to the offtaker under a PPA  (i.e., “shaping” the electricity 
supply). This means they also need to sell some of their production on the spot market to have a 
matching volume of electricity.  It is thus important that the reform of the Electricity Market Design 
does not negatively impact liquidity in short -term energy marke ts, even as it promotes long -term 
contracting for clean energy.  
 
2. End infra-marginal revenue caps 
 
The implementation of the infra-marginal revenue cap measures under the Emergency Regulation of 
6 October 2022 has been fragmented and uncoordinated. The flexibility granted to Member States 
on how they implement the cap (e.g. level of the cap, affected parties, duration etc.) has led to a 
complex patchwork of national regulations that have created market inefficiencies and considerable 
uncertainty for businesses in Europe who hold PPAs.        
 
These emergency measures must end in June 2023 as planned  and should not become a feature of 
the permanent electricity market design. The cap has negatively impacted PPAs in several countries 
(see below) and retaining it would continue to have this effect.       
 
In implementing the 6 October 2022 Emergency Regulation, several Governments, notably Poland 
and Romania, have not properly factored in hedging or virtual  power purchase agreements. As a 
result, they are in breach of the rule whereby the revenue caps must apply to realised profits only. 
Renewables investors in those markets expect losses in the hundreds of millions of Euros.  In some 
countries, the way in w hich the inframarginal cap has been implemented has made it completely 
impossible to sign a RES PPA (e.g. in Greece), massively stalling the energy transition. 
 
Crucially, the Emergency Regulation has not stopped national Governments from making further 
interventions in the market such as taxing what is perceived by Governments as ‘excessive profits’.  
 
This is the case in Italy where, in addition to a revenue cap of €180/MWh on inframarginal generation, 
the government has adopted a 50% windfall profit tax on the electricity sector as part of its 2023 
budget.This new tax comes on top of two existing measures which are already impacting renewables: 
1) a cap of €60/MWh on certain solar and onshore wind plants; and 2) an extraordinary tax based on 
the increase in net revenues for the period 1 October 2021 - 30 April 2022.      
      
Even when the Government fully attempts to follow the letter of the Emergency Regulation, this has 
unintended consequences. Germany is a good example of this.

As the EU allowed individual countries to deviate and lower the cap, Germany introduced a “flexible” 
cap for unsubsidized renewable energy projects, which ranges between €100/MWh and €130/MWh, 
due to the possibility of capturing safety margins of up to €30/MWh. Unfortunately, the cap doesn’t 
clearly exempt Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs). These meas ures were enacted through the 
Electricity Price Break Law on January 1 2023, and will have legal validity between December 1 2022 
and June 30 2023. The German government is currently exploring a potential extension of the cap. 
The BDEW (Germany utilities A ssociation) has been publicly very critical  of the system. BDE W 
successfully argued t hat its implementation required such complex IT systems that they would be 
technically unable to comply with it until March 2023.                                                      
 
Permitting Member States to implement a revenue cap has thus created a complex system of caps 
and taxes that are dist orting the market and creating considerable uncertainty and complexity for 
investors. The option for Member States to implement a revenue cap should be terminated in June 
2023 in line with the emergency regulation in order to restore efficiency and clarity in the market. 
Integrating revenue caps in the Market Design would send a negative signal to renewable energy 
investors and corporates who seek to hedge their electricity costs via PPAs. 
 
3. Enable PPAs and other forms of consumer-led energy sourcing       
 
As acknowledged in the Consultation text, Corporate renewable PPAs have an important role to play 
in the energy transition. Access to PPAs should be improved for consumers to drive more renewable 
deployment and support European industrial competitiveness. While the EU regulatory framework 
allows the development of PPAs, the electricity market design reform is an opportunity to introduce 
provisions that could help accelerate their uptake.  
 
One of the challenges is to get more players active in the Corporate renewable PPA space on the 
demand side. There is currently a limited number of corporate buyers with the credit worthiness and 
the ability to engage on long term renewable energy procurement, though it is understandable that 
the long-term and considerable volume commitment typically found in PPAs may be less attractive 
or suited for smaller consumers.  
 
The consultation document focuses on measures that could help primarily SMEs to sign PPAs, 
although it should be noted that PPAs for larger consumers is hardly a solved exercise. For example, 
in electro -intensive sectors, PPAs tend to be concentrated in countries with large volumes of 
hydropower, which allows the RES electricity to be shaped at extremely competitive rates. Smartly 
designed support schemes (in line with Article 6 of the RED III proposal) that are aimed at eliminating 
the remaining barriers and mitigating specific risks of PPAs could allow both producers and 
consumers to reap the benefits of PPAs, without requiring large, disruptive changes to the electricity 
market design.

In addition, while there is currently nothing in the legislation preventing the combination between 
CfDs and PPAs, this is not explicitly recognised. This prompts investors to question whether thi s 
model is possible under EU State aid rules. We thus recommend that the possibility to combine CfDs 
and PPAs in the same project  be explicitly recognised in the final legislation on Electricity Market 
Design.  
In order to facilitate this, the proposal should also clarify that projects should receive Guarantees of 
Origin for the output covered by a PPAs, regardless of whether they receive state support.       
 
It is important to note that, in addition to PPAs, there are other instruments that businesses can use 
to source clean energy and provide an even better hedge to their electricity costs. For example, 
Carbon-Free Energy supply agreements allow consumers to source clean energy at all times, 
providing a fixed price for t heir electricity in every trading window, unlike single PPAs which only 
provide this hedging protection to consumers at the times when the asset is generating. This 
advantage is noted by Pexapark in its 2023 European PPA Market Outlook. Other innovative types of 
long-term contracts like this can also play an important role in helping consumers to insulate 
themselves from the variable price of gas. The market reform s hould ensure that consumers are 
supported to enter into any form of long-term contract for clean energy, not limited to PPAs. 
 
Combination Options  
 
Prior to responding to the options put forward by the European Commission to combine PPAs and 
CfDs, we would like to stress that we primarily call for the establishment of a robust framework for 
standalone PPAs, functioning in complement with Contracts for Difference. 
In order to enable the combination of Contracts for Difference and PPAs, the legislation should:  
- Ensure that hybrid CfD/PPA contracting is explicitly permitted: At a minimum, Member 
States should be required to allow projects bidding for state support (whether a CfD or other 
type of support) to choose to sell a portion of their generation via a PPA (or other merchant 
mechanism), while the other portion would be under CfD. It would remain the choice of the 
developer to decide whether to bid 100% of their project into the CfD auction, or whether to 
reserve a portion to be settled in energy markets or sold via PPA. This approach has been 
implemented successfully in the UK. It has the advantages of enabling the CfD support to 
benefit a greater number of projects, giving developers the flexibility to adopt a portfolio that 
suits their own needs, and ensuring that PPA offtakers are not crowded out of the market. 
The EU should require that auction rules cannot exclude developers from adopting such a 
CfD/PPA hybrid approach, and ensure that GOs are issued for the volume not covered by CfD. 
 
- Auction criteria that favour hybrid CfD/PPA projects : Member States could choose to  go 
one step further and introduce qualitative criteria in their auction design that give preference

to projects that have committed a portion of their generation to a PPA. This could be an

The legislation should allow various combination models: 
- Combination over time - opting out of CfDs to go into a PPA.  
- Combining CfD revenue for part of the output of a wind or solar farm, with PPA revenue for 
the remaining output. 
 
 
Recap of measures required to maximise the potential of PPAs in Europe  
 
The EU is embarking on a very significant reform of its electricity market, which will largely determine 
whether it can unlock the investments necessary to deliver climate neutrality. In the absence of a 
proper impact assessment, we urge the European Commission to draw on the experience of the 
energy sector, and corporate buyers of renewables, who will be central to delivering these 
investments.  
 
To that end we urge the European Commission to:  
 
⮚ End revenue caps on infra-marginal generation in June 2023 in its current form. 
⮚ Avoid embedding revenue caps as a permanent feature of electricity market design. 
⮚ Ensure Government-backed Contracts for Difference remain voluntary for generators and do 
not de facto crowd out PPA and merchant investments. 
⮚ Explicitly allow the combination of Contracts for Difference and Corporate renewable PPAs. 
⮚ Mandate national Governments to award guarantees of origin to every MWh of renewable 
electricity, regardless of whether it results from a Government auction.  
➢ Advance targeted schemes advancing the corporate PPA market through optimal and cost -
effective mitigation of risks, including for electro -intensive consumers, producing strategic 
raw materials which will be required for the twin transition. 
➢ Put in place instruments to  underwrite PPAs targeting SMEs and to remove the remaining 
barriers for other corporates (in line with Article 6 of the RED III proposal).  
⮚ Ensure that consumers are supported to enter into any form of long -term contract for clean 
energy, not limited to PPAs. 
⮚ Accelerate grid expansion to avoid grid congestion as more renewables come online.

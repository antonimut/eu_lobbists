Proposals for a long-term market design for secure, sustainable and affordable 
electricity supply 
 
We welcome the opportunity to comment on the important topic of electricity market 
design and its possible reforms.  
In principle, many features of the current market design are, in our view, proven and 
worth preserving. The European single electricity market is a major achievement of the 
European Union and the largest market for electricity of the world. The existing short-
term markets guarantee optimal and efficient use of available resources such as power 
plants, storage facilities and cross border interconnections.  
From our point of view, the main reason for high electricity prices in the last year was a 
too fast and unforeseen price increase of natural gas, caused by the Russian war of 
aggression in the Ukraine. Due to the speed of the price increase, the European 
electricity generation fleet could not evolve fast enough to adapt to the changing 
commodity prices.  
A future market design should be able to react more resiliently to such external shocks - 
but at the same time maintain the efficiency of the current short-time market design. 
Meanwhile, the current market design must be supplemented with elements that 
safeguard investments in both renewable energy and CO2-free firm capacity.  
 
Why new market design elements are necessary 
The competitive spot market, in conjunction with European market coupling, forward 
markets and emissions trading, is the core element of an efficient market design. It 
enables efficient European power plant deployment (short-term dispatch), financial 
hedging and implements the European requirements for CO2 reduction by means of 
appropriate pricing.  
However, further elements of the market design are necessary, especially if a CO2-
neutral electricity system is already targeted in the medium term by 2035, as Germany 
for example does. Such elements, especially the safeguarding of investments in 
renewable electricity generation as well as in CO2 -free secured power (e.g. gas power 
plants based on renewable hydrogen) through bilateral Contracts for Difference (CfD) 
and Capacity Remuneration Mechanisms (CRM), have already been implemented to 
varying degrees in European countries. In contrast, other countries, including Germany, 
are still essentially adhering to an energy-only market design, supplemented by support 
mechanisms for renewable energies.  
 
The challenge: An energy-only market design cannot guarantee a secure, 
sustainable and affordable energy supply 
Essentially, there are three reasons why an energy-only market design cannot guarantee 
a secure, sustainable and affordable energy supply in the future:

1. Investment uncertainty 
 
The energy-only-market (EOM) does not provide sufficient signals for new 
investments - both in renewable energies and in flexible, CO2 -free secured 
capacity due to sometimes considerable price volatility and revenue 
uncertainties. Moreover, it does not ensure by itself that politically set expansion 
targets, especially for renewable energies, are reliably met. Nor can the transition 
from natural gas-based electricity generation to flexible low-carbon gas-based 
electricity generation be controlled solely by the CO2 price signal contained in the 
electricity price, as there is a risk that these clean energy sources will be 
significantly more expensive than natural gas plus CO2.  
 
2. Lack of robustness 
 
Marginal cost pricing is the basis for efficient short-term signals to all market 
participants, but it can lead to a rent distribution that is socially unacceptable 
and provokes market interventions that further increase the already inherent 
revenue uncertainty of the energy-only market, as it was the case in 2022.  
 
3. Lack of local investment signals for system stability  
 
The lack of regional investment signals increases the necessary grid expansion 
and the need for cost-intensive redispatch measures. There are no locational 
markets that enable the efficient and accurate procurement of grid services such 
as reactive power and instantaneous reserve. 
 
These three challenges should be addressed through an improved market design. 
 
The proposals: Expanding renewable energies, Integrated thinking on security of 
supply and system resilience 
1. Investment protection  
 
Expansion of renewable energies: Well-designed bilateral Contracts for

investment risks for developers and supply chains and ensure that targets are 
met in a cost-efficient way.

2. Strengthening the robustness of the electricity market 
 
Two-sided Contracts for Difference and capacity markets / CRM reduce

shocks for consumers. The prerequisite is that the repayments from CfDs are 
used to relieve electricity consumers when electricity prices are high. This effect is 
stronger the more plants in the electricity market have a CfD.  
 
3. Creating local investment signals for system stability 
 
Power grids have a regionally specific demand for system services such as 
reactive power, instantaneous reserve and redispatch capacity. In order to 
adequately cover this demand, system services should be tendered regionally 
together with the system-wide demand for firm capacity. In contrast to 
alternative measures such as smaller bidding zones or nodal pricing, this would 
not increase uncertainty for investors, but reduce it.  
 
What would damage the European energy transition 
 
1. Prolongation of the revenue cap 
The revenue cap for so-called inframarginal technologies (mainly renewables) 
was designed and implemented in an extraordinary emergency situation. It must 
remain a temporary mechanism. A prolongation would cause severe problems for 
the electricity market and especially the investors’ confidence. 
The revenue cap is a strong intervention in the electricity market and the rights of 
the electricity producers. If this measure would be transposed into a permanent 
feature of the regulatory framework, the confidence of investors will deteriorate 
and the green transition will be severely harmed. In addition, consequences for an 
efficient dispatch and thus the proper functioning of short-term markets cannot 
be ruled out. 
 
Another downfall of the inframarginal revenue cap is the enormous complexity of 
its design and implementation. Even if lots of the implementation work for the 
companies will have to be done for the current temporary application of the cap, 
additional costs will occur in the long run. Unintended effects will very likely keep 
occurring.

2. Split markets 
Split market models present a false choice between the current wholesale market 
and a market which is “split” into a renewable “pool” based on long-term contracts 
and a separate capacity market for firm capacity to balance renewable 
intermittency. This is envisaged as being  an efficient way to “decouple” long -term 
renewable prices from fuel prices and to “insulate” the renewable investments from 
price cannibalisation as renewable penetration increases. These models are 
incomplete and fail to explain how the price for the renewable and firm output is set 
and how investment in firm and intermittent resources is underwritten. They are 
also silent on how the balance is st ruck between firm and intermittent generation 
and how the separate markets provide the correct dispatch signals both for the 
firm capacity to generate and for the intermittent renewables to be curtailed when 
there is insufficient demand. The reality is that the existing wholesale market is not 
an alternative to a “split” market, but an essential complement to the development 
of renewable resources backed by two -way CfDs and capacity markets for firm -
flexible capacity. The wholesale market will provide the efficient dispatch signals for 
firm and intermittent power alike and, in doing  so, will inform the economic trade-
off between investing in additional firm versus intermittent capacity.  
The split market models are also based around the concept of consumers actively 
managing their individual procurement of “as available” and “firm” power with 
long-term contracts for renewables and capacity. This begs the considerable 
question of whether decentralised procurement can deliver the same savings on 
the cost of cap ital compared to two -way CfDs. The recent crisis and collapse of 
part of the retail sector has demonstrated significant risk management failures in 
the sector and has left many suppliers unable to honour fixed term tariffs for one 
or two years, never mind underwriting a renewable or capacity investment for 15 -
years or more. Our current view is that decentralised split market models cannot 
currently be delivered without radical reform to the retail sector and that 
governments should continue to prioritise ce ntralised procurement of renewable 
and low-carbon energy and capacity as the most efficient route to net zero. 
 
3. Mandatory CfDs for existing plants 
Subsequent regulatory changes undermine investor confidence and will thus lead 
to less investment in renewable energy in Europe. Furthermore, mandatory 
participation of all renewables in CfDs would remove large amounts of power 
from the forward or PPA market, which will negatively impact liquidity and thus 
hedging opportunities for customers. 
 
 
4. Nodal Pricing 
Nodal pricing’s ability to optimise long-term investment decisions across the power 
market is complex and largely unproven even in jurisdictions that have employed it. 
Nodal pricing  alone fails to account for the efficient build of new transmission  
assets and is unlikely to prove sufficient to drive investment in generation and 
large-scale demand (e.g. electrol ysers) given the current need for parallel low -
carbon support and capacity markets to underwrite investment against the single 
energy price market. At the same time, n odal pricing introduce s significant

Executive summary  
Wind Energy Ireland (WEI) would like to thank the European Commission for the opportunity to respond 
to this consultation on the reform of the European Union's electricity market design to better protect 
consumers from excessive price volatility, support their access to secure energy from clean sources, and 
make the market more resilient. 
WEI is Ireland’s largest renewable energy organisation with nearly 200 members who have come together 
to plan, build, operate and support the development of Ireland’s onshore and offshore wind generation. 
We work to promote wind energy as an essential, economical, and environmentally friendly part of the 
country’s low-carbon energy future. As a leader in Ireland’s fight against climate change, wind energy 
creates jobs, invests in communities, and reduces CO2 emissions. 
WEI welcomes recent EU-level initiatives, particularly the REPowerEU P lan, and its urgent objectives to 
end the EU’s dependence on Russian fossil fuels and tackle the climate crisis. We particularly welcome the 
ambition and intent to  scale up and speed up renewable energy deployment, including  the proposal to 
increase the 2030 Renewable Energy target to 45%. The Irish Government has recently increased its 2030 
ambition, aiming to deliver 9 GW onshore wind, 8 GW solar, and at least 7 GW of offshore wind by 2030.  
WEI is committed to delivering these targets but warn that certainty and predictability in the market will 
be crucial for their achievement. 
WEI believe that well -functioning EU electricity markets are crucial to providing revenue certainty and 
investor confidence for renewable generators. We recall the ACER Final Assessment of the EU Wholesale 
Electricity Market Design of April 2022, which found that t he current electricity market design is not to 
blame for the current crisis, that it ensures efficient and secure electricity supply under relatively ‘normal’ 
market conditions, and that, as such, ACER’s assessment is that the current market design is wort h 
keeping. WEI believes that the Commission must be mindful of this assessment as it prepares legislative 
proposals on the Electricity market design in the weeks ahead. 
WEI highlight that the transition to climate neutrality requires a regulatory framework that makes 
electrification the natural choice for industry and households and passes on the benefits of renewable 
electricity generation to all consumers. Irish consumers, like those in other EU Member States, have been 
heavily impacted by the  extraordinarily high retail prices of natural gas and electricity, particularly over 
the last t welve months following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.  WEI highlight the need to support EU 
consumers and businesses,  particularly vulnerable consumers, during times of high energy prices and 
price volatility. Consumers must be at the centre of Europe’s Electricity Market reform. At the same time, 
the reform should keep separate how Europe delivers on the long -term goal of a carbon -neutral power 
sector at the lowest cost for society and the short-term policy response to high power prices.  
Ultimately, the most effective way to shield consumers from high and volatile fossil fuel prices is by 
continuing to support the rollout of renewables at the lowest possible price for consumers. 
Ireland has set itself an ambitious target of delivering 80% Renewable Electricity by 2030. The neede d 
policy frameworks to incentivise enormous levels of investment into the deployment of renewable energy 
technologies are now being put in place. Ireland has a strong pipeline of wind projects both onshore and 
offshore, currently progressing through the va rious stages of the development process with the aim of 
delivering additional capacity onto the system by the end of the decade.

These projects, and their financial backers require certainty in the market. They are the projects that will

put projects’ business models into serious question, and ultimately damage o ur ability to deliver on our 
2030 ambition and prolong the EU’s reliance on imported fossil fuels.  
Finally, WEI would like to outline its support for the consultation responses of both Wind Europe, our EU-

responses se t out clear positions on how to ens ure the market can provide the needed signals for

• WEI support the Commission’s intent incentivise the proliferation of CfDs and PPAs, however this

Power Purchase Agreements 
WEI welcome the Commission’s identification of Power P urchase Agreements (PPAs) and Contracts fo r 
Difference (CfDs) as important routes to market  for renewable electricity that should be  better 
incentivised to help shield European electricity consumers from the price spikes we have seen in recent 
months.  
WEI believe that PPAs are an efficient way of mitigating the impact of short-term markets on the price of 
electricity paid by the consumer as they  allow for the provision of long term contracts to ensure that 
entities holding such contracts are hedged against short term market volatility . WEI believes PPAs can 
play an important role in delivering on the EU’s renewable energy ambitions and offer an opportunity to 
create a win-win situation for several stakeholders: 
• Consumers benefit from reduced wholesale energy prices and savings related to achieving 2030 
renewable energy targets. 
• Renewable generators benefit from a more diverse range of options for a route to market. 
• Corporates can demonstrate true ‘additionality’ in delivering new -build renewable projects to 
meet Environment, Social and Corporate Governance (ESG) goals; and  
• EU Member State attractiveness for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) from large multi-nationals is 
increased. 
PPAs also can provide a strong investment signal for the development of new renewable energy 
generation and can aid the rapid deployment of renewable energy in the coming years. However, it is 
important to emphasise the need for liquid short-term markets which underpin such PPAs 
Barriers 
PPAs have an important role to play in the energy transition  particularly for the renewables -based 
decarbonization of energy -intensive industries. PPAs are typically struck between renewable energy 
generation facilities and Large Energy Users whose generation and demand are well matched.  As such it 
is more difficult to use them to cover Small and Medium Energy consumers who may be better served by 
utilities using CfD/PPA arrangements. Additional Barriers include: 
 
Credit Risk: Credit risk is the likelihood that the offtaker will be unable to pay amounts owed under a PPA  
and this risk increases relative to the term of the PPA itself. At present the PPA market in Ireland is limited 
to a very small pool of large corporates that have sufficient credit rating to be deemed acceptable to  
generators (and their investors and funders) for long-term contracts which are necessary to underpin the  
construction of generation assets. 
 
Term Risk: Many corporates looking to enter a PPA may find the duration challenging. In some instances, 
corporates may pay more for a shorter term or use other flexible designs to make the commitment work 
for funders. For this reason, any incentive scheme should be flexible enough to incorporate varied terms, 
e.g. Corporate A enters into a 15 year PPA for €60 per MWh for 50% of a generator’s output and Corporate 
B enters into a 10 year PPA at €70 per MWh for the remaining 50% of the output. It is recommended that 
any incentives should be flexible to allow this and also to allow the incentives to accrue to the generator 
or offtaker for the remainder of the term in this instance. This would encourage a replacement offtaker 
for years 10-15 or would cushion the merchant risk that the output will be exposed to.

Strengthening the roll-out of PPAs 
In the  Consultation paper, the Commission has set  out several measures that may  be effective in 
strengthening the roll-out of PPAs. WEI has the following comments on each measure set out: 
a) Pooling demand in order to give access to smaller final customers 
This possibility is most likely to exist in more  mature and liquid markets . While it does escalate 
counterparty credit risk for the PPA holder , ultimately the  pooling of demand could help overcome 
some transaction costs and allow smaller customers to access PPAs – these must remain voluntary 
b) Providing insurance against risk(s) either market driven or through publicly supported 
guarantees schemes (please identify such risks) 
PPAs offer customers the ability to hedge market price risk.  When these PPAs are used to support 
renewable energy generation within the EU, they also improve security of supply, increase 
sustainability, and reduce Europe’s dependence on Russian gas. 
c) Promoting State-supported schemes that can be combined with PPAs 
WEI fully agree with the  section in Wind Europe ’s response regarding this matter. While there is 
currently nothing in legislation preventing the combination of state supported CfDs and PPAs, this is 
not explicitly recognized, and prompts investors to questions whether the model is possible and 
compliant with EU State aid rules. The EU Market Design reform must explicitly allow the combination 
of CfDs and PPAs for the same project in view of their complementarity: CfDs guarantee price stability 
while PPAs secure off-takers for a renewable energy generator’s output. 
 
There are already cases where Government -backed Contracts for Difference are combined with 
corporate renewable PPAs. This is the case of the Seagreen offshore wind farm in the UK where CfDs 
cover part of the output of the wind farm, and a PPAs covers the remaining output. The EU framework 
should al low for the development of such innovative models which will ensure a cost -effective 
deployment of wind energy.  
 
But this also requires a change to the EU rules on Guarantees of Origin (GOs). There is no reason for 
National Government to withhold Guarante es of Origin for projects that have won a government 
auction. Each MWh should have a Guarantee of Origin, this will help innovation in combining different 
revenue streams from CfDs and PPAs which could lower the overall amount of needed support. 
 
d) Supporting the standardization of contracts, 
WEI agree that the standardization of contracts is needed to develop PPAs, especially where there are 
multiple off takers. This will be of benefit to all stakeholders involved.  
e) Requiring suppliers to procure a predefined share of their consumers’ energy through PPAs 
The end consumer needs to be able to avail of the supply best suited to them in terms or price, 
sustainability etc.  Mandating suppliers to purchase or procure contracts in a fixed manner is likely to 
decrease the competitive difference between suppliers, and thus represents a sub-optimal outcome 
for the end consumer 
7 
 
f) facilitating cross-border PPAs. 
PPAs will be struck against a market price, as such the facilitation of PPAs is limited by the borders of 
nation states but rather the borders between power markets.  By having larger power markets across 
Member State borders,  PPAs are automatically facilitated , providing greater competition for both 
renewable generator and end customers.  
WEI note that PPAs are underpinned by the existing electricity markets,  which have taken 30 years to 
develop through the use of price indexation, reducing risk to renewable generators and end consumers.  
If the current market design is to be substantially  revised, this will fundamentally undermine such 
contracts and seriously risk decreasing the roll out of renewables,  increasing price risk to the end 
consumer, maintaining the EU’s dependence on imported gas, and decreasing the EU’s energy suitability.  
Existing Generation 
The Commission’s consultation paper also ask s whe ther there is a possibility to provide stronger 
incentives to existing generators to enter PPAs for a share of their capacity. WEI believe that it is possible 
that existing generators could be incentivised to enter new PPAs through PPAs designed specifically for 
such existing units, which would avoid them acting to the detriment of the roll out of the provision of a 
route to market for new generating units.  
It is possible that there is a “win-win” scenario here where certain PPAs are dedicated to the repowering 
of existing projects (though this may be more suited to a CfD model, rather than PPAs).  This would ensure 
greater renewable output at cheaper prices for a longer period, though, as noted above, it must be 
ensured that this is not to the detriment of new projects and, as such, may be more suited to tailored CfD 
schemes, rather than PPAs. 
Stronger Obligations 
Finally, WEI would advi se c aution around setting stro nger obligations on su ppliers and/or  large final 
consumers to hed ge their portfolio using long -term contracts. By mandating consumers or suppli ers to 
enter contracts of a particular type , it reduces the range of options available to them.  By doing so it 
decreases competition and will lead to sub optimal pricing outcomes. 
Merchant Investments 
WEI agree with Wind Europe that alongside CfDs and PPAs, it is critical that there is some room for purely 
merchant investments. This is essential for electricity producers to meet their obligations under PPAs. 
Generators often need to buy electricity on the spot market to meet their obligations to deliver power to 
the off taker under a PPA. This means they also need to sell some of their production on the spot market 
to have matching revenue. 
 
Risks of increasing uptake of PPAs 
The Commission question whether incr easing the up take of PPAs would entail risks around several 
identified elements. WEI has provided the following answers on whether the uptake of PPAs would entail 
risk as regards:

Liquidity in short -term markets: If PPAs are indexed to short term markets this should, if anything,

Level playing field between undertakings of different sizes: PPAs will tend to be most suited to Large

Forward Markets 
The Commission pose several questions around the topic of potentially introducing hedging obligations 
on suppliers. WEI advise caution around requiring electricity suppliers to hedge for a share of their supply. 
Attempts to force hedging (whether by a specific obligation or alternative) may be counterproductive and 
involve additional cost to consumers from either too much or too little hedging . This would also distort 
market liquidity in ways which may not reflect the underlying risk profiles and appetites.   
As regards the creation of vi rtual hubs for forward cont racts complemented with liquid transmission 
rights, WEI b elieve that there is there is alr eady sufficient liquidity at many physical hubs, so creating 
virtual hubs seem unlikely to generate additional liquidity in and of themselves. WEI believe that the focus 
should instead be on improving competition between generators and suppliers and ensuring cross border 
arrangements, removal of price caps / other regulatory interventions. 
 
Contracts for Difference 
WEI welcome the Commission’s identification of the Contract for Difference (CfD) model as an important 
means of shielding consumers from the impacts of fossil fuel-driven price spikes that we have seen over 
the past 18 months. The current crisis has highlighted that CfD act as a valuable hedging instrument as at 
times of high fossil fuel prices, renewable projects contracted under these arrangements pay back to 
consumers. WEI believe that greater deployment of new CfD contracted renewable generation, for longer 
tenors, provides renewable generators with the revenue c ertainty they need for efficient low cost of 
capital investment, while at the same time protecting consumers from the worst impacts of geopolitical 
events driving fossil fuel price spikes. 
 
WEI agree that two-way contracts for difference or similar arrangements are an efficient way to mitigate 
the impact of short-term markets on the price of electricity . The fact that the CfDs are two -way protect

the consumer from short term market volatility wh ilst simultaneously guaranteeing the generator the 
route to market and price stability needed to realise a project.  
 
In the consultation paper, the Commission asks a series of questions about how to proliferate CfD’s across 
the EU electricity market, which we have sought to answer below. 
 
Should new publicly financed investments in inframarginal electricity generation be supported by way 
of two-way contracts for differences or similar arrangements, as a means to mitigate electricity price 
spikes of consumers while ensuring a minimum revenue? 
WEI believe that such investments should be supported by way of two-way CfDs, however, caution must 
be taken to ensure that the Market Design does not limit investor decision -making and sound financial 
planning, driving inefficiencies at the expense of end consumers. 
Mandating CfDs as the exclusive instrument to invest in wind energy would crowd out PPAs and merchant 
investments – both of which are crucial to the roll -out of new clean renewable generation capacity, the 
EU’s decarbonisation objectives as set out in the Climate Law and related sectoral legislation, and ending 
our dependence on imported fossil fuels.  
Additionally, CfDs’ potential imposition on existing projects would amount to a  transgression of the 
Renewable Energy Directive and an unmanageable regulatory risk that  could lead to an even sharper 
decline in renewable investments. 
What technologies should be subject to two-way contracts for differences or similar arrangements and 
why? 
Renewable generation technologies – this is to deliver a secure, sustain able supply of  electricity to all 
Europeans and to aid the delivery of REPowerEU, while noting the points in our above answer.  This will 
encourage the rapid deployment of renewable energy throughout the EU.  
What technologies should be excluded and why? 
Fossil fuel technologies should be excluded as such technologies decrease the security of European energy 
supply, as has been clearly demonstrated over the course of 2022. Such fuels are not sustainable and will 
fundamentally undermine the aims of REPower EU and the EU’s over arching Climate and Energy 
Frameworks.  
What are the main risks of requiring new publicly supported inframarginal capacity to be procured on 
the basis of two -way contracts for difference or similar arrangements, for example as regards of the 
impact in the short-term markets, competition between different technologies, or the development of 
market based PPAs? 
Given that the two-way CfDs are usually struck against a defined short term market price it is important 
that the short -term market price index is credible.  As such , short-term markets, which have been 
established and have functioned well over 30 years must not be interfered with or undermined. This is an 
extremely important point and one not to be missed.  
Two-way CfDs must support the aims  and deliver the objectives  of REPowerEU in being used only for 
renewable generation.  Concerns over security of supply to European markets have been acutely 
10 
 
illustrated since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and its weaponization of gas supply. Furthermore, Europe is 
acutely aware of its responsibilities in the transition to a zero-carbon system. Consequently, such CfDs 
should be limited to renewable energy technologies.  
It is possible that there may be competition between CfDs and PPAs, which will deliver competition and a 
better price outcome for the end consumer , as well as security and sustainability aims.  In less mature 
markets, government-backed CfD schemes may well be delivered first and can be used to kick start the 
development of PPAs.  Furthermore, CfDs may be better used to provide power to smaller customers, or 
agglomerations of customers, in less mature markets.  
What design principles could help mi tigate the risks identified in question 4, in particular, in terms of 
procurement principles and pay out design? Should these principles depend on the technology 
procured? 
Procurements should strictly be l imited to renewable technologies.  Such two -way CfD schemes are 
already common throughout Europe and can be utilised across all Member St ates using a simple “copy 
and paste” of best -practice Terms and Conditions. Such CfD schemes should be used to aid the 
deployment of inframarginal generation.  
Additionally, long-term contracts provide energy consumers, asset developers and investors  with 
certainty and reduce the impact of short -term fluctuations in prices. This duration of support should be 
fixed at 20 years as this gives investors certainty of a longer-term contract and will encourage investment 
in the field.  
 
Particularly in the current u ncertain economic environment, WEI believes that to stimulate investment 
and to drive down the cost of renewable  deployment, all CfD’s should be indexed against inflation. The 
purpose of a support scheme is to provide long -term certainty, removing price risk over an extended 
period. The more certainty that can be provided, the fewer the risks are that will have to be factored into 
auction bids. Lower risk and reduced uncertainty will have a downward effect on costs, to the benefit of 
consumers. Inflation over a prolonged period represents a massive unknown and introduces significant 
risk. Forecasting the future rate of inflation with any degree of accuracy is a task that even central banks 
struggle with. Given current market dynamics, without indexation built into the CfD c ontract, investors 
would seek to mitigate risk by overestimating the future rate of inflation. 
 
How can it be ensured that any costs or pay -out generated by two-way CfDs in high price periods are 
channelled back to electricity consumers? Should a default approach apply, for example, should these 
revenues or costs be allocated to consumers proportionally to their electricity consumption? 
The roll out of progressively larger volumes of renewable generation will, in itself, put downward pressure 
on power prices, protecting end consumers. ACER identified the need to shield vulnerable customers 
especially in times of high prices. WEI recommend that the Commission would coordinate  and facilitate 
the sharing of best practice  between EU Member States to ensure that lessons are learned from 
experience over the past 12-18 months as regards how to effectively support consumers. 
What should be the duration of a two -way CfD for new generation and why? Should this differ 
depending on the technology type?

WEI believe that a 20-year support duration for two-way CFD projects would enable projects to achieve a 
lower overall cost of capital. It is important to highlight at the outset that a renewable project has a 
different business case compared to most conventional electricity generation projects  in that they have 
high capital costs and low operational costs, making the recovery of the high upfront investment the key 
focus of any investment case.  
This leads to a need for a higher return to counter the higher risk of making upfront investment on the 
renewable electricity project before it is operational. Before an investment decision is made, a risk analysis 
is carried out. If the risk perception is high, this is reflected in a higher fee for making the initial investment 
capital available i.e., a higher cost of capital. The cost of capital is a function of  two key concepts: the 
Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) and hurdle rates. The ratio between the cost of debt and cost 
of equity in an investment, i.e., the debt-equity ratio, makes up the WACC. The lower the cost of debt and 
equity, the lower will be the required WACC.

The perceived risk for an investor in each EU jurisdiction is directly related to the auction design or policy 
environment under which the investment is made in the country. 
For example, the WACC in Sweden is significantly higher than in Denmark. This difference can be tied back 
to the difference in risk perception associated with both auction policy designs. Denmark’s near shore 
wind auction design places the burden of site evaluation, clearances , permits, etc. on the Government. 
This results in a reduction of required WACC as less risks need to be considered by the developer in 
Denmark. 
In an Irish context, as RESS contracts currently last 15 years, we see a large revenue gap towards the end 
of the useful lifetime of the plant, which is typically 25 -30 years in Ireland. After the end of the subsidy 
period the developer will look to utilise a forward price projection to estimate the potential revenues that 
could be earned post subsidy. The lev el of conservatism taken by a developer in this period can have a 
large impact on bid price in RESS auctions as the assumed revenues will directly impact their cost of 
financing which can impact on bidding behaviour.

be subject to a lifetime pay-out obligation? 
The question as to the optimal tenor of a schem e is considered in our answer  above; once a unit has 
completed such a contract the terms and conditions of such a scheme no longer apply and the unit must

It is logical that after the contractual obligation between a State and a renewable energy investor comes 
to the end of its originally foreseen application, the developer is free to decide how to manage its power 
infrastructure within a broader company por tfolio of energy infrastructure projects. Imposing lifetime

pay-out obligations on new power generation projects will drastically alter sound financial planning for

It is possible that post-CfD units may be able to enter further short term CfDs or PPAs at this stage, typically

Without prejudice to Article 6 of Directive (EU)2018/20016, should it be possible for Member States to 
impose two -way CfDs by regulatory means on existing generation capacity? If such possible use of 
regulated CfDs for existing generation is deemed appropr iate, should the obligation apply to all types 
of existing inframarginal generation or be limited to certain types of generation (and if so, which types)? 
As outlined in Wind Europe ’s response to this consultation, this would limit investors’ decision -making 
and sound financial planning across their energy portfolio and will drive inefficiencies at the expense of 
end consumers. Notably it will crowd out PPAs and merchant investments which are indispensable to the 
EU meeting its energy security and climate targets. CfDs’ potential imposition on existing projects would 
amount to a transgression of the Renewable Energy Directive and an u nmanageable regulatory risk that 
could lead to an even sharper decline in renewable investments. 
The discussion of market price interventions for existing generation capacity requires assessment under 
the same fundamental principles under which the interventions for new generation capacity have been 
proposed:  balancing investment certainty against consumer harm.    
Given the different starting position for existing generation (with finance secured and reasonable 
expectation of return at the time of investment) relative to new investment (still developing the business 
case in advance of the initial investment decision), applying the same principles do not necessarily lead to 
the same outcome for the different classes of generator. 
It is our view that  a mandatory imposition of a two -way CfD on existing generation capacity cannot 
credibly be implemented in a manner leading to equitable outc omes across all the different financing 
arrangements for those generators within a Member State, never mind across the Energy Union.  Raising 
the possibility of such imposition would create uncertainty in existing investments. 
Those with investments in existing generation are the same parties who evaluate the risks of future capital 
investments in energy generation and infrastructure in the Energy Union.  All future generation projects 
will become “existing generation” at some point, subject to the same vagaries of potential imposed policy 
changes, raising the risk profile of those projects.  
Under what terms and conditions could regulated two -way CfDs on existing generation capacity be 
imposed? 
WEI is of the view that mandatory imposition of regulated two-way CfDs on existing generation capacity 
would not be an appropriate approach . At the same time, howeve r, we reiterate  our support for any 
actions for strengthening the long-term certainty (at the investor’s discretion based on available market 
prices) under any strengthened PPA / forwards market regime. 
Additionally, the opportunity to allow for voluntary CfDs could be beneficial and is something for further 
consideration.

How would you rate and address the following potential risks as regards the imposition of regulated 
CfDs on existing generation capacity? 
Investment Certainty / Legitimate Expectation :  WEI contend that th e list set out in the consultation 
paper misses one of the larger risks, namely that the parties who are invested in existing generation are 
largely the same parties investing in new generation . Were regulated CfDs to be imposed on existing 
generators, new generation investment w ould be evaluated within t he context of an investment 
environment with a policy-driven legacy that overwrites the expectations of those investments. 
Accurately Defining Contract Price :  In Ireland’s Integrated Single Electricity Market  (I-SEM), there are 
three operational government -mandated support schemes (one market -revenue premium based, one 
regulated floor tariff-based with indexation, and one competitively -set tariff based with no indexation).  
There is a nascent corporate PPA market, which tends to result in highly bespoke, highly confidential, 
negotiated long-term PPAs with wider provisions associated with additionality and guarantees of volumes. 
Post-subsidy generation is frequently sold between parties based on the expectations of merchant market 
revenues (sometimes secured through OTC related financial hedges, sometimes through forward gas -
proxy hedging arrangements) at the time of the transaction.  It is not possi ble for any single party to 
“accurately define the level of the price levels envisaged across these contracts ” under reasonable 
timeframes without recourse to uncertain (for some parties) global assumptions of revenue.  
Locking in Existing Capacity at Excessively High Prices:  This is a significant risk, made more manifest by 
the risk of legal challenge of any uniform price or price -setting process which inadvertently and unduly 
discriminates too low a price for a particular class of generators.  
Impact on Efficient Short -Term Dispatch:  As a general statement regarding the proposals in the 
consultation paper, WEI considers there to be  insufficient examination of the volume risk for new 
renewables development within the context of network constraints, wider grid stability curtailment, and 
oversupply (other than to promote demand flexibility in generic terms).   
These are issues in the I-SEM already coming to the fore with our growing reliance on variable sources of 
renewable generation as we continue to decarbonise the electricity system.  Until the interaction of these 
CfDs with market arrangements for compensation for downwards redispatch is known, the impact on 
price formation, generator dispatch and interconnector flows via  market coupling arrangements cannot 
be known. 
Similarly, how such revenues are recycled to end consumers could result in dampening demand response 
from those customers capable and willing to do so under normal market conditions. 
Addressing the Risks: The risks are addressed by not proceeding to mandate a CfD on existing generation 
for reasons outlined above. 
 
Would it be enough for existing generation to be subject only to a simple revenue ceiling instead of a

Accelerating the Deployment of Renewables 
WEI reiterate that a vital investment signal for renewable energy is provided by having liquid short term 
markets in place and providing confidence for investors in these markets built up over 30 years.  If changes 
are made to short term market markets this signal will be eroded leading to a lack of investment in low 
carbon flexibility and storage  – this would be extremely detrimental to the roll -out and acceleration of 
renewable deployment, and so we caution against significant changes to how markets function. 
In terms of speeding up the deployment of renewable electricity, many of the key barriers lie outside the 
electricity market design. WEI welcomes the REPowerEU Plan and its urgent objectives to end the EU’s 
dependence on Russian fossil fuels and tackle the climate crisis. We particularly welcome the provisions 
in the draft legislative texts aimed at scaling up and speeding up renewable energy deployment, including 
the proposal to increase the 2030 Renewable Energy target to 45%. 
WEI also strongly support the position set out by Wind Europe that the accelerated deployment of 
electricity grids is an absolute priority for renewables deployment going forward. Europe has 
underinvested in its electricity grids in the last decade. System operat ors, renewable asset developers, 
technology suppliers and end-users need deeper cooperation since the early design stages to accelerate 
grid development of the power grid and, where necessary and appropriate, renewable hydrogen 
infrastructure. Permitting rules for power grids should be clarified and aligned between EU, national, local 
authorities so that the required infrastructure can be unlocked. 
Limiting Revenues of Inframarginal Generators 
The consultation paper questions whether a form of revenue limitation for inframarginal generators, as 
was introduced in the Emergency Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854 last year. WEI does not believe that

permanent revenue limitations can help the effectiveness and efficiency of the system. This would 
increase the risk profile of renewables investments, increasing the cost of capital which eventually will be 
passed onto co nsumers. That could also have the unintended consequence of shifting renewable 
investments away from the EU at a time of increasing global competition for skills and investment. 
The recent energy crisis was not caused by the current design of the market, but instead underlines how 
Europe has failed to deploy renewables in line with its own decarbonisation targets in o rder to be

Similarly, as regards the possible prolongation of the inframarginal re venue cap , we believe that a 
prolonged revenue cap would send the wrong signal to the market, risking moving investments away 
from the EU and into other parts of the world.  
In circumstances where generators appear to be benefitting from geopolitical impacts on the gas market, 
then other methods should be considered to limit consumer impact – such as taxation and rebates which 
can more accurately account for overall profitability without undermining fundamental market design . 
Prolonging a short term ‘fix’ beyond a short time -limited extension for the duration of the current crisis 
will lead to a range of unintended consequences across all the subheadings above. 
Limiting revenue on renewable generation provides a negative signal to investment in renewable energy 
technologies at a time when Europe needs it like never before to increase energy security and eliminate 
power sector carbon emissions. Limiting revenue is damaging to such investment. This is made worse if 
Member States do not act in a uniform manner which leads to greater complexity and a lack of clarity. 
 
 
Alternatives to Gas to Keep the Electricity System in Balance 
As regards the functioning of short-term markets, WEI echoes the Wind Europe position that short-term 
wholesale markets (based on the marginal cost approach) are very efficient in reflecting the real value of 
electricity at a given time. Short-term wholesale markets should remain the main mechanism for ensuring 
cost efficient power plant dispatch and settlement of electricity market contracts. The EU Market Design 
reform should safeguard the functioning of liquid and efficient short -term markets. The reform should 
ensure the functioning of short-term markets is not distorted by revenue caps and other uncoordinated 
market interventions from National Governments. 
PPAs and CFDs are struck against a market price index, as such it is vital that the short-term market price 
index is credible, otherwise such CfDs and PPAs will be very difficult to develop.  As such, short -term 
markets, which have been established, and functioned well, over 30 years must not be interfered with or 
undermined. 
WEI note that the consultation paper questions are included on whether the cross-border intraday gate 
closure time should be moved closer to real time ( e.g., 15 minutes before real time ). We take the view

that while exploring this move closer to real time may have merit, it should not take priority over the 
achievement of our overall policy goals – i.e. delivering our 2030 targets, a zero -carbon EU electricity 
system, and ultimately Net-Zero by 2050 – upon which the efforts and resources of the Commission for 
the purposes of this legislative process must be focused. 
WEI note that the EU also needs to enhance the availability of flexibility products in the market specifically 
targeting storage and demand response. Short-term market signals can help in incentivising flexible 
behaviour (e.g., balancing market pricing, peak shaving) even though there is room for improvement in 
their current deployment to increase the access to variable renewables. However, these will not be 
sufficient to drive all necessary capital investments in flexibility capabilities and new flexibl e resources. 
This will only take place if the right long-term investment incentives are in place. 
Role of the EU-ETS and carbon pricing 
The EU-ETS provides a price signal to reduce the amount of Carbon generated by targeted sectors, 
including electricity.  As such it can be used to provide a signal for the development of renewable energy 
generation.  As such generation grows it will lead to greater volatility in short term markets which in turn 
provides the market signal for storage and flexible generation, when combined with the ETS Carbon 
scheme this will provide the incentive for low Carbon flexibility. 
It must again be noted that this signal is provided by having liquid short term markets in place and 
providing confidence for investors in these markets built up over 30 years.  If changes are made to short 
term market markets this signal will be eroded leading to a lack of investment in low carbon flexibility and 
storage. 
Mandatory participation in the DA market 
Whilst mandatory participation prevents participants from exploring other opportunities, which will 
benefit the end customers, having CPPAs and CFDs which are indexed to the Day Ahead price will provide 
strong incentive for PPA and CfD holders to participate in Dy Ahead markets, thereby improving liquidity. 
Advantages and drawbacks of further locational and technology-based information in bidding 
WEI take the view that there would not be clear advantages  to the requirement of such further 
information, and we would expect an increase in complexity as a result. 
Enhancing the development of flexibility assets 
As the consultation highlights, investment in renewables should be supported through long-term financial 
instruments such as PPAs or CfDs , but there also needs to be a focus on the long -term procurement 
frameworks needed for essential supporting technologies such as energy storage.  
To ensure the secure, reliable operation of the electri city system at close to 100% System Non -
Synchronous Penetration (SNSP) levels and with close to 0MW of constrained on thermal generation, 
European Transmission System Operators will need to be able to access all necessary system stability 
services from zero carbon sources. 
Supporting market f rameworks need to be established to allow all inertia to be procured from devices 
such as synchronous compensators, frequency response, short term reserves and ramping services can 
be procured from storage and demand side response technologies, and voltage support / reactive power

services can come from a wide variety of sources including appropriately located synchronous condensers, 
storage, demand side response and renewable generators.  
Key role for storage 
At very high RES -E levels, it is likely that we will need to find ways of time shifting very significant 
renewable energy volumes. There is potentially a critical role for new and emerging long duration storage 
technologies to address this issue. A recent Baringa report commissioned by Energy Storage Ireland 
entitled Gamechanger4 highlights the potential of these technologies to manage system level curtailment 
and oversupply levels, and provide clean dispatchable capacity, reduced emissions, and if  appropriately 
located in congested parts of the transmission grid, t hey also have the potential to simultaneously crate 
significant additional “space” for renewables on the transmission grid. 
Such deployments require efficient and appropriate market investment signals if they are to be delivered. 
In addressing this reform  of the electricity market design and making it “Fit for 55” and in line with the 
EU’s REPowerEU Plan, it will be important to consider the inherent commercial / economic characteristics 
of these essential technologies that we know we will need. 
Some techn ology solutions such as demand side response have limited capital costs and likely more 
material marginal operating costs and can potentially be deployed under modestly evolved energy, 
capacity and system service markets, and this flexibility will have a n et system value. However, it is 
extremely unlikely that these technologies can provide energy time shifting services at the scale and in 
the locations, required to support the level of decarbonisation we are targeting.  
Large scale long duration storage te chnologies will likely have very significant capex and relatively low 
marginal operating costs (excluding energy market charges) and as such investment in these technologies 
would likely be more efficient if they were provided with a much higher degree of revenue certainty. For 
storage technologies however, it is also important to preserve short term energy market price signals to 
ensure that deployed technologies operate efficiently on the system once built. 
Design the market for high renewable penetration and the technologies that support it 
There are challenges associated with designing a market capable of accommodating the high levels of 
renewable penetration required to deliver on the EU’s 2030 and longer -term targets. The reformed 
electricity market will need to drive investments across a wide range of technologies, including low carbon 
generation, electricity storage, and flexible demand from consumers. 
To cost effectively get to net zero, and before that to a fully decarbonised electricity system, we must 
ensure that the market is designed to optimise both investment and dispatch (where and when to produce 
and use electricity) given the current market design is one based on a fossil -fuel powered electricity 
system. In this regard, WEI welcome the Commi ssion’s focus on long-term contracts in the consultation 
paper as these appear to be the best way of incentivising the needed levels of investment in a cost -
efficient way. 
The incentivisation of flexibility is also a key aspect and it will be important ensure accurate market signals 
for these technologies. Such market signals could deliver much of the flexibility needed for a zero-carbon

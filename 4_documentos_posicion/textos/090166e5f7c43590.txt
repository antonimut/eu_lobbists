Ørsted replies to public consultation: revision of the 
EU’s electricity market design – ANNEX 
 
Part 1 (PPAs) 
Question 1, reasoning: By entering into a PPA, consumers will profit from stable electricity prices. 
Consumers who have signed PPAs in the last years now reap the benefits of having locked in lower 
prices, while the ones signing today can benefit from locking in expectations that prices will go down 
again. Given that large consumers procure electricity in tranches, PPAs are normally used to cover 
demand that will occur with certainty. Less certain peaks are then shaved through market trading. As a 
result, PPAs not only provide stable and lower prices, but do so while the necessary incentives for flexible 
behaviour via e.g. demand response in the short-term markets are preserved. 
 
Question 3:  
(c) promoting State-supported schemes that can be combined with PPAs: There is a tension 
between any support mechanism that provides strong de-risking (compared to support mechanisms that 
provide additional cash flows) and the need for securing long-term offtake agreements to mitigate 
investment risks.  
State-supported schemes should be for situations where the buyer is prevented from entering renewables 
PPAs due to economic reasons, and currently relies on greenhouse gas intensive processes and energy 
sources. Examples include chemical plants with gas-fired on-site co-generation, industries in which 
sourcing high-quality renewable electricity would make them uncompetitive due to unfair trade practices 
in third countries, or SMEs for which entering into a renewables contract would come with unbearable 
administrative and transaction cost.    
The goal of state-support should be to enable potential renewable PPA offtakers to overcome their 
funding gap. Of course, identifying which companies have a justifiable funding gap might provide some 
challenges. However, the strong foundation of EU competition policy and state-aid rules, combined with a 
vision for a Green Deal Industrial Plan, will help to overcome this challenge, thus providing long-term 
contracts to substantially more electricity consumers, while reducing emissions. 
 
(e) requiring suppliers to procure a predefined share of their consumers’ energy  
through PPAs: This could strengthen the roll-out of the PPA market, as many potential offtakers do not 
see long-term contracting as strictly needed and inherently complex/risky. In order to have this PPA 
requirement drive the deployment of new green production facilities, however, the procurement obligation 
would have to be for a timeline sufficiently long to effectively enhance the de-risking of the business case 
for new renewable assets, meaning likely 10+ years. However, impacts have to be studied further, which 
is why we do not advise obligatory PPAs (see also Q6).  
  
(f) facilitating cross-border PPAs: Ørsted firmly believes that the deployment of new green production 
facilities is a common goal for all Member States and not a target that should be seen in a narrow 
geographical scope. While proving some level of geographical correlation between production and 
consumption is useful to avoid congestion of power grids, there is also a consideration around whether 
new production can be deployed near large consumption centers in a way that will prove cost effective. It 
should therefore be possible to deliver physical green PPAs across borders and across bidding zones, 
with a common EU target for green production and a common framework for evidencing and verifying 
green credentials across borders and bidding zones. If each Member States or Bidding Zone implements 
unique requirements for evidencing green credentials, it would be a major obstacle to both the roll-out of 
PPAs and green production facilities.

Facilitation of cross-border PPAs should focus on PPAs with physical delivery. By contrast, virtual PPAs 
would not merit the same system benefits. Virtual (or financial) PPAs are defined as “a financial derivative 
contract in which the price for the underlying electricity is settled with a financial product” (RE-Source). 
These financial products, for example based on the virtual purchase of solar power from Iberia consumed 
in the Nordics, do not reflect the physical limitations of the grid, and thus have a limited contribution to 
further integration of the electricity system.   
 
Part 4 (Accelerating RES build-out): 
 
Question 1, additional content: CfD-solutions aside, the TAG provides a reasonable compensation to 
offshore wind developers. The formula and trigger conditions can be made more precise and targeted, 
but the main importance of TAG is to provide some floor for investments in offshore wind connected to 
hybrids while also giving TSO’s an incentive to allocate adequate capacity to export the wind generation. 
This is essential for unlocking offshore wind connected to hybrids.  
 
TAG does not constitute double state aid, as it is a compensation for lost revenue. It will not hurt 
consumer tariffs worse than redispatch within an onshore bidding zone and it is not overly favourable 
towards offshore generators, as they are simply hurt harder from lack of capacity allocation on the 
borders than generators within an onshore bidding zone where the effect is diluted.  
For TAG to be a trustworthy and useful tool securing investments, it should draw its funds from 
congestion income – which also makes sense as a price collapse in the offshore bidding zone will 
increase the congestion rent unjustly – as a priority use.   
The proposed formula by Engie Impact could be improved by including the day-ahead value of the 
prospectively curtailed volumes to mitigate under compensation in the case that the value of the curtailed 
volumes is significant. Further, the trigger for the TAG formula should be changed so that it only triggers 
when the offshore generator is experiencing an impact.  
It is important to implement the TAG in EU legislation and not leave it optional to member states. First, 
because the TAG compensation should be an automatic part of the market design and not be watered 
down by member states. Second, because only by being an integral part of the market design will it affect 
investments in offshore generation connected to hybrids.   
It could be considered to apply TAG not just to offshore bidding zones but to all significantly unbalanced 
bidding zones with large renewable potentials. Between large bidding zones, the 70% minimum 
interconnector use stives to ensure socio-economic efficient outcomes by making sure interconnector 
capacities are used to the benefit of European consumers. In small bidding zones that are out of balance, 
meaning that there is much more generation than consumption, 70% capacity can lead to a price collapse 
and curtailed generation with serious detrimental impacts on investor confidence. Offshore hybrids will 
typically be imbalanced, with much larger generation than consumption and hence be at serious risk of 
interconnector curtailment.  
Giving such a guarantee will provide TSOs with a strong incentive to provide adequate interconnection 
capacity to the market for the bidding zones that primarily provide power to be consumed in other bidding 
zones. This will allow for the continued investment in renewables in these bidding zones where the 
demand is low resulting in a more optimal geographical placement of renewable generation. One 
example for this could be DK1 where the renewable potential far exceeds any realistic path for the 
demand, and which could cover a significant portion of German demand – if the interconnection 
capacities are allocated to the market.   
 
 
 
Question 3, more detailed explanation regarding measures:  
(a) at national regulatory or administrative level,  
  
1. A two-tracked approach to building out offshore wind and renewable power-to-X:  
As a complement to a wider-scope evaluation in public tenders, a parallel and market-driven 
approach for offshore wind deployment should also be explored. Where the government-driven 
tender pipeline will support a minimum required deployment to achieve general electrification, this 
market-driven approach will allow developers to link merchant offshore wind projects directly to 
large-scale consumption onshore, including for future renewable power-to-X production, and thus 
work around potential onshore grid bottlenecks.   
  
With a pipeline of industry-driven developments outside centralised tender schemes, developers, 
OEMs and supply chain companies can also form a firmer outlook for upcoming projects, further 
facilitating supply chain investments.  
  
Greenfield projects should be facilitated through national Maritime Spatial Planning (MSP) that 
predesignate sufficient sites in ‘greenfield development zones’ reserved for greenfield 
applications. Mechanisms for allocating rights to seabed for greenfield projects should effectively 
address and mitigate country-specific concerns regarding system integration costs, secured 
offtake, sustainability, and biodiversity impacts, etc. These concerns may vary across country-
specific circumstances.  
2. Simplify and shorten permitting procedures for all renewables.  
Due diligence and proper permitting, including environmental impact assessments and public 
hearings, are essential to any large-scale infrastructure project. In fact, permitting procedures can 
often take as long as – or longer than – the installation. Hence there are large potential gains in 
terms of buildout speed if they can be streamlined.  
  
To this end, permitting processes should be as simple and transparent as possible with clearly 
defined responsibilities between governmental agencies, ideally setting up a single, central point 
of contact for renewable energy developers (a ‘one-stop-shop’). Policymakers and authorities 
should always seek to provide flexibility, give clear guidance on unavoidable trade-offs, and avoid 
permitting becoming a bottleneck. This also applies to related types of infrastructure, e.g. 
transmission grids and harbour expansions.  
3. Facilitate European supply chain scaling  
On top of fast-tracking processing of currently planned buildout and acceleration of plans for 
offshore wind, governments and the industry each hold several concrete levers that can help 
overcome supply chain constraints and incremental by-project investments, including:  
Levers for governments  
• Ensure steady and coordinated buildout scheduling, including a substantial and predictable flow 
of projects from developers to OEMs.  
• Prioritise mega projects such as energy islands and hubs, enabling bulk component orders with 
cost efficiencies for supply chain.  
• Facilitate giga-scale factories and streamline permissions for larger factories and facilities, 
allowing supply chain participants to benefit from economies of scale.   
• Moderation of local content requirements. By not setting detailed requirements at component 
level, many inefficiencies can be avoided without necessarily reducing local economic benefits.   
• Provide of low-cost financing to OEMs, boosting business cases by reducing cost of capital, e.g. 
by strengthening multilateral financial institutions’ ability to further support the necessary 
investments in supply chains via loans, equity, and guarantees.  
Levers for the industry  
• Standardise design specifications for projects and components, to unlock further supply chain 
efficiencies.  
• Establish of framework agreements including scale commitments, reducing uncertainty around 
OEMs’ pipelines by providing visibility on order volumes and bearing additional sharing risk.  
 
 
 
• Ensure flexible project design, allowing the opportunity to change project design to benefit from 
supply chain efficiencies – for instance, using HVAC instead of HVCD in marginal cases. 
 
(c) via changes to the current electricity market design? 
We caution against rushed and fundamental change of the electricity market design. The existing market 
design has been over 20 years in development and does give strong signals to the market that are in 
consumers interest (see also F Q2). Recent volatility and high prices were a result of unprecedented 
volatility in underlying commodities, rather than a failing of the electricity market design. And any rushed 
fundamental changes could have significant unintended consequences and be counterproductive to 
investor confidence and the deployment of renewable generation. We refer to our targeted input for 
improvements in the regulatory framework. A key element to unlock investments in offshore hybrid 
capacity are Transmission Access Guarantees. The TAG should be complemented with an option for 
member states to allocate some share of the actual or expected congestion rent to the offshore wind 
developer (see Q1 for more details). This will align incentives so that member states do not have a 
financial disincentive to mature hybrid projects where need for support for the offshore developers is 
significant, as most value are captured as congestion rent. Such an option should not be mandatory, but it 
should be an option for Member states to realize the hybrids they need with other tools than state aid. 
The opposite case is being seen in Denmark right now, where the winner of the Bornholm Energy Island 
tender will be expected to pay the residual infrastructure cost after the expected congestion rent income. 
So, this link between the offshore wind developer and the owner of the transmission infrastructure is quite 
natural and it makes sense to extend it also in the direction of the offshore wind developers. 
 
 
 
Question 3, case study: Ørsted and Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners (CIP) have entered a 
partnership to develop approx. 5.2 gigawatts of offshore wind in Denmark across four projects via the so-
called Open-Door procedure. If the projects obtain approval and all goes well, the wind farms are 
expected to be operational in 2027.  
  
Alongside the government planned offshore wind tenders, the open-door procedure allows developers on 
their own initiative to develop an offshore wind project by submitting an unsolicited application for a 
license to carry out preliminary investigations. The legal basis for approving such open-door applications 
is based on a criterion of ‘relevance’, which leaves the Danish government with a broad discretion to 
decide on whether the projects are considered desirable. This means that the government may include a 
variety of considerations when deciding how an open-door project affects e.g., the environment, current 
infrastructure planning, national security, and other socioeconomic factors. Today, there is no solid 
administrative practice for which parameters are considered and how they are assessed, which leaves 
uncertainty for both project developers and the state.  
  
For our four open-door projects specifically, the Danish government could potentially raise concerns 
regarding the perceived need for grid reinforcements—which the Danish electricity consumers would pay 
through their consumption tariffs—and their impact on electricity prices, which may lower state revenues 
from other upcoming offshore wind tenders.   
  
Our intended grid connection arrangement for the projects, however, will limit the need for project-specific 
grid reinforcements. We intend to deploy massive PtX production in connection with the wind farms. We 
will therefore use most of the electricity at the site. We don’t want to go off-grid, as (1) we want to sell 
electricity to the grid in the high price hours with low renewable generation where we intend to sell some 
of the power to lower the consumer price a bit, and (2) we want to provide ancillary services. Because we 
could not contribute electricity when the system needs it most, we believe the worst-case scenario for 
society would be if we went off-grid. The problem is that, while the Danish government understands our 
intentions, they cannot set up requirements for anything related to the grid connection arrangement, as 
that will be considered discrimination. At least, that is our and the Danish Energy Agency’s reading so 
far.  
 
 
 
  
Our primary wish is, therefore, that the Danish government or TSO have the power to only connect these 
open-door projects to the grid in a way that limits the impact on power prices and grid cost.  
 
Part 6 (Alternatives to Gas to keep the electricity System in Balance) 
 
Q1, reasoning: 
 
(c) Yes. However, future actions should focus on getting correct price signals in place in all situations, i.e. 
by making market-based procurement of all non-frequency ancillary services mandatory, by increasing all 
price caps to or above the estimated Value of Lost Load, and by fully implementing the 2019/943 
requirements on market access to cross-border transmission capacity (“the 70% requirement”) 
 
(e) Yes. Consumers have full freedom to choose between variable and fixed price contract. While fixed 
price contracts reduce the consumers’ exposure to price volatility it comes with increased (price 
insurance) costs. Roll-out of smart meters with 15/60 min measurements for all EU consumers will enable 
low-cost variable price contracts and will allow consumers to take control of their energy costs by 
reducing consumption in time periods with high prices. Increased demand side response is a prerequisite 
for the transition towards a fully renewable power system and a cornerstone in current EU regulation. 
 
(f) Yes, subject to (i) current bidding zone delineation (see response Q15 below) and (ii) ensuring that the 
requirements of 2019/943 on making available at least 70% of cross-border capacity to the markets are 
observed across Europe.

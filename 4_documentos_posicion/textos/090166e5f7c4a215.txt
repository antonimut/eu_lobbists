Executive Summary: 
The Irish Solar Energy Association (ISEA) was established in 2013 to advance a policy and regulatory 
landscape promoting solar as a leading renewable energy technology that will decarbonise Ireland's 
electricity system and contribute to a successful and strong clean economy. As the leading voice for 
the Irish solar industry, ISEA works closely with stakeholders to advance the solar agenda on behalf 
of our members. ISEA is committed to ensuring that solar provides at least 20% of electricity demand 
by 2030 and strongly endorses Minister Ryan’s recently announced ambition for 8GW of solar to be 
connected to the grid by 2030. 
As the trade association for the solar industry in Ireland, ISEA is responding on behalf of our 
membership of 195 parties currently active in the Irish solar market. We are grateful for the 
opportunity to respond to this crucial consultation and look forward to continuing to engage with 
the European Commission on policy issues. In our response, we outline our views on the changes to 
the EU electricity market design. 
The goal of the market revision should be to rapidly decrease the penetration of gas into the energy 
system by accelerating investment in renewables. This requires the opening of all routes to market for 
solar PV investments. We also want to ensure that consumers access the benefits of renewable 
electricity (long-term, stable, low-cost electricity). Generators and consumers must still be exposed to 
volatile spot market prices, but neither should endure them. Market prices  that reflect supply and 
demand are the primary tool to achieve a continuous adjustment of consumption and production. It 
incentivises demand -side response, as well as the v ery useful flexibility provisions from marginal 
pricing are fundamental to ensure this balance. 
 
Within this frame, the EU electricity market design should not limit or impose any route-to-market 
option. To incentivise investments into renewables and flexibility solutions while ensuring affordable 
electricity prices for consumers, appropriate price signals must be delivered both in the short and 
long-term electricity markets.  
 
Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) are an efficient way of mitigating the impact of short-term 
markets for residential & industrial consumers. However, this is subject to a suitable policy & 
regulatory framework being in place to incentivise the uptake of PPAs. While all routes to markets 
must be preserved to allow developers maximum flexibility in delivering renewable power, PPAs will 
serve as the main driver for the deployment of renewables and conduit for consumers to benefit 
from the clean energy and stable, low-cost power that renewables provide.  
Barriers to the use of PPAs in Ireland include the allocation of network constraints, system 
curtailment, oversupply risk to the generator (thus increasing strike prices), and lack of access to 
direct lines as a route to market. Increasing the pool of creditworthy off-takers by offering public 
credit support, guaranteeing regulatory support, limiting market interventions, and accelerating the 
permitting process for renewable projects would help in boosting the uptake of PPAs. 
Long-term contracts for difference for renewable generators are essential for the accelerated 
decarbonisation of EU power grids.  However, CfDs cannot be the only route to market for new 
renewable deployment as other routes to market can be deployed more quickly – thereby increasing 
the pace of the energy transition. 
Equally, CfDs should not be imposed on existing generation under any circumstance, as this would 
be close to a right to retroactive regulatory change, therefore increasing the remuneration 
uncertainty and the cost of investments.

To ensure that a viable renewables pipeline is developed in Ireland, CfD prices should be linked to 
inflation, availability compensation should be open to corporate and government-supported CfDs 
and flexible support durations should be an option for government-supported PPAs. 
Measures that would help to accelerate the deployment of renewables in Europe include the 
introduction of renewable auctions in each member state and implementing Article 16 of RED II to 
reduce permitting timelines for renewable projects.  
 
A key regulatory/network tariff change to ensure the necessary investments in network 
infrastructure is to restructure use of system charges to be based on generation instead of capacity. 
Anticipatory grid development should be prioritised by TSOs. TSOs should analyse potential grid 
investments in terms of a ‘cost to society’ instead of the cost of such infrastructure to the TSO.  
In light of this, grid investments should be made based on the current renewable project pipeline.

Making Electricity Bills Independent of Short-Term Markets: 
1. Do you consider the use of PPAs as an efficient way to mitigate the impact of short-term markets 
on the price of electricity paid by the consumer, including industrial consumers? 
 
In our view, PPAs are an efficient way of mitigating the impact of short-term markets for residential & 
industrial consumers. However, this is subject to a suitable policy & regulatory framework being in 
place to incentivise the uptake of PPAs. 
 
In Ireland, this includes changes to our renewable auction system to enable such a framework. To that 
end, the Irish solar industry has recently requested that: 
 
• Indexation is added to government-supported PPAs.  
• Availability compensation for curtailment  is made available to corporate & government-
supported PPAs. 
• Flexibility of support duration in government-supported PPAs. 
 
We have also asked that reforms are made to the planning system to ensure that there is a lower 
attrition risk for renewable projects to improve the perceived attractiveness of PPAs. 
 
While all routes to markets must be preserved to allow developers maximum flexibility in delivering 
renewable power, PPAs will serve as the main driver for the deployment of renewables and conduit 
for consumers to benefit from the clean energy and stable, low-cost power that renewables provide. 
In this way, PPAs demonstrate the real value of renewable energy ge neration while providing the 
facility for industry to develop innovative business models.   
 
2. Please describe the barriers that currently prevent the conclusion of PPAs. 
 
In Ireland, a significant number of the barriers to the use of PPAs relate to issues in the policy and 
regulatory structures which force developers to increase their costs – which causes many renewable 
projects to become: 
 
1. Become commercially unviable when entering into state-supported routes to market.  
2. Less attractive to potential PPA users/customers due to their increased prices.  
 
In Ireland, network constraints, system curtailment, and oversupply risk are principally allocated to 
the generator. This means that generators will price in a certain level of future dispatch/re-dispatch 
into their auction bids/CPPA prices over the life of the assets, locking in these costs at a conservative 
level for the tenor of the contract.  
 
As Ireland is the EMEA HQ for many large technology & pharmaceutical companies, there is a large 
market for Corporate PPAs both via virtual PPAs and direct lines OOAs. However, it is difficult for

many large energy users to access PPAs in the most cost-effective as direct lines are not currently 
permitted under Irish legislation unless the energy user has been refused a connection by the 
regulator. This is a reform that is much needed for unlocking Ireland’s renewable potential.  
The latest version of the Government of Ireland’s Climate Action Plan states a regulatory 
consultation on direct lines will be published within Q1 2023. This is desperately needed to open the 
direct line route to market for solar PPAs. 
Beyond this, we would suggest that access to PPAs is eased by de-risking the contracts of large 
energy users and amending retail market rules to allow retailers to offer long-term contracts.  
 
3. Do you consider that the following measures would be effective in strengthening the roll-out of 
PPAs: 
 
a. pooling demand in order to give access to smaller final customers 
 
Pooling demand would be an excellent idea for strengthening the roll-out of PPAs. By 
allowing smaller customers to be aggregated into pools of demand another source of 
liquidity in the market can be created. This will be crucial for empowering customers and 
enabling community power. 
 
b. providing insurance against risk(s) either market driven or through publicly supported 
guarantees schemes (please identify such risks) 
 
In our view, insurance is unnecessary and inappropriate. Renewable developers have opted 
to enter the market with a specific commercial position. It, therefore, follows that a certain 
amount of risk must be assumed for this. 
 
However, publicly supported guarantee schemes do operate in Ireland in the form of CfD 
auctions called the Renewable Energy Support Scheme (RESS). We welcome this as it allows 
projects to enter the market with a sufficiently low cost of capital to be commercially viable. 
RESS has been successful in procuring a total of 3.2 GW across all technologies so far. Thus, 
publicly supported guarantee schemes have been instrumental in strengthening the roll-out 
of renewable PPAs in the Irish market.  
 
c. promoting State-supported schemes that can be combined with PPAs 
 
ISEA supports the provision of flexibility within state-supported schemes that allow for the 
co-metering of projects whereby components of renewable generation are compensated by 
separate routes to market.  
 
While it is difficult to advise if this will strengthen the roll-out of PPAs, we welcome efforts 
to provide access to different routes to market for developers as this will allow a larger 
number of renewable projects to be commercially viable. Within the Irish market, it is hoped 
that this change will be made in RESS 3. 
 
d. supporting the standardization of contracts 
 
No. Renewable energy producers need flexibility in their contracting structures to ensure 
that a business case can be made for as many projects as possible. 
 
e. requiring suppliers to procure a predefined share of their consumers’ energy through PPAs

No. ISEA would strongly support suppliers procuring as much of their consumers’ energy 
from renewables as a practice, but requiring them to procure a predefined share via PPAs

Equally, renewable energy is currently more economical than thermal assets owing to recent

In theory, restored access to the EU electricity market once the Celtic Interconnector would

The following would help in boosting the uptake of PPAs:

Due to the current state of the market in Ireland, this will not be possible as all renewable

Forward Markets: 
1. Do you consider forward hedging as an efficient way to mitigate exposure to short term volatility 
for consumers and to support investment in new capacity? 
 
Forward markets are a great complement to PPAs/CfDs as they reduce the overall risk of the projects. 
Often projects secure, for instance, 70 -80% of their output under PPAs and complement with a 30-
20% exposure to merchant prices.  
However, this is currently not permitted within Ireland’s RESS auction structure. It is hoped that this 
will be changed in RESS 3 to ensure that more projects are viable. The combination of different 
revenue streams is essential to guaranteeing the feasibility of a larger pipeline of renewable projects. 
2. Do you consider that the liquidity in forward markets is currently sufficient to meet this objective? 
 
Currently, there is insufficient liquidity in forward markets. This is caused by two reasons: insufficient 
demand (mitigated by increasing demand-side flexibility) and marketplace products not being tailored 
to renewables. Products traded in forward markets include standard, long-duration, baseload-
adapted contracts.  
Thus, to increase liquidity in forward markets there are two suggestions. First, to introduce measures 
to incentivise demand-side flexibility, and second, to adapt the forward market products to renewable 
generation, introducing simpler, more flexible, and more granular products (e.g., hourly instead of 
month-ahead). The forward market needs to be reformed and adapted thinking about the electricity 
system of the future, dominated by RES generation. 
The liquidity issue on forward markets will be solv ed as we unlock the demand, by enabling and 
incentivising retailers to hedge their portfolio, for instance by amending the retail regulation or by 
increasing the transparency requirements on retailers.  
 
3. In your view, what prevents participants from entering into forward contracts? 
 
The reasons are as follows: 
 
• Attractiveness of state subsidised contracts, Corporate PPAs & direct line renewables. 
• Uncertainty in power prices due to geopolitics and increasing scarcity of thermal assets. 
 
4. In your view, would requiring electricity suppliers to hedge for a share of their supply be beneficial 
for consumers and for retail competition? 
 
Such a measure may be beneficial for retail competition & consumers as a temporary measure when 
price spikes have been anticipated as a result of market developments, but this can cause radical 
price increases once hedges expire. In the event such a measure is adopted, suppliers should also be 
required to inform consumers of projected increases in price after the hedge expires to ensure that 
consumers can prepare. 
 
5. Do you consider that the creation of virtual hubs for forward contracts complemented with liquid 
transmission rights would improve liquidity in forward markets? If yes, do you consider that such 
virtual hub(s) should be developed at national, regional or EU level? 
 
The creation of virtual hubs for forward contracts complemented with liquid transmission rights

Contracts For Difference: 
1. Do you consider the use of two-way contracts for difference or similar arrangements as an 
efficient way to mitigate the impact of short-term markets on the price of electricity and to support 
investments in new capacity (where investments are not forthcoming on a market basis)? 
 
Long-term contracts for difference for renewable generators are essential for the accelerated 
decarbonisation of EU power grids. As we start to deliver more deeply decarbonised grids (as 
currently envisaged in Ireland with our 80% RES-E target by 2030) we need to bring greater 
sophistication to the design of these auctions.  
Contracts for Difference have been very beneficial for the development of RES. However, CfDs cannot 
be the only route to market for new renewable deployment as other routes to market can be deployed 
more quickly – thereby increasing the pace of the energy transition. By contrast, CfDs are dependent 
on the publication of tenders and results of tenders, whereas B2B deals are much easier) and alter the 
deployment of innovative technologies.  
 
CfDs should not be imposed on existing generation under any circumstance, as this would be close to 
a right to retroactive regulatory change, therefore increasing the remuneration uncertainty, and the 
cost of investments.  
 
2. Should new publicly financed investments in inframarginal electricity generation be supported by 
way of two-way contracts for differences or similar arrangements, as a means to mitigate electricity 
price spikes of consumers while ensuring a minimum revenue? 
 
ISEA supports publicly financed investments in inframarginal electricity generation being supported 
by way of two-way contracts for differences in Ireland due to the current nature of the renewables 
market in Ireland. With that being said, we understand that market structures in different member 
states may not be suited to this.   
Strategic interventions in the policy & regulatory spheres should be made to reduce the risk of 
developing projects – thereby progressively lowering the cost of capital over time to enable 
renewables to operate without state subsidy. 
3. What technologies should be subject to two-way contracts for differences or similar arrangements 
and why? 
 
The current policy in Ireland is that wind and solar, wind and storage, and solar and storage are 
eligible to compete for CfDs in RESS in a ‘technology agnostic’ manner. This should continue – with 
possible scope for adding in other renewable technologies subject to their feasibility in the Irish 
market. 
 
4. What technologies should be excluded and why? 
 
Thermal assets should be excluded from consideration for CfDs. In our view, supporting CfDs for 
thermal assets will counteract efforts to transition towards renewable energy and impede the path 
toward European energy independence.  
 
5. What are the main risks of requiring new publicly supported inframarginal capacity to be procured 
on the basis of two-way contracts for difference or similar arrangements, for example as regards of 
the impact in the short-term markets, competition between different technologies, or the 
development of market based PPAs?

In the event that new inframarginal capacity is constrained to only participating via publicly 
supported CfDs, this will likely inhibit the development of market-based PPAs as participants will 
hold back from engaging in alternative PPA structures and/or routes to market. This also means that 
the rollout of assets will be driven by auction timelines which will cause assets to deploy in peaks as 
opposed to a smoother rollout which is more likely in a scenario with multiple routes to market.

CfDs must expose RES to market signals to ensure that plant design and operation are as system

● Indexation is added to government-supported PPAs. 
● Availability compensation for curtailment is made available to corporate & government-supported

7. How can it be ensured that any costs or pay-out generated by two-way CfDs in high price periods 
are channelled back to electricity consumers? Should a default approach apply, for example, should 
these revenues or costs be allocated to consumers proportionally to their electricity consumption? 
 
In Ireland, the PSO levy is charged to consumers to subsidise new renewable generation within the 
RESS auction scheme. The generators under RESS are remunerated when market prices are below 
the project’s strike price. In the event the market price exceeds the project’s auction strike price, 
generators pay into the PSO levy.  
 
The 2022/2023 PSO credit (€89.00 for each household) was heavily funded by payments from RESS-
supported renewables. RESS payments account for 63.80% of the items that fund the PSO credit for 
homes and businesses. A similar compensation structure could likely be adopted (and adapted as 
appropriate) in each member state. 
 
8. What should be the duration of a two-way CfD for new generation and why? Should this differ 
depending on the technology type? 
 
In our view, renewable generators should be free to agree the duration of their CfDs with their off-
takers. This will allow generators to structure their CfD PPAs in as flexible a manner as possible – 
thereby increasing the number of potential off-takers and thus the speed of deployment of

It is our understanding that most renewable generators in Ireland will seek another route to market 
once their current CfD expires that is not based on full market revenues as this will provide greater

New generation should be permitted to seek full market revenues or an alternative route to market

 In our view, a key regulatory/network tariff change to ensure the necessary investments in network

Limited Revenues of Inframarginal Generators: 
1. Do you consider that some form of revenue limitation of inframarginal generators should be 
maintained? 
 
In ISEA’ s view, inframarginal revenue caps are not  needed once the measures specified in  the 
Contracts for Difference section of the response are made. There is currently a very limited volume of 
inframarginal generation exposed to short -term markets. Typically, a significant proportion of such 
generation has secured long-term stable revenues through PPAs, CfDs, or other tools.  
Market interventions create significant regulatory instability slowing down much-needed investments 
in the energy transition. For this reason, it is our view that the revenues of inframarginal generators 
should not be limited . The problem of electricity prices should be ta ckled by acting on the route of 
the problem: the gas market, or directly at the retail level.  
The existing market revenue cap, stemming from the EU Regulation, has led to a patchwork of 
measures difficult to read for the industry or financially impacting project developers by taxing 
revenues that did not exist. We question the need to prolong the measure or make it a more 
permanent feature of the electricity market design.  
 
2. How do you rate a possible prolongation of the inframarginal revenue cap according to the 
following criteria: 
 
a. the effectiveness of the measure in terms of mitigating electricity price impacts for 
consumers 
 
Such a measure may mitigate electricity price impacts temporarily for consumers – but this 
will cause prices to increase substantially once the revenue cap expires. As stated in our 
response to Question 1, the problem of electricity prices should be tackled through cost

Energy Sharing & Demand Response: 
1. Would you support a provision giving customers the right to deduct offsite generation from their 
metered consumption? 
Collective self-consumption (CSC) will unlock the full financial value of small -scale solar installations 
for the majority of EU citizens. It allows for high returns for generators and low costs for off-takers. It 
allows renters and tenants to benefit from distributed generation. It contributes to mitigating grid 
congestion by incentivizing load shifting. Even so, the successful implementation requires a dedicated 
legal framework: 
This includes a definition, the conditions for its implementation, and the rights and obligations of the 
parties involved. The existing regulatory frameworks for Energy Communities, jointly acting 
renewables self -consumers, peer -to-peer trading , and renewables self -consumers provide no 
guidance for collective self-consumption. The legal framework should be based on three pillars: 
1: The legal dimension: a) Consumers shall have the right to choose all their electricity suppliers and 
technologies. b) Both generation and consumption should be open to citizens, and public and private 
entities. They may set up a CSC entity by reaching a contractual agreement. c) Collectively acting self-
consumers have the right to fully or partially transfer the operation and management of the CSC entity 
to a certified representative.  
2: The economic dimension: a) Applicable grid tariffs should be cost-reflective. They should reflect the 
utilized voltage levels. This incentivises local generation and contributes to reducing grid congestion. 
b) Tax exemptions could be envisioned to support CSC in multi-dwelling units. Furthermore, member 
states should have the right to offer supporting measures. c) CSC entities should have the right to 
freely choose how to market excess generation. 
3: Integration with regulations for retailers and system operators. a) Electricity generators under CSC 
should not qualify as retailers while existing rights and requirements for retailers should remain. b) 
Network operators should set up metering calculation tables to assign metered generation to the right 
suppliers. c) Simplified permitting procedures should apply. 
2. If such a right were introduced: 
 
a. Would it affect the location of new renewable generation facilities? 
 
Yes. If this right were implemented without further guidance, it would incentivize remote large-scale 
installations. They have the lowest Levelised Costs of Electricity (LCOE) and consumers would start 
acquiring shares of these plants. This would be detrimenta l to consumers of retail electricity and 
rooftop solar deployment. 
Following some reflection, we concluded a) that the maximum generation size should be limited to 5 
MW. If larger power plants would be allowed, utilities would prefer selling directly to end-consumers, 
bypassing electricity markets and their tax framework, and retailers. The price for retail electricity 
could increase and market dynamics could change. b) The collective generation capacity (kW) shall 
not exceed the collective consumption capacity (kW). This ratio allows powering the CSC entity’s entire 
consumption with its generation and it allows the CSC entity to optimize the consumption/generation 
ratio to maximise profits. At the same time, this guarantees that CSC remains focused on of fsetting 
local consumption, instead of selling on the market, for example during price hikes.  
The right economic framework will ensure that CSC incentives local generation. Grid tariffs should be 
cost reflective, i.e. they should reflect the utilized voltage levels. For example, electricity shared on 
the low- and medium-voltage grid should only pay fees for this part, potentially plus a fixed

infrastructure levy. This would lead to significant incentives for local generation because around 50 
% of grid tariffs are dedicated to transmission grids.  
Such a framework would incentivize citizens to maximise their renewable energy installation sizes to 
increase the returns from shared electricity. This will support using all sealed space on artificial 
structures for renewable generation. Citizens and companies who sell their renewable energy will 
become an integral part of the energy transition. It will reduce grid congestion because citizens have 
the incentive to adapt consumption to the local generation. This re duces the strain on the unused 
voltage levels. 
b. Should it be restricted to local areas – why? 
 
Yes. The location and perimeter of CSC entities should be aligned with the locations of low-, 
medium, high, and very-high voltage grids. Off-take and generation of CSC entities should either be 
located on the same grid section or under the same transformer station. To facilitate this, system 
operators shall publish the location of their grid sections. This obligation shall only be limited to 
preventing gaming and the abuse of power. The perimeters of CSC entities on high and very high 
voltage grids shall be limited to 20 km and 40 km respectively. 
If DSOs don’t provide locational grid data, the perimeter shall be 4 km for installations on low-voltage 
grids, and 8 km for medium -voltage grids. The fallback option of geographic criteria is required to 
facilitate CSC entities when DSOs don’t provide the required data to follow the main criterion. 
c. Should it apply across the Member State/control/zone – why and what should happen if 
bidding zones are changed? 
 
Yes. When location and perimeter match with the grid infrastructure, it will be in line with the Member 
State/control/zones, in most cases. We see no need to increase the perimeter beyond this area. There 
will be dynamic interactions between energy-sharing arrangements and wholesale electricity markets. 
Wholesale markets will adapt to provide the residual electricity demand. 
 
3. Would you support establishing a right for customers to a second meter/su b-meter on their 
premises to distinguish the electricity consumed or produced by different devices? If yes, what 
particular issues should be taken into account? 
Yes. Controlling DER devices independently via their submeters is required to activate the dema nd 
response potential of distributed devices. CSC entities should be entitled to utilize this potential, as 
well. It further incentivizes load shifting, hence increasing their consumer’s financial returns, and 
reducing grid congestion. 
A prerequisite for CSC is that network operators set up conclusive allocation tables using virtual 
allocation points. These tables combine metering values measured at the final customer with energy-
sharing arrangements. From these values, they will calculate the energy balances from energy sharing 
and retailers at the final customers. They can then forward the calculated values to CSC participants, 
aggregators, and electricity retailers. 
The EU framework should also account for two prevalent metering approaches. In the first one, every 
generating asset is directly connected to a generation meter, before being connected to loads. 
Generators share all electricity according to the sharing coefficients, without taking into account self-
consumption. This works well  for solar plants which are owned by a community. However, the 
additional meter has costs and it takes time to connect and for DSOs to verify the connection. In the 
second approach, a generating device is connected first to loads of a facility and then to the smart

meter. In this case, self-consumption is always prioritized over shared electricity. This can boost shared 
installations on detached and semi-detached houses and in energy communities. 
The EU framework should allow energy-sharing providers to complement each other at one 
connection point. For example, when a CSC entity generally doesn’t provide electricity at night, 
citizens should be able to sign with an additional energy-sharing provider for some of the missing 
electricity supply.

Conclusion: 
ISEA welcomes the Commission’s consultation on the EU electricity market design. We would 
welcome the opportunity to discuss the analysis underpinning our submission and the contents of 
the same. In conclusion, we would like to emphasise the following key points:    
I.  Making Electricity Bills Independent of Short-Term Markets: 
• PPAs are an efficient way of mitigating the impact of short -term markets for residential & 
industrial consumers. However, this is subject to a suitable policy & regulatory framework 
being in place to incentivise the uptake of PPAs. 
• While all routes to markets must be preserved to allow developers maximum flexibility in 
delivering renewab le power, PPAs will serve as the main driver for the deployment of 
renewables and conduit for consumers to benefit from the clean energy and stable, low-cost 
power that renewables provide.  
• Barriers to the u se of PPAs in Ireland include the allocation of network constraints, system 
curtailment, oversupply risk to the generator (thus increasing strike prices), and lack of access 
to direct lines as a route to market. 
• ISEA supports the provision of flexibility within state-supported schemes that allow for the co-
metering of projects w hereby components of renewable generation are compensated by 
separate routes to market.  
• Requiring suppliers to procure a predefined share of their energy via PPAs may be seen as 
excessive state intervention in the market. 
• The following would help in boosting the uptake of PPAs: 
o Increase the pool of creditworthy off-takers by offering public credit support for off-takers 
o Guarantee regulatory stability and limit market interventions. 
o Accelerate renewable permitting.  
 
II.  Contracts For Difference: 
 
• Long-term contracts for difference for renewable generators are essential for the 
accelerated decarbonisation of EU power grids.  
• As Europe starts to deliver more deeply decarbonised grids (as currently envisaged in Ireland 
with our 80% RES-E target by 2030) we need to bring greater sophistication to the design of 
renewable auctions.  
• CfDs cannot be the only route to market for new renewable deployment as other routes to 
market can be deployed more quickly – thereby increasing the pace of the energy transition. 
• CfDs should not be imposed on existing generation under any circumstance, as this would be 
close to a right to retroactive regulatory change, therefore increasing the remuneration 
uncertainty, and the cost of investments.   
• CfDs must expose RES to market signals to ensure that plant design and operation are as 
system friendly as possible.  
• To ensure that a viable renewables pipeline is developed in Ireland, the following CfD design 
changes should be implemented:  
o Auction calendars should be published well in advance of the auctions to give market actors 
time to duly plan and prepare.  
o CfD prices should be linked to inflation. 
o If auctions are undersubscribed for a specific technology, conditions should be reviewed for 
the next.

o Availability compensation for curtailment  made available to  corporate & government-
supported PPAs. 
o Flexibility of support duration in government-supported PPAs. 
 
• The regulatory imposition of two-way CfDs on existing generation would likely be seen as 
excessive state intervention in the market and hurt investor confidence in European 
renewable projects. 
 
III. Accelerating The Deployment of Renewables: 
 
• Measures that would help to accelerate the deployment of renewables in Europe include 
the introduction of renewable auctions in each member state and implementing Article 16 
of RED II to reduce permitting timelines for renewable projects.  
• A key regulatory/network tariff change to ensure the necessary investments in network 
infrastructure is to restructure use of system charges to be based on generation instead of 
capacity.  
• Anticipatory grid development should be prioritised by TSOs. TSOs should analyse potential 
grid investments in terms of a ‘cost to society’ instead of the cost of such infrastructure to 
the TSO.  
• Grid investments should be made based on the current renewable project pipeline.

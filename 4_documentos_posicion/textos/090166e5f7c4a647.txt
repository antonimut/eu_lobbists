HSE Group welcomes the European Commission’s intention to propose certain changes to the current 
electricity market design with the aim “to build a more resilient market, providing an added value for 
European citizens and the industry at large in the current crisis context and beyond.”  
 
We agree that the current  energy crisis has provided a different experience with functioning of the 
market and an opportunity to propose some improvements. However, it is crucial to distinguish 
between emergency measures to tackle effect of high energy prices and structural solutions, therefore 
no market intervention measures that would distort the function ing of the market in the long run 
and hinder new investments in renewable and low-carbon energy sources should be introduced.  
 
We agree with Eurelectric’s assessment that the “current market design is not the root cause of the 
high electricity prices.«  Therefore, a market design reform should not be done in a hasty manner and 
without adequately and carefully assessing all relevant impacts  of the proposals . Any radical and 
rushed reform could undermine investor confidence in the sector. Moreover, internal energy market 
needs to be preserved, while acknowledging that Member States have the possibility to introduce 
certain market-based measures that reflect their national specifici ties without endangering the 
functioning of the wholesale market.  
 
As also stated in the  ACER’s Final Assessment 1 of the EU Wholesale Electricity Market Design, »the 
current electricity market is not to blame for  the current crisis…it is essential to retain the underlying 
features of the current market design and assess any possible changes considering their long -term 
benefits, rather than the impact of the current crisis.« 
 
In the light of the REPowerEU action plan and ambitious EU 2050 climate neutrality goals, as well as 
efforts to decrease EU’s dependence on fossil fuels, the focus should be put into acceleration of RES 
investments and permitting procedures for the uptake of the RES projects , as well as flexibility and 
storage technologies, by preserving and enhancing incentives for investments, and providing investors 
with certainty and predictability. Reliable and sufficient long -term investment signals are needed to 
ensure achieving EU’s ambitious climate and energy goals.

HSE Group highlights some of the  following key aspects  with regards to the Commission’s 
consultation paper: 
 
1. Long-term contracts represent a successful tool from consumers perspective (price stability) as well as 
for producers, but currently the lack of  adequate collateral represents the biggest obstacle for their 
development. However, they need to remain voluntary, without obligations imposed by the state  for 
existing or new investments. 
 
2. Liquidity in forward markets  needs to be enhanced , since they have crucial role in achieving EU’s 
climate objectives. Forward hedging beyond the current timeframe (3 year s or beyond) should be 
incentivised. 
 
3. HSE Group does not support any obligations imposed on suppliers (with regards to fixed price contracts, 
hedging) that limit competition. 
 
4. We do not support the embedding of below cost regulated prices in the market design reform as a long-
term measure. However, for reasons of security supply, self-sufficiency and price stability a certain level 
of price regulation for suppliers, when purchasing electricity from domestic electricity producers, could 
be beneficial.  
 
5. HSE Group opposes any revenue limitations for inframarginal generators to be maintained beyond its 
current expiry date as a crisis intervention measure. 
 
6. Marginal pricing model as regards the short -term markets provides efficiency, transparency, and 
incentives to keep costs as low as possible and needs to be preserved . However, a dynamic 
mechanism to minimise speculative trading and consequently disproportionate price spikes, could 
be established. Additionally, the problem of increasing margin calls requirements by clearing banks 
should be adequately addressed in the reform. 
 
7. HSE Group considers well-designed capacity mechanism as a necessary element of the (new) electricity 
market design, which ensure that the firm and flexible capacity is valued and invested in. However, a 
simplification of approval process is needed, and an emphasis on lowering import dependency and 
improving self-sufficiency should be a part of the evaluation process.   
 
8. The network tariff de sign should reflect the power system needs, reward and not burden the role of 
flexibility and storage systems, such as pumped storage, as well as enable investment decisions in new 
storage facilities. We also reiterate our opposition to a G-tariff implementation at national level to not 
hinder the necessary investment signals for new capacity. 
 
9. HSE Group believes the most important measure to accelerate the deployment of renewables relates 
to the removal of administrative barriers , the acceleration of permitting procedures with strict 
deadlines for obtaining permits, including environmental assessment, defined in EU legislation and lack 
of public guarantee schemes for PPAs . Recognising hydropower’s role as a key indigenous renewable 
source of energy and not excluding any technology from “go-to” areas is also crucial. 
 
10. As HSE Group advocates for regulatory stability and predictability for market participants, we believe 
no major fundamental changes to the already tasking REMIT regime are n ecessary. However, certain 
practical improvements could be beneficial to ensure a uniform interpretation and implementation of 
the REMIT Regulation provisions.

1. MAKING ELECTRICITY BILLS MORE INDEPENDENT FROM SHORT-TERM COST OF FOSSIL FUELS 
 
HSE Group recognises the positive role that long-term contracts can have on customer protection 
against extensive price volatility by ensuring a fixed price for a longer time horizon as well as on the 
investment costs and stability with regards to long-term income. But this should not be the main goal 
for their acceleration, they are foremost an  effective tool to enable energy transition.  We believe 
that the use of such long-term contracts should be increased (including at smaller end users, not only 
large energy intensive users) by introducing additional incentives such as state guarantees, but the 
nature of the contracts should remain voluntary to avoid the path towards a regulated market.  
 
To pursue the key objective of long -term markets more effectively, which is to offer efficient and 
effective hedging possibilities to market participants and to provide price signal for investment s, HSE 
Group proposes that the market design reform considers the following points: 
 
1. Long-term contracts represent a successful tool from consumers perspective (price stability) as 
well as for producers, but currently lack of adequate collateral represents the biggest obstacle for 
their development. However, they need to remain voluntary, without obligations imposed by the 
state for existing or new investments. 
 
2. Two-way CfDs need to remain voluntary, without obligations imposed by the state for existing or 
new investments.  
 
3. Liquidity in forward markets needs to be increased, since they have crucial role in achieving EU’s 
climate objectives. Forward hedging beyond the current timeframe (3 years or beyond) should be 
incentivised. 
 
1.1 POWER PURCHASE AGREEMENTS  
 
Q1. Do you consider the use of PPAs as an efficient way to mitigate the impact of short- term markets 
on the price of electricity paid by the consumer, including industrial consumers?  Yes/No 
 
The use of PPAs brings advantages both for investors  and suppliers as well as for the consumers. The 
wider use of power purchase agreements will contribute to the long -term moderation and 
predictability of electricity prices, but their role is primarily important as an instrument in further 
promoting investment in renewable energy sources.  
 
Eurelectric position paper on market design underlines a positive role of long-term contracts but points 
out that »to ensure the compatibility of such long -term contracts with short -term energy markets, 
specific care must be given to their design to minimise distortions and ensure that they do not reduce 
liquidity on short-term markets and notably on organised long-term markets.« 
 
Q2. Please describe the barriers that currently prevent the conclusion of PPAs. 
 
HSE Group is convinced that the biggest obstacle preventing the wider use of PPAs is the lack of 
sufficient collateral an d adequate guarantees  when concluding contracts, particularly with smaller 
buyers. We therefore propose a clear regulatory framework that would enable to diminish credit risks 
by increasing means of collateral (for example through guarantees underwritten or provided by public 
bodies) and thus incentivise the correct market signals for the uptake of  long-term contracting, with 
diverse products to meet both generators and consumers’ needs (2 -4 up to 10 -15 years or even 
longer).

Q7. Do you consider that increasing the uptake of PPAs would entail risks? 
 
We do not see specific risks deriving from the accelerated use of PPAs, for example, as outlined by the 
Commission, a risk for increasing the electricity generation from fossil fuels. This general risk remains 
on a structural, systemic basis, with the accelerated deployment of variable RES and the lack of reserve 
(stable, conventional) capacities in times of low electricity production from (variable) renewables. The 
role of existing storage technologies such as pumped hydro needs to therefore be recognised and the 
investments in new RES projects, as well as batteries and flexible solutions should be prioritised. 
 
Let us also point out a relevant (and increasing) risk here that electricity generators with hydropower 
portfolio are faced with in times of extreme weather events such as drought, occurrence of which can 
be as well attributed to climate change.  In case of low electricity production, producers that have 
hedged a certain part of their generation  (through long -term contracts) , must buy the share of 
electricity contracted, but not produced due to such occurrences, on the spot market at the current 
spot price, possibly increasing the producer’s costs.

1.2 FORWARD MARKETS  
 
 Q2. Do you consider that the liquidity in forward markets is currently sufficient to meet this 
objective? 
 
HSE Group recognizes efficiently functioning forward markets need liquidity, and the latter requires 
simplicity and transparency as well as a stable regulatory framework. The causes of liquidity issues can 
differ in the different regions. However, increased price volatility in the electricity system, due to the 
acceleration of renewable energy sources and exceptional geopolitical circumstances (war in Ukraine), 
the long-term markets now face several challenges, including a lack of liquidity.  
 
 Q3. In your view, what prevents participants from entering into forward contracts? 
 
HSE Group aligns with Eurelectric 2 and EFET  position with regards to the development of forward 
markets, underlining the need to: 
 
− Ease of collateral regulations on forward markets, by widening the collateral accepted, such as 
non-collateralised bank guarantees, including ETS permits (Regulation (EU) 2022/2311 introducing 
temporary emergency measures on collateral requirement is not sufficient as it can help alleviate 
the liquidity pressure of only around 15% of the energy market participants); 
− Increase regulatory stability; 
− Increase the volume of cross-border capacity allocated by the TSOs; 
− Investigate the possibility to increase the frequency of auctions for LTTR products (monthly 
auctions for yearly products and weekly auctions for monthly and weekly product); 
− Facilitate secondary trading (transfer of one flexibility provider’s contracted flexibility obligations 
to another suitable flexibility provider); 
− Consider introducing a voluntary scheme of market makers in forward markets to stimulate 
liquidity. 
 
Q5. Do you consider that the creation of virtual hubs for forward contracts complemented with liquid 
transmission rights would improve liquidity in forward markets? 
 
We can support the idea proposed by the Commission to “increase the liquidity in forward markets by 
establishing virtual trading hubs for forward contracts.”  This idea however requires a detailed impact 
assessment in terms of efficiency before being implemented. It already exists in some regions, and 
might be functioning well, however, an assessment is needed whether this is a suitable tool to be 
implemented at wider EU level.

Q4. What technologies should be excluded and why? 
Voluntary CfD contracts can be seen as an effective instrument to encourage investment in RES in 
connection with other forms of support schemes, in particular as an instrument for those low-carbon 
technologies that are not yet competitive in the market. CfDs that do not distort the short-term market 
signals and negatively affect optimal dispatching can be perceived, since it is a type of public support, 
as an effective tool to address the long-term needs for system adequ acy and a means to trigger the 
missing investment towards decarbonisation, rather than primarily mitigate electricity price spikes for 
consumers. They do, however, need to be, like other long-term contracts, adequately designed to 
avoid negative impacts on the market.  
 
We believe that the CfDs should not go beyond inframarginal technologies. However, they are better 
suited for variable low-carbon technologies, but their use should in any case reflect (national) energy 
and climate plans and system needs to ensure security of supply.  
 
For sure, there is no one size fits all. Two-way CfDs should be tailored for different technologies and 
targeted for promoting the use of types of low -carbon technologies according to the national 
specificities and system needs in each Member State.  CfDs should therefore be based on a case -by-
case principle, project by project and applying specificities (also within  the same technology) when 
determining a price. 
 
Q5. What are the main risks of requiring new publicly supported inframarginal capacity to be 
procured on the basis of two -way contracts for difference or similar arrangements, for example as 
regards of the impact in the short-term markets, competition between different technologies, or the 
development of market based PPAs?  Q6. What design principles could help mitigate the risks 
identified? 
 
In the case of CfDs , as well as in the case of PPAs, we do not support the introduction of any 
obligations for producers/suppliers to enter long-term contracts for new projects and negatively 
affecting competition, posing an obligation to existing assets on the other hand would even further 
decrease liquidity in the wholesale markets  and also impact flexibility on the market . Let us also 
warn against the risk of excessive centralisation of power purchasing by the state and one centralised 
entity (regulator for example) responsible for investments (as proposed in the Spanish non -paper), 
which would distort the internal energy market. Consumers can have specific needs and each producer 
can propose specific innovative solutions.  The role of state can certainly act as means of collateral 
when entering long-term contracts, but the role of the state needs to be clearly defined. 
 
Development of CfDs should not hinder the development of market based  PPAs, what is being 
recognised by the Commission as well. Since this is a type of state aid, specific notification procedures 
by the Commission in accordance with the CEEAG are already in place. In order to accelerate the use 
of CfDs, a simplification of these procedures could be considered. 
 
Another risk is related to the times of negative prices on the market since it adds revenue uncertainty 
for supported generators.  Design of contracts should include an insurance clause that would tackle 
this risk (e.g. temporary suspension of the contracts during negative prices).

Q10. Without prejudice to Article 6 of Directive (EU)2018/2001[1], should it be possible for Member 
States to impose two-way CfDs by regulatory means on existing generation capacity? If such possible

all types of existing inframarginal generation or be limited to certain types of generation (and if so, 
which types)? Yes/No 
 
Regarding the obligation of regulated CfDs for existing generation, HSE Group believes that this 
measure represents a threat for re -regulation of the energy sector. As proposed for example in the 
Spanish non -paper, also existing hydropower and nuclear would be subject to regulation and be 
obliged to enter CfDs, based on assumptions that these technologies are amortised and non -
contestable. This does certainly not hold true. In Slovenia for example significant investment in the 
hydropower sector have been made in the last years that have not yet been recovered. Potential for 
new investments also exists, and only recently a concession agreement for 1TWh  of new electricity 
from hydropower was signed. Slovenia is also planning to build a second unit of the Krško nuclear 
plant. We believe that long -term contracts, including PPAs, can play a critical role in large -scale 
investment in RES and low -carbon technologies which are capital intensive, while providing efficient 
hedging opportunities to suppliers and consumers, but entrusting the entire development of new 
renewables to a regulated scheme would undermine the competitiveness of the market.   
 
We agree with the Commission's own assessment in the consultation document itself that contrary to 
the situation for new generation, the CfDs “ for these types of existing generators would typically not 
result from market-based tendering but would result fr om ex-post price regulation. Whilst this would 
accelerate the uptake of contracts for difference, it would also create significant uncertainty for 
investors in renewables. This could risk the necessary investments in this type of generation, increase 
the costs of those investments and as a result be counterproductive.” 
 
Existing generation capacities should remain free to enter private long -term contracts such as PPA 
or to sell their production on the wholesale markets, state can have a role in helping reducing credit 
risks, but it is necessary to find a balance. Europe needs a sound framework of centralised procurement 
of RES, but we need to strive for decreasing risks and costs rather than increasing them.

1.4 ACCELERATING THE DEPLOYMENT OF RENEWABLES 
 
HSE Group welcomes the recent European Commission’s initiatives that aim to accelerate and facilitate 
renewable energy projects, focusing on key barriers that hinder RES project implementation. We are

1. Acceleration of permitting procedures with strict deadlines for obtaining permits, including

2. Establishment of public guarantee schemes for acceleration of PPAs. 
 
3. Application of the principle of overriding public interest to all RES technologies/projects as a

4. Designation of “go -to” areas for all RES  technologies, recognition of dual land use and the

stability by providing flexibility services. 
 
7. Promoting investment in large storage solutions, including pumped hydro power, considering 
larger shares of intermittent sources in the energy mix. 
 
8. The network tariff design that reflects the power system needs, reward and not burden the role

Q3: How should the necessary investments in network infrastructure be ensured? Are changes to the 
current network tariffs or other regulatory instruments necessary to further ensure that the grid 
expansion required will take place? 
 
In view of the necessary integration of RES into the grid, it is important to plan new generation 
capacities in a timely and coordinated manner, together with energy storage, near -demand 
production, aggregation of distributed renewable energy production and demand-side management. 
Unfortunately, the development of the network in certain areas is lagging behind and does not meet 
the needs of achieving ambitious RES and climate targets. 
 
An important aspect is the mutually coordinated planning of the transmission and distribution network 
and the development of production capacities in both systems. In addition to the objective of including 
a high share of RES, the objective of security of supply and long -term cost -effectiveness of the 
operation of the system must also be pursued. The concept of renewable electricity supply should take 
advantage of large production units and large energy storage units on the transmission network to 
ensure security of supply, while at the distribution level the focus should be  on the efficient use of 
distributed energy sources, with the possibility of consumption on-site or close to the production site 
combined with smaller energy storage units and other flexibility assets. 
 
In the short term, urgent network investments will need to be carried out to allow faster integration 
of all generation capacity already built and planned, that will require additional private and public 
funding, as all costs of the necessa ry network development and upgrading under the existing 
network operator’s methodology will not be able to be passed on to users. 
 
NETWORK TARRIF DESIGN 
 
Investments in infrastructure should be based on the principle, that final consumers and electricity 
storage facilities, whose investments and operations reduce the network load should be rewarded by 
lower (or omitted) network tariffs especially the energy storage facilities.  Inflexible consumers 
should be stimulated, by higher network tariffs, to invest in increasing their demand flexibility. 
Network tariffs should be flexible and transparent, supported by digitalised measurement system, 
providing consumers and storage facilities providers with online real -time load information, enabling 
them to optimise their load according to the dynamic network tariffs. 
When reviewing the (national) methodology for calculating network charges, it is necessary to take 
into account the specific role of energy storage systems, which they play in the operation of the 
electricity power system at the time of the increased share of variable renewable energy  sources, 
especially the role of larger storage systems, such as pumped hydro storage plants , which are 
connected to the transmission network.  Currently, 95% of EU’s storage capacity is represented by 
hydropower pumped storage, ensuring firmness, flexibility and reliability needs.  
The possibility of energy storage will be of increasing importance in the electricity system also in 
Slovenia, similar to that in other EU countries, in ensuring the safety and stability of the operation of 
the electricity system , especially in terms of  ensuring the necessary security of supply when coal is

phased out (to replace one 600 MW thermal unit in Slovenia, 2885 MW capacity of solar power plants

Pumped storage power plants provide primary and secondary regulation. In addition to the provision

In the HSE Group, we are of the opinion that it is essential that the status of the existing hydro pumped

Network fees for pumped hydro storage shou ld reflect the reference costs of the impact on an 
optimally constructed network, while at the same time stimulating the role of pumped hydro storage 
in ensuring the security of supply, offering system services, and also the environmental aspect of the 
technology used. 
 
We hereby also reiterate our opposition to a G -tariff implementation at national level.  The most 
important consequence of the introduction of a G-tariff would be  a lower profitability of new 
production facilities, which could slow down or even  stop the investment cycle in the energy sector 
and lead to large deficits in production capacities and higher import dependence in the long term. As 
a result, electricity would become more expensive for end consumers. 
 
1.5 LIMITING REVENUES OF INFRAMARGINAL GENERATORS 
 
HSE Group believes it is important to ensure long-term regulatory stability for investors in low-carbon 
and RES technologies. T he envisaged structural reform of the electricity market must include the 
minimum possibility of exceptional interventions that could lead to market distortions. Therefore: 
 
HSE Group strongly opposes any revenue limitations for inframarginal generators to be maintained 
beyond its current expiry date as a crisis intervention measure.

maintained?

including in Slovenia, has the aim to pass the raised surplus revenues on to final electricity customers 
to mitigate the impacts of exceptionally high electrici ty prices. This has been introduced as an 
emergency intervention measure and should not result in a long -term structural measure.  Any 
emergency measure, ad hoc by nature, implemented in times of crisis should be temporary and 
addressing specific needs of the situation.

Q2 Do you see alternatives to marginal pricing as regards the functioning of short -term markets in

HSE Group shares a general consensus within the energy sector that the marginal model is an optimal

Q1. What improvements into the REMIT framework do you consider as most important to be 
addressed immediately? 
 
REMIT Regulation/implementing regulations should provide for clear provisions regarding the 
definition of inside information, including thresholds for publication. 
 
Market participants manage large amounts of information daily that could potentially qualify as inside 
information regarding the unavailability of installations. As the definition of inside information, as it 
currently stands, lacks clarity on several of its aspects, including the likelihood of having a significant 
price effect, market participants, having to make quick decisions on publication, face a significant level 
of uncertainty when identifying and interpreting the necessity to publish certain information.  
 
To reduce implementation uncertainty, ensure a uniform interpretation, and enable market 
participants to make quick, efficient, and compliant decisions on publication, clear thresholds should 
be prescribed to identify, treat, and publish inside information. The definition of inside information is 
interpreted differently across the EU, on the level of different market participants as well as regulators. 
The definition of thresholds on EU or at least on national level for the publication of inside information 
would significantly reduce the uncertainty market participants are facing regarding the obligation to 
publish the inside information. 
 
REMIT Regulation/implementing regulations should provide for clear provisions regarding 
information concerning large retail customers. 
 
HSE Group proposes to modify the definition of “wholesale energy products” by deleting the reference 
in Article 2  point (4) of REMIT Regulation to “contracts for the supply and distribution of electricity or 
natural gas to final customers with a consumption capacity greater than the threshold set out in the 
second paragraph of point (5) shall be treated as wholesale energy products” as well as to 
“consumption capacity”.  
 
The definition leads to considerable interpretation issues for market participants  with the risk of 
assessment contributed in full to the market participant , since the term “consumption capacity” 
relates to a theoretical consumption on the basis of installed capacity, although the installed capacity 
is in no way related to actual consumption on the site. The uncertainty is reinforced by the fact that 
there are divergent provisions in the REMIT Regulation and in the Implementing Regulation.

HARMONISATION OF THE ENFORCEMENT REGIME 
 
HSE Group does not support possible harmonisation of fines that are imposed under REMIT at 
national level.  
 
The national sanctioning framework is based on broader national administrative and penal sanctioning 
structures and adjusted to national circumstances. Harmonization of fines would affect these 
structures in an excessive way.   
 
STRENGTHENING OF THE ENFORCEMENT REGIME 
 
Implementation uncertainty regarding the obligations of market participant if RRM fails to ensure 
the completeness, accuracy, and timeliness of the data or if the IIP is out of service should be 
reduced.  
 
Commission Implementing Regulation 1348/2014 clearly states in Article 11 that where a market 
participant reports data through a third party the market participant shall not be responsible for 
failures in the completeness, accuracy or timely submission of the data which are attributable to the 
third party.  
 
However, in conflict with this provision it also states that market participants shall nevertheless take 
reasonable steps to verify the completeness, accuracy, and timeliness of the data which they subm it 
through third parties. 
 
We believe this provision should be omitted. If a market participant uses third party RRM and has proof 
that reports have been successfully submitted to the RRM in a complete, accurate and timely manner, 
it should not be the resp onsibility of market participant to “nevertheless take reasonable steps to 
verify” that the RRM has submitted those reports to ACER in a complete, accurate and timely manner. 
It should be considered that the market participant has fulfilled its reporting o bligation, if the report 
has been successfully submitted to the registered third party RRM. In our opinion, it is ACER’s 
responsibility that only credible RRMs are registered for third party reporting. Market participants’ 
only responsibility and reasonabl e precaution in the case of choosing to report through third party 
RRM should be to verify, if the relevant RRM has a valid registration at ACER. 
 
As for IIPs, the REMIT Guidance clearly states that market participants are not responsible for 
temporary technical problems of IIPs fulfilling the minimum quality requirements. If the information 
was transmitted to the IIP in time and there were temporary technical problems, the market 
participant should therefore not be considered for having breached the obliga tion to disclose inside 
information.

However, Guidance goes on to state that in the event the technical problems persist and disclosure is 
delayed in such circumstance, the market participant will need to consider its compliance with the 
prohibition of insider trading prior to the information actually being disclosed to the market. 
 
The market participant would therefore need to continuously check whether the publication of 
information took place, and if not, refrain from using that information for market activities. We believe 
this to be an unreasonable ask, since its implementation would have to based on additional IT 
requirements and, most importantly, in would delay/hinder the necessary market decisions in 
circumstances where market participant has to make such decision as near real time as possible.  
 
We thus propose to amend the REMIT Regulation to make clear that in instances of technical problems 
of IIPs the market participant, making a decision on the basis of information not properly disclosed as 
a result of such technical problems, should not be held liable for insider trading prohibition breach.     
 
Enforcement powers of ACER regarding requirements for and responsibilities of RRMs and IIPs 
should be strengthened. 
 
The REMIT Regulation should be amended to include a definition of IIPs and RRMs and their respective 
responsibilities, thus strengthening ACER’s supervisory role regarding the requirements they have to 
fulfil and responsibilities they have to undertake.

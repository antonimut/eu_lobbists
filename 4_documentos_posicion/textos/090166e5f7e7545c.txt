MOL Group’s position on the Commission proposal for a regulation on packaging and packaging waste 
  
MOL Group is committed to helping the EU in reaching its circular economy objectives and is transforming 
to adapt to a circular and low -carbon world. We welcome the Commission proposal for a Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) as it has the pot ential to establish a well -functioning market for 
secondary raw materials through fully harmonised rules on packaging while tackling negative impacts on 
environment and health from packaging and packaging waste.  
Transition to a more circular economy requi res significant investments, and the PPWR can ensure 
regulatory certainty and increased incentives for investments in innovative solutions. The PPWR could 
also enable the contribution of the packaging sector to the EU’s climate neutrality goal, by setting up a 
framework based on  the full life -cycle impact of packaging and taking into account regrettable 
substitution. Below we summarised our key points related to the proposed regulation that we believe will 
be crucial to achieve these objectives. 
1. The exact rules and methodologies on defining recyclability and recycled content in plastic 
packaging must be elaborated as soon as possible (Art 6, 7)  
2. Recyclability rules should be defined in a way not to exclude innovative technologies (Art 6) 
3. Labelling on material composition would create unnecessary confusion for consumers (Art 11) 
4. We do not support restrictions and bans without the full assessment of alternative packagi ng 
materials (Art 22) 
5. The full lif e-cycle environmental impact of the re -use targets should be clarified, including CO2 
emissions, water consumption, air pollution (Art 26) 
6. Proposed rules for return and collection systems and deposit and return systems (DRS) should 
take into account creating efficiency and improving performance (Art 43-44. and Annex X.) 
 
Legal basis – internal market 
We welcome that Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) is and 
remains the legal basis of the regulation. This Article allows the EU to harmonise rules and administrative 
measures across Member States and allows harmonised application of rules.  
 
Article 5 – requirements for substances in packaging – should only relate to circularity 
We welcome the fact that the proposal does not aim to restrict substances due to chemical safety beyond 
the existing restriction on the concentration level of lead, cadmium, mercury and hexavalent chromium. 
The PPWR is indeed not the appropriate measure for regulating substances due to chemical safety. These 
are already addressed in REACH, and in other legislation which complements REACH, such as food contact 
materials (FCM) legislation.  
The concept of ‘substances of concern’ needs to be considered only in the context of circularity  i.e. 
recycling and reusability. This means that a substance that may be of concern in a particular product 
category (e.g. because there is potential exposure o r release during recycling) may not be of concern in 
another product category or in another recycling technology. In addition, recycling technologies evolve 
over time. The same is applicable for packaging and packaging materials.

Article 6 – all packaging to be recyclable – rules to be published in time before obligations; need to 
remain open to innovative technologies 
We support a focus on design for recycling (D4R)  as a way to  ensure that all packaging is recyclable by 
2030. However, the Commission should have a set deadline (by January 202 6 the latest) to develop the 
delegated acts referred to in Article 6 paragraph 4 , including the preferred design methods , to ensure 
sufficient time for the packaging industry to adopt the changes. Moreover, industry D4R guidelines, such 
as the ones initiated by the Circular Plastics Alliance and currently being elaborated by CEN (new Working 
group 10 at CEN TC 261) should be revised and approved with priority to avoid duplicating efforts. 
In terms of the recyclability at scale requirement, we are concerned that a narrow framework would limit 
the development of  innovative materials and recycling processes , such as chemical recycling . T he 
definition (in Article 3) of ‘recycled at scale’ already includes a 75% EU coverage requirement, which is a 
very ambitious target to achieve for new technologies. We believe such threshold should not be set in a 
definition, when the specific methodologies for packaging types to assess if those are recyclable at scale, 
referred to in Article 6 paragraph 6 , are yet to be  published. Rather than setting a threshold in the 
definitions, only the upcoming methodologies for each packaging type should set – if ap plicable – 
infrastructure related requirements. Even in those methodologies, the infrastructure related 
requirements should not be set as an EU -wide coverage requirement, but the regional realities of 
collection and recycling should be taken into account to allow some flexibility for new technologies . In 
addition, producers should have the opportunity to organise the at scale collection and recycling of their 
specific packaging types on their own cost  or through a Producer Responsibility Organisation (PRO), and 
keep those packages on the market as long as they can prove at scale recycling in the specific collection 
and recycling scheme they develop. Moreover, similarly to the D4R delegated acts, the Commission should 
have a deadline (January 2030 the latest) to develop the methodologies to assess if packaging is recyclable 
at scale otherwise, the delay of such methodologies could create significant uncertainty on the market. 
In terms of the modulation of the financial contributions to be paid by producers to comply with their 
extended producer responsibility obligations, Paragraph 11 states that these shall be modulated on the 
basis of the recyclability performance grade, and for plastic packaging also in accordance with the Article 
7(6) based on the percentage of recycled content used. For plastic packaging, a prioritization should be 
set regarding these two criteria, otherwise there is a risk of having lower EPR fees for plastic packaging 
with recycled content, even if they are not recyclable. In addition, the eco-modulated fee structure should 
not create an uneven playing field for those applications where the uptake of recycled content is not 
technically feasible and therefore not even mandatory based on the proposal (e.g. pharmaceutical 
immediate packaging).  
 
Article 7 – Minimum recycled content in plastic packaging – calculation methodology needed as soon 
as possible 
The chemical industry is now going through the circular transition and is currently investing in significant 
chemical recycling capabilities. Setting the implementing act on establishing the methodology for the 
calculation and verification of the percentage of recycled content , including how chemically recycled 
materials will be accounted for , only by 31 Dec ember 2026, is too far in the future and unnecessarily 
prolongs legal uncertainty. For boosting the circular economy  it is important to have a supporting legal 
framework – including a robust and transparent mass balance chain of custody system , creating a leve l 
playing field for all technologies – clarified as soon as possible (by 2025 the latest),  as major investment 
decisions depend on having this legal certainty. The deadline for the Commission should also be set earlier

because even with a deadline set, del ays in publishing those acts are frequent (e.g. for the Single -use 
Plastics Directive, the methodology for the calculation and verification of the percentage of recycled 
content has been delayed for more than a year), which can further increase investment uncertainty. 
Regarding the level of the targets, we miss a detailed feasibility and impact assessment of the 2040 targets 
(e.g. is it possible to reach 50% recycled content in food packaging by 2040 so that no hazardous 
substances migrate from the packaging into the food; can the 65% recycled content ratio be achieved so 
that the material can be recycled several times without any strong degradation or loss of quality). 
 
Article 8 – compostable packaging – clear disposal instructions needed to maximise their potential 
We agree with the proposal that for limited packaging applications made of biodegradable plastic 
polymers, there is a demonstrable environmental benefit of using compostable packaging, which enters 
composting plants, including anaerobic digestion facilities under controlled conditions. 
At the same time, we fully support the provision that packaging made of biodegradable plastic polymers, 
shall allow material recycling without affecting the recyclability of other waste streams,  in order to avoid 
consumer confusion about the correct disposal.  Sorting systems must be developed in a way to be able 
to separate biodegradable materials.  
 
Article 9 – packaging minimisation – supported  
We welcome the provision for packaging to be designed so that its weight and volume is reduced to the 
minimum necessary for ensuring its functionality taking account of the material that the packaging is 
made of, as well as its overall environmental impact. 
 
Article 11 – Labelling of packaging – not supported for material composition 
While we support harmonised labelling on recycling instructions, reusability and recycled content of 
packaging, as well as labelling on waste bins to incentivise proper consumer behaviour, we do not see the 
value in mandatory labelling on material composition, e.g. polymer types. The labelling should only be as 
detailed as needed for consumers to understand how to recycle that package. We believe that information 
on material composition would just create unnecessary confus ion for consumers, while the objective of 
empowering consumers for the green transition can be achieved by other elements of the label, such as 
recycling and reuse instructions . It is the task of the waste management company during sorting to 
distinguish and separate the collected waste per material and polymer.  
On the other hand, we support disclosing material composition with d igital watermarking technologies, 
as these have  proven to enable the sorting of even  shredded plastics . However, this requires the 
watermarking all over the plastic part. To use digital marking technologies on a label makes only partially 
sense, if the container is not marked. For simplified identification, the complete plastic part should have 
a digital watermark.

Article 22 – restrictions on the use of certain packaging formats – assessment of alternatives needed  
We do not support restrictions without a full assessment of alternative solutions. The restrictions on 
certain single -use packaging formats should only be set in a way that they  take into account  any 
unintended consequences, and make sure that the functionality of the packaging is retained and that 
other parameters (such as the protection of packaged products and the resources required for packaging 
production) are not adversely affected. If these conditions are not met by the alternative solution, instead 
of banning single-use applications, rather their proper collection and recycling should be ensured. It also 
should be ensured that this Article is in line with the provisions of the Single-use Plastics Directive, which 
also includes bans and consumption reduction targets for certain items.  
In terms of the list of packaging formats and p urposes listed in Annex V, the table should be carefully 
designed not to cause any misunderstanding and clarify that the ‘illustrative examples’ are only restricted 
if used in the restricted use  (e.g. single-use plastic grouped packaging in collation films is mostly used to 
facilitate distribution, while the restricted use should at maximum be those grouped packages that do not 
have a distribution function, and are only used to incentivise consumption). 
 
Article 26 – Re-use and refill targets – maintain a holistic approach  
In line with the waste hierarchy, we support reuse targets. However, when setting the targets, the PPWR 
should strive for a holistic assessment of the sustainability of packaging.  
The impact assessment states that for setting  the targets, a scoping process assessed the full packaging 
range against six criteria for its respective re-use potential (e.g. “Does the multiple-use option meet the 
functional requirements of containment/tidiness, health/hygiene, and safety?”, “Does the multiple -use 
option decrease the generation of waste?”, “How many reusable models are available or in place for a 
given product/packaging (e.g., business-to-business, refill at home, etc.)?”, “Is the reuse system easy to 
implement and apply?”). While we welcome that hygiene and safety aspects were considered, the full 
life-cycle environmental impact of the re -use targets should als o be clarified, including CO2 emissions, 
water consumption, air pollution. As an example, it should be carefully assessed, especially for transport 
packaging, whether the reusable options increase the size and weight of packaging and thereby increase 
CO2 emissions of logistics.  
 
Article 38 – prevention of packaging waste – EU-wide unified target not proportional 
The targets should take into account that Member States below the EU average of per capita packaging 
waste generation are still in the economic development phase which increases the complexity of products 
and the amount of products and packaging placed on the market. Therefore, EU -wide per capita targets 
might pose a higher burden on these Member States, while the focus should be to address the waste 
generation in Member States with per capita waste generation way above the EU average. In addition, 
the amount of waste per capita also depends on the quality of collection and sorting, selection culture 
and statistical data provision.

Article 41 – Producer Responsibility Organisation – coordinated measures supported 
We take note of paragraphs 1 and 2, and support that Member States may adopt measures to make the 
entrustment of a producer responsibility organisation mandatory , as well as to oversee that producer 
responsibility obligations are fulfilled in a coordinated manne r, as these can contribute to achieving the 
environmental objectives of this regulation. 
 
Article 42 – Authorisation on fulfilment of extended producer responsibility  – flexibility for 
Member States supported 
We support that Member States have the flexibility to lay down administrative and procedural rules, as 
well as to establish the requirements and details of the authorisation procedure, which can be different 
for either individual or collective fulfilment of the extended producer responsibility. 
 
Article 43 – return and collection systems – performance and efficiency should be the main driver 
Regarding point (a) of paragraph 3 – according to which the system must be open to the participation of 
the economic operators of the sectors concerned, the  competent public authorities and third parties 
carrying out waste management on their behalf –, we believe that for the sake of efficient waste 
management, Member States and third parties acting on their behalf should have the opportunity to 
subcontract selected parties who are responsible to carry out the waste management of packaging waste. 
 
Article 44 and Annex X – minimum criteria for deposit and return systems (DRS) – performance and 
efficiency should be the main driver 
In addition to allowing for equal access and fair conditions of all economic operators wishing to become 
part of the system, Member States should have the power to make in mandatory for operators to join, 
provided they make available on the market packagin g that belongs to a packaging type or category 
included in the system. 
We do not support point (f) of Annex X in its current form, stating that the DRS system operator should 
be a non-profit legal entity . While we agree that the legal entity should be independent from the 
producers and the authorities included in the system, in our opinion it should not be required that this 
entity be non-profit. It is already questionable that independence requirements are as strict as for energy 
infrastructure operato rs, but the non -profit expectation goes far beyond this strong operational 
requirement. The non-profit requirement is not serving the purpose of this regulation and does not make 
sense economically in the context of stimulating investments for the developm ent of such large central 
systems, as well as  their safe, long -term sustainable and responsible operation . I n fact, based on  
experience in  other industr ies, this could be counterproductive. The DRS system is not a consumer 
grassroots community, nor should it be confused with such local sustainability initiatives. So, the non -
profit expectation would not serve the system and its long -term development, thus would not serve 
consumer needs and the fulfillment of waste management and environmental protection objectives. Also 
a for-profit entity can and is obliged to make sure that the deposits and material revenue from the system 
are reinvested in the DRS to maintain and develop a high performing system , with producers – in 
accordance with the relevant national legislation and EU requirements – only funding the necessary costs.

In addition to the appropriate economic conditions and regulatory environment necessary to stimulate 
investments and developments, there are several advantages to an operator having a for profit experience 
and mindset, as this can help establish the necessary efficiency and  continuously improve the overall 
performance, while maintaining transparency in the system.  An efficient and high performing system is 
needed to contribute to the environmental objectives of this Article as well as the PPWR itself. 
Similarly, w e do not support point (g) of Annex X stating that the system operator should perform 
exclusively roles arising from the rules of the PPWR, and any additional roles related to the coordination 
and operation of the deposit and return system as established by the Member States.  This is an 
unnecessary separation requirement that is completely unjustified from a professional point of view and 
strongly hinders the operation of integrated systems for building a circular economy. There are several 
advantages to an operator utilising its expertise acquired from other (e.g. EPR) roles and the related 
infrastructure, IT system and partner network built for those roles , as this can help create the necessary 
efficiency, including cost efficiency, and performance needed to achieve environmental objectives. Having 
other roles outside of the DRS coordination and operation do es not preclude the scheme operator from 
operating transparently, being properly auditable by the authorities, as well as regularly preparing reports 
on performance and financial accounts in order to ensure transparency for governments and - obviously 
with a narrower content - for producers. 
We support point (m) of Annex X stating that deposit is exempted from sales taxes. 
We believe it should be clarified what regions with “high transboundary business” means, in which it is a 
requirement to ensure that the functioning of the DRS allows for the inter-operability of DRS. Without a 
proper definition this provision could result in diverging implementation.  Guidance is needed on the 
requirements for setting up inter-operability in practice. 
 
Article 52-54 – packaging presenting a risk – might increase enforcement difficulty  
We recommend the removal of provisions for packaging that presents a risk / serious risk at national level. 
Packaging can either comply with the provisions of the regulation, and so it can be placed on the market, 
or it cannot. Introducing new categories might unnecessarily complicate enforcement.

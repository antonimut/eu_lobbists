Summary of key concerns and observations 
 Active account-requirement: 
 Careful design, calibration and implementation of the active account-requirement and its 
phasing-in, including the ability to introduce changes quickly when needed, is essential 
to avoid distortion of competition within the EU and on international level and other 
unwanted / harmful effects. Consequently, it is important and appropriate that the 
details are to be determined by RTS.  
 In order to avoid harmful effects and potentially severe disruptions, the active account-
requirement should be structured as a two-stage system – the first stage being based on 
qualitative criteria without quantitative targets. Quantitative targets should only be 
introduced if the qualitative approach does not – over time - lead to the desired 
outcome. 
 In order to safeguard continued access of EU-institutions to international markets and 
customers, market making activities of EU clearing members and clearing activities for 
3rd-country clients must be disregarded when calculating the proportion of contracts 
counting towards the active account-requirement.  
 It is essential that the requirement is applied consistently and in a coordinated manner 
across the EU. 
 The classification of EUR-CDS clearing as systemically important needs to be 
reconsidered following ICE Europe’s recent decision to phase-out its services. The 
extension of the active account-requirement to PLN-IRS and EUR-STIR should also be 
reviewed in view of the limited liquidity. 
 Timelines should not be too ambitious and there will be a need for clarity over the 
extension of the equivalency decision regarding UK CCPs as early as possible. 
 CRD/IFD planning + target setting obligations: The new obligations combined with the 
new supervisory enforcement powers are a disproportionate and inappropriate tool to address 
the effects of the ESMA decisions to classify certain derivative classes as systemically 
important and are likely to result in far-reaching interferences with the core business models 
and client relations of institutions. 
 New calculation outcomes and 3rd country clearing activities reporting obligations: 
The new reporting obligations regarding calculation outcomes and 3rd country clearing 
activities are largely unnecessary in view of already existing EMIR reporting obligations and 
should at least limited to key information not already covered by existing reporting obligations. 
 EU clearing options information: A trade-by-trade information obligation conflicts with 
established clearing processes and workstreams. It should therefore be clarified that the 
obligation to inform clients of alternative EU-CCP clearing options can be addressed in a more 
general/standardised form. 
 CCP margin models + potential losses information obligation: Institutions can only 
provide information that it is publicly available or provided by CCPs. CCPs should therefore be 
subjected to parallel obligations to provide all relevant information to clearing members. 
 3rd-country pension funds exemption: The exemption is helpful and necessary to prevent 
serious competitive disadvantages for EU-institutions in international markets. 
 Intragroup exemptions: The new listing-based approach is an important and helpful 
simplification of the very complex procedures yet needs some refinement, e.g., regarding 
adaptation periods in case of future changes. 
 IM-model validation: The deletion of the requirement to develop technical standards 
concerning IM-model validation is a helpful reduction of formalities and complexities, allowing 
for a more flexible approach. 
 Simplified CCP recognition process: The new risk-based approach for the recognition of 
Tier 1 third-country CCPs simplifies the process significantly and could greatly help to maintain 
access to important third-country markets for EU-market participants.

I. Overview 
 
1.Background 
 
The German banking industry continues to support the European Commission’s (COM) initiative to 
increase the attractiveness and resilience of the EU clearing landscape and therefore generally supports 
the package of measures set out in the proposals for  
‒ a regulation amending Regulations 648/2021 (EMIR), 575/2013 (CRR) and 2017/1131 (MMFR) - 
hereinafter referred to as the Amending Regulation, and 
‒ a Directive amending the Directives 2009/65 (UCITD), 2013/36 (CRD) and 2019/2034 (IFD) - 
hereinafter referred to as Amending Directive. 
 
The changes introduced hereby can roughly be grouped into four different – sometimes overlapping -
categories: 
 
 Active account-requirement and connected/supporting measures 
 
The centrepiece is the introduction of a new obligation under EMIR to establish and maintain active 
accounts with an EU-CCP and to clear a certain proportion of derivative contracts belonging to 
derivative classes deemed systemically relevant through these accounts (active account-
requirement). The active account-requirement is supported by targeted complementary measures 
incentivising clearing in the EU, and also the introduction of new specific regulatory obligations and 
supervisory powers under the CDR and IFD compelling institutions to actively reduce their exposure 
towards third-country CCPs. 
 
 Simplification of regulatory framework and procedures 
 
The proposals also include a set of changes streamlining and simplifying certain regulatory 
requirements and especially procedures thereby reducing complexities and avoiding unnecessary 
competitive disadvantages for EU market participants. 
 
 Increased market transparency 
 
In addition, the proposals introduce a number of targeted measures aimed at increasing transparency 
for the supervisory authorities and also market participants over certain markets and activities. 
 
 General reform of regulatory framework for CCPs 
 
Lastly, the proposals provide for a comprehensive restructuring and modernisation of the general 
regulatory framework for CCPs. 
 
 
2. General assessment 
 
We believe that the active account-requirement (if carefully designed to avoid damaging the 
competitiveness of EU market participants) can be an important and effective step to gradually increase 
clearing in EU. It can constitute a key component of a long-term strategy to foster efficient, resilient and 
internationally competitive clearing markets within the EU.

However, certain elements in the COM proposals raise concerns and should be reconsidered or adjusted: 
 
 As regards the package of measures centering on or supporting active account-requirement – which 
includes the changes in the CRD and IFD which have to be seen in conjunction with the active account-
requirement – the following needs to be noted: 
 
 The active account-based approach may appear to be less aggressive and intrusive than more 
direct measures forcing market participants to clear in the EU (forced relocation). However, while 
the proposal indicates that the requirement is to be phased-in over time, it fails to provide clarity 
regarding the specifics, in particular the manner and timing of the phase-in, the proportion of 
derivative contracts eventually expected to be cleared in the EU and necessary exemptions. Thus, 
unless very carefully designed, calibrated and applied, this active account-requirement can, in 
effect,  amount to a forced relocation with all of the same lasting and very damaging ramifications 
for EU market participants and EU financial markets. In this context it should be taken into 
account that – in contrast to EU market participants – many non-EU-institutions have full access 
to the EU markets while also retaining access to international markets without any of the 
restrictions and limitations under the EU regulatory regime allowing them to offer clients the full 
range of clearing options on all international markets.  
 
 Neither this active account-based approach nor any more aggressive direct measures can 
overcome the core challenge to establishing a fully viable, balanced and competitive EU clearing 
market, which is the fact that even the liquidity in EUR denominated derivatives contracts is 
primarily provided by non-EU market participants (contributing the by far largest share): This 
disparity between the share of the market liquidity generated by EU and non-EU-market 
participants means that a forced relocation or measures amounting thereto will always result in a 
structurally unbalanced and significantly less liquid EU-clearing market and can also effectively 
cut-off EU institutions from international markets and business.  
 
We therefore strongly urge for certain adjustments and clarifications in order to ensure that the 
package of measures intended to make clearing in the EU more attractive and to increase the 
resilience of the EU clearing market does not achieve the opposite of its intended purpose and also 
severely reduces the international competitiveness of the EU financial markets and EU institutions. 
 
 As regards the measures simplifying certain procedures, we believe that these are indeed important 
steps towards a reduction of unnecessary complexities and help to make the EU clearing framework 
more efficient and attractive. 
 
 As regards the measures aimed at increasing transparency, we understand and generally support the 
objectives. However, at least certain elements should be reviewed in order to avoid duplications of 
already existing reporting obligations and prevent unnecessary and disproportionate burdens for EU 
market participants and counterproductive effects. 
 
 As regards the general reform of the regulatory framework for CCPs, we see the need for an 
adaptation and further modernisation. As this primarily affects the interrelation of supervisory 
authorities and the internal governance we only comment on selected aspects and only in a general 
manner in our responses to individual elements of the proposal in Section III.

II. Key concerns / observations 
 
1. Active account-requirement – new Art. 7a EMIR 
 
a) Design, calibration, implementation of active account-requirement 
 
The active account-requirement as currently proposed includes the obligation to clear a certain 
proportion of derivatives transaction in derivatives classes deemed to systemically important via an 
EU-authorised (and thus EU-based CCP). The concrete design and metrics for determining this 
proportion as well as the applicable calculation methods are to be set via regulatory technical 
standards (RTS). While we understand and support the approach to rely on RTS for the design of any 
metrics (see below) and we also note that the proposal envisages a phasing-in (see e.g. recital 11), 
we believe that the objective to increase clearing in the EU in a sustainable manner can best be 
achieved by structuring the active account-requirement as a two-stage system: In the first stage, the 
active-accounts should be based on qualitative requirements only, in the form of an obligation to 
open and maintain operative accounts with an EU-CCP. The active nature can-  in this stage - be 
effectively and easily demonstrated and monitored through the daily margin payments to CCPs. This 
qualitative approach alone should already strongly incentivise market participants to shift clearing 
activities to EU-CCPs to a considerable extent and generate momentum for shifting positions. Only 
where this qualitative approach does not – over time – lead to the desired outcome, quantitative 
targets could be phased-in as a second, escalatory stage. The timeline for the relevant observation 
period for the first stage has to be sufficiently long. 
 
This second stage based on quantitative targets will need to be designed with great care and the 
relevant metrics will have to be calibrated with great precision. The design and metrics chosen must 
not only allow for gradual/incremental modelling of the targets over time (phasing-in), but also have 
to permit the swift implementation of necessary corrections, recalibrations and adjustments in 
reaction to market developments or unwanted and disproportionate effects. ESMA will - in this 
context - need to be afforded considerable flexibility regarding the design details for the phasing-in, 
including regarding the manner, timing and extent to which product categories and sub-categories 
thereof are to be affected. 
 
The RTS-based approach furthermore provides for a way to adequately ensure that certain activities 
necessarily associated with an institution’s participation in international markets, memberships with 
third-country market infrastructures and the provision of services to international clients, such as 
market-making activities or clearing services for third-country clients, can be disregarded in the 
calculations or are otherwise excluded. These activities are essential to safeguard the continued 
access to and competitiveness of an institution in international markets. It also has to be taken into 
account that the decision whether and where to clear is always made by the client and not the 
clearing service provider. Especially non-EU clients are unlikely to clear via EU-CCPs as they usually 
have multi-currency portfolios which currently cannot be served with the same breadth and liquidity 
by EU-CCPs. 
 
In addition, legacy contracts which have been accepted for clearing before the entry into force of the 
new requirements also need to be excluded from the scope of the obligations.

In order to avoid uncertainties and disruptions, the exclusion from the scope of the above mentioned 
market-making activities, client clearing services for non-EU clients and legacy transactions should 
be expressly addressed in the Amending regulation itself.  
 
b) Consistent and coordinated application 
 
In order to prevent a distortion of competition between EU-institutions and also on an international 
level, it will be necessary that supervisory authorities apply the active account-requirement 
consistently and in a coordinated manner with consideration of the business model of the institution. 
This needs to include a close coordination regarding the additional obligations and supervisory 
powers under the CRD and IFD (see also below).  
 
c) Timelines 
 
The objective to strengthen clearing in the EU has to be a long term project. Accordingly, the 
timelines to be set need to be realistic and cannot be too ambitious and rigid. The upcoming changes 
to the clearing framework in the EU will require considerable operational changes and preparations. 
All this means that market participants will need sufficient lead time. It is also already clear that the 
time until the scheduled ending of the current extension of the equivalency decision in June 2025 will 
neither be sufficient for the introduction of the operational changes and the necessary adjustments 
by market participants nor for seeing the impact of the active account-requirement on the markets. 
However, market participants need certainty and clarity over the continued future access to UK CCPs 
sufficiently in advance of the date. This issue should be resolved in or in conjunction with the 
Amending Regulation. 
 
d) Material scope: 
 
Under the proposal, the active account-requirement is to cover the following derivatives categories 
(being deemed to be of systemic relevance for the EU):  
‒ EUR denominated interest rate swaps (EUR-IRS) 
‒ Zloty-denominated interest rate swaps (PLN-IRS) 
‒ EUR-denominated short term interest rate futures (EUR-STIR) and  
‒ EUR-denominated credit default swaps (EUR-CDS).  
 
 EUR-CDS: Reconsideration of systemic importance 
 
The classification of EU-CDS clearing via ICE Clear Europe Limited as systemically important will 
need to be reconsidered following the recent announcement by Ice Clear Europe Limited to phase-
out the relevant clearing activities. 
 
 EUR STIR and PLN-IRS: Unsuitability because of lacking liquidity  
 
The markets in PLN-IRS and  EUR-STIR are significantly less broad and liquid than the EUR-IRS 
markets. They are also of limited systemic relevance.  
 
The active account-requirement is likely to produce split markets for the affected derivatives 
categories and there is a clear risk that this will result in a balanced and liquid non-EU market on 
the one hand and a less balanced and significantly less liquid EU-market on the other. This risk

will be particularly pronounced in case of these significantly less liquid derivatives categories. The 
inclusion of PLN-IRS and EUR-STIR in the scope of the active account-requirement should 
therefore be reconsidered. 
 
2. Exposure reduction planning and target setting obligation for institutions – Art. 76 (2) 
CRD/Art. 29 (1) IFD and corresponding new supervisory exposure reduction enforcement 
power – Art. 104 (1) (n) CRD/Art. 39 (2) (b) IFD 
 
The obligation to develop specific plans and set targets for a reduction of the concentration risk 
towards third-country CCPs in systemically relevant derivatives categories, together with a new 
supervisory power to actively enforce an exposure reduction by requiring an institution to “realign” or 
reduce their positions where deemed an excessive concentration risk, is a very aggressive and 
invasive tool which can result in a significant interferences with core business activities of an 
institution, client relations and the business model of an institution, and its general competitiveness. 
 
Against this background and especially in view of the fact that the explanatory memorandum for the 
proposal of the Amending Directive itself states that the competent supervisors already possess 
powers to adequately address excessive concentration risks under the existing CRD-framework (see 
page 8/Art. 81 CRD), the introduction of such new far-reaching and potentially very intrusive 
obligations and powers should be reconsidered.  
 
At the very least it would need to be ascertained that such new obligations and powers will be 
applied with utmost care, full consideration of the business model and the potential implications for 
the business activities of the relevant institution and in all material aspects consistently and in a 
coordinated manner across the EU in order to prevent a distortion of competition and other harmful 
effects. Should these new regulatory requirements and enforcement powers under the CRD and IFD 
indeed be introduced despite the above concerns it would specifically be necessary to clearly set out 
in the Amending Regulation and Amending Directive how these new requirements and powers under 
the CRD and IFD regime are supposed to interact with the active account-requirement under the 
parallel EMIR regulatory regime, especially considering the potentially diverging regulatory objectives 
(strengthening of EU-clearing in case of EMIR and reduction of concentration risks in case of the CRD 
and IFD). In particular, it is vital that the necessary exemptions regarding market-making activities, 
non-EU client clearing and legacy transactions regarding the EMIR based active account-requirement 
(see above) are also fully recognised and/or incorporated into CRD and IFD based requirements 
concerning the reduction of concentration risk in order to avoid a fundamental regulatory divergence 
and severe and lasting adverse effects for the international competitiveness of EU institutions. 
 
Without full recognition /alignment of the relevant exemptions and clear rules on the interaction 
between the two separate regulatory regimes, metrics and thresholds would be set independently by 
different regulators with potentially very significant differences in the outcomes. The existence of two 
separate regulatory regimes with differing objectives and separate enforcement powers is also likely 
to cause additional duplicative and even conflicting regulatory burdens for market participants. 
 
A further unresolved question is, how this new regulatory powers under the CRD and IFD are 
intended to address concentration risks vis-à-vis EU-CCPs and how this can be aligned with the 
objectives of the EMIR regime.

In any event we understand that – since these obligations and powers are intended to reduce 
concentration risks – institutions will be able to address any excessive concentration identified by the 
supervisory authority by spreading their exposure across various CCPs, including other third-country 
CCPs. 
 
 
3. New reporting obligations regarding calculation outcomes and third-country CCP clearing 
activity– new Art. 7a (4) and new Art. 7b (2) EMIR 
 
a) Calculation outcomes obligation 
 
The introduction of another new annual reporting obligation regarding calculation and third-
country CCP exposure is unnecessary in view of the already existing the existing EMIR 
transaction reporting obligations to trade repositories (TR-reporting obligations). If retained, it 
should at least be limited to key information not already available through the existing TR-
reporting obligations. In case of the CCP-exposure reporting obligation it is also unclear what the 
terms “other financial instruments or non-financial contracts” or “largest payment obligation” 
intend to capture and why such information is needed for the purposes of the active account-
requirement. 
 
b) Clearing activities reporting obligation 
 
The need for the new reporting obligation established under Art. 7b (2) concerning clearing 
activities via third-country CCPs has to be questioned or at the very least reduced to the 
otherwise unavailable key information in view of the already existing TR-reporting obligations. 
This obligation covers most if not all of the relevant information as it covers the derivative 
contracts classes, valuations of the contracts and margin posted and received. Should the 
reporting obligation be retained despite this concern it should at the very least be limited to the 
absolute minimum and clearly defined key information. In this connection we note that it is 
unclear what the proposal attempts to cover with information on the “largest payment obligation” 
and why this information is necessary. Likewise, it is unclear what the proposal intends to cover 
with “other financial instruments and non-financial contracts cleared”, especially considering that 
the active account-requirement only concerns derivatives contracts so that any information 
requirement should accordingly be limited to these types of contracts. The – undefined – terms 
“financial instruments and non-financial contracts cleared” are also used in Art. 14 (provisions 
regarding the authorisation of CCPs). 
 
The establishment and maintenance of the operational framework and processes for such a new and 
broad reporting obligation will be very burdensome for all affected market participants. To the extent 
the necessary information is already covered by other, already existing reporting obligations, the 
introduction of such new obligation would also be clearly disproportionate.  
 
 
4. Client information on alternative EU clearing possibilities – new Art. 7b (1) EMIR 
 
The current proposal can be read to require a trade-by-trade information obligation. This would 
fundamentally conflict with the existing established clearing processes and workstreams which do not 
allow for the provision of such specific information on a trade by trade basis. Such an understanding

Comments on the Amending Regulation EMIR/CRR/MMFR and Amending Directive UCITS/CRD/IFD 
would therefore require far reaching procedural changes which would again cause significant delays 
and inefficiencies. Clearing clients are professional market participants and thus very much aware of 
the set-up of the clearing markets and possible alternatives and therefore do not need a trade-by-
trade reminder. It should therefore be clarified that the obligation for clearing members and clients 
offering clearings services both via EU and 3rd country CCPs to inform clients of the possibility to 
clear via an EU-CCP can be implemented in a more general, standardised form.  It should also be 
considered that where transactions are entered into on trading venues, the CCP is already fixed. 
 
 
5. Client information obligation regarding CCP Margin models: – Art. 38 (7) EMIR 
 
Clearing services offering institutions can only provide information on the margin models of a CCP as 
well as the way they are intended to work, and provide simulations, if and only to the extent they 
themselves receive the relevant information and data from the relevant CCPs. Otherwise, they will 
not be in a position to comply with the new information obligations in a meaningful way or be forced 
to provide incomplete or even unverified and potentially misleading information.  
 
Thus, the introduction of such new information obligation for clearing service providers needs to be 
accompanied by a parallel obligation on CCPs to provide the relevant information and data to their 
clearing members. 
 
Furthermore, it should be considered to introduce a de-minims threshold for this information 
requirement in view of the very considerable burdens it will pose for clearing service providers: 
Clients with very limited clearing positions do not require such detailed and complex information for 
their liquidity management. Moreover, the considerable burdens associated with this obligation, 
especially, if applicable to all clients indiscriminately, will even further discourage institutions to offer 
client clearing services. 
 
 
6. CCP potential loss information: Client information obligation – Art. 38 (8) EMIR 
 
As in case of the information requirement regarding margin models, clearing services offering 
institutions can only provide information on potential losses caused by default management 
procedures if and to the extent the relevant CCP provides information to the clearing members. The 
introduction of this information obligation for clearing service providers thus also needs to be 
accompanied by a parallel obligation on CCPs to provide the relevant information and data to their 
clearing members. 
 
In addition, it has to be noted that it will never be possible to quantify potential losses with sufficient 
accuracy or certainty. This needs to be reflected to avoid any misconceptions. 
 
 
7. Third-country pension funds exemption – Art. 4 (1) EMIR 
 
The proposed introduction of a new sub-paragraph setting out an exemption from the clearing 
obligation for third-country pension funds which are not themselves subject to a clearing obligation 
under the regulatory regime applicable to them ensures that EU-institution can continue to be active 
in international markets under the same conditions as their international competitors. Retaining

clearing obligation in relation to these third-country pension funds would mean that EU-institutions 
would be internationally uncompetitive with regard to this important client segment. 
However, to ensure that this measure achieves its intended effect, the existing CRR-requirements 
and exemptions regarding the CVA-risk would have to adjusted accordingly in order to avoid that 
transactions with third-country pension funds do not become punitive capital charges. 
 
 
8. New intragroup exemption concept (listing approach) – new Art. 3 (4) and (5) EMIR 
 
The introduction of a simplified new approach for the intragroup exemption under which the 
intragroup exemption does not apply to entities operating in jurisdictions classified as high-risk for 
money laundering purposes, or non-cooperative for tax purposes or otherwise identified by the EU 
Commission as non-eligible for the intragroup exemption, significantly reduces the complexities and 
burdens for all involved parties and thus increases the efficiency of the EU regulatory framework. 
 
However, authorising the EU Commission to add countries to the high-risk list (beyond AML and non-
cooperative issues) is not necessary and would – without further qualifications regarding the EU 
Commission’s powers in this respect – be disproportionate. 
 
In addition, it will be necessary to include an adaptation period in case of future changes to the list 
since the addressees of the obligations will require sufficient time to implement the required 
changes. 
 
Furthermore, the deletion of the mechanism (Art 13) to avoid duplicative or conflicting rules should 
be reinstated. 
 
 
9. Initial Margin model validation – deletion of Art. 11 (15) (aa) EMIR 
 
The deletion of the currently existing Art. 11 (15) (aa) requiring ESMA to develop RTS for the 
supervisory procedures regarding the ongoing validation of IM-models is a helpful reduction of 
formalities and complexities. More importantly, it allows for a more flexible approach. The necessary 
consistency within the EU can be achieved via EBA guidelines recommendations if and to the extent 
necessary, which EBA is now entitled to issue under the newly introduced new subparagraph in Art. 
11 (3) EMIR. 
 
 
10. Recognition of third-country CCPs – Art. 25 (4) EMIR 
 
The introduction of a risk-based approach for the recognition procedure regarding systemically less 
relevant Tier 1 third-country CCPs, in particular allowing for a recognition without ascertaining full 
equivalence of the regulatory framework for CCPs, simplifies the recognition process significantly 
without increasing systemic risks. This can greatly help to ensure and maintain access for EU 
institutions to important third-country markets by significantly accelerating the complex and time-
consuming recognition process. This risk-based simplification is fully consistent with the distinction 
EMIR makes regarding the recognition procedure for Tier 1 and Tier 2 third-country CCPs.

8. Art. 1 (21) AR / Art. 25 (4) EMIR: Simplification of recognition of third-country CCPs 
The introduction of a risk-based approach for the recognition procedure regarding systemically 
less relevant Tier 1 third-country CCPs, in particular allowing for a recognition without 
ascertaining full equivalency of the regulatory framework for CCPs, simplifies the recognition 
process significantly without increasing systemic risks. This can greatly help to ensure and 
maintain access for EU institutions to important third-country markets by greatly accelerating the 
complex and time-consuming recognition process. This risk-based simplification is fully consistent 
with the distinction EMIR makes regarding the recognition procedure for Tier 1 and Tier 2 third-
country CCPs.

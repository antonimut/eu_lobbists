FNADE’s position paper on Urban Waste Water Treatment 
Directive revision  
 
February 2023 
 
FNADE, the French association for waste management and environmental services, welcomes the 
European Commission’s draft on the revision of Urban Waste Water Treatment (UWWT) Directive: 
Directive 91/271/EEC which was adopted in 1991 really needs new provisi ons to address some 
issues and to be consistent with the European objectives of zero pollution ambition.  
FNADE’s members support this revision proposal as it aims to better control urban water pollution 
with simple and clear objectives of results likely t o limit distortions between Member States. It 
should also provide for local authorities an improved legal framework while tackling new societal 
challenges (i.e. phasing out micropollutants, microplastics...).  FNADE also appreciates that the 
proposal encourages efficient water and energy management to reduce greenhouse gas emissions 
and accelerate s the transition to the circular economy. With provisions on improving access to 
waste water treatment for all , and improving transparency, the propo sal addresses the challenges 
of the 21st century. 
Nevertheless, FNADE, whose members are involved in the waste  water treatment and sludge 
recovery, would like to take the opportunity to make some comments and provide the European 
Commission with some inputs on major topics: 
 
Prevention and insure waste water treatment quality: 
Respect of waste hierarchy principle: mandatory pre-treatment steps and separation at source: 
Water, a fundamental resource, must not be wasted and the waste hierarchy principle should also 
be required and applied for waste water. Industrial waste water should not be mixed with domestic 
waste water, as it is already required in France . Similarly, lightly loaded waste water should not be 
mixed with heavily loaded water. For example, in France, dentists have an obligation to treat their 
waste water separately so as not to discharge pollutants into the domestic waste water system. This 
would help to reduce treatment costs, allow the production of high quality sludges and promote 
their agronomic recovery with many benefits for soils ( microbiological presence, soil structure 
improvement, water retention, erosion control, carbon removal…). In addition, the use of sludge in 
agriculture (direct spreading or after pre-treatment by liming, composting or anaerobic digestion) 
ensures the recovery of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulphur and many other trace 
elements.  
Furthermore, for a coherent and ecological vision of waste  water treatment, thresholds and input 
criteria should be required before treatment plants take over , which would lead to the 
implementation of pre-treatment solutions and improve waste water and sludge quality. This would

enable some waste water to be released into the natural environment  without passing through 
treatment plant and reduce treatment cost. It would also  avoid the pollution of unpolluted waste 
water with some heavily polluted waste  water, which should first be pre -treated on site before 
being connected to the global or collective waste water treatment plant.  
European criteria for sludge quality: 
Then, Europe should set quality criteria and thresholds on sludge quality , as it is already done in 
France, to allow any direct or indirect (composting or liming) agronomic valorisation of sludge, as 
well as specific process standards and thresholds for composted sludge (NFU standards). It would 
help to promote  agronomic recovery of sludge , achieve the European zero pollution ambition , 
restore soils and biodiversity, and be part of a more circular economy.  
Indeed, sludge spreading on soils is a natural recycling activity in the respect of the waste hierarchy 
principle, whether it is done through direct agronomic valorisation or after composting the sludge  
in NFU standards. 
These thresholds should also be the criteria for implementing the polluter pays principle. 
 
Polluter pays principle: 
In the current context of high inflation and economic uncertainty,  citizens should not have to pay 
for the pollution contained in waste  water that is due to industrial discharges or industry-related 
pollutants (and in particular chemicals and microplastics, but textiles and pesticides could also be 
part of the list). The social dimension has to be taken into consideration so as not to leave the extra 
costs of treatment to the citizens. 
That’s why FNADE strongly supports the “polluter pays principle” as it implies a fairer approach to           
financial responsibility for additional costs, which is a necessity to invest in innovative solutions and            
to prevent pollution where it is generated. FNADE therefore considers the “polluter pays principle” 
as a good funding leverage. 
The broadening of the tax base is absolutely necessary to secure and fund  the current economic 
model: producers must pay for the treatment of chemicals and polluting substances released in the 
environment. 
No Producer Responsibility Organisation (PRO): 
Beyond the principle, the right way to implement it in the legislation is still a contentious topic. The 
Commission’s approach is, in accordance with what has been established for waste management, 
to set up a PRO. This approach is problematic as water management in the EU already has a 
functional, well-established system in place. In some countries, organisations that are already in 
place could act as intermediaries (i.e. collecting funds and earmarking them). Adding another layer 
of complexity would be costly without bringing added benefits. FNADE therefore suggests to delete

the requirement of setting up a PRO (and related provisions) so that Member States could decide 
on the best way to enforce the polluter pays principle.   
For instance, France already has regional committees, water agencies and local authorities dealing 
with water (discharge basin, quality, treatment, control…). These authorities are already involved in 
water management and would have a better overview of local collection and treatment facilities . 
Maintaining the current system would be more efficient than setting up a new organisation. Finally, 
it is crucial that member State authorities have the authority over these schemes.  
FNADE hopes necessary investments linked to the implementation of the revised regulation would 
partly be supported by energy and productivity savings, Extended Producer Responsibility, but also 
by fee increases. But FNADE would like European authorities to think right now about how to set up 
a funding system able to give European urban water cycle the visibility and the means to cope with 
climate change and the expectations of citizens.

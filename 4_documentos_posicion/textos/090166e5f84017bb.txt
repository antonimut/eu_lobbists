HIGHLIGHTS
On 26 October 2022, the European Commission adopted a proposal to revise the
rules on treating urban wastewater to better protect the health of Europeans and
the environment. We broadly welcome the proposal, but stress the need to create
an enabling framework that respects the key principle below for the Directive to
work effectively.
EXECUTIVESUMMARY
The proposal's obligation to apply additional treatment to urban wastewater in
order to eliminate a broad spectrum of micro-pollutants requires a fair distribution
of costs to strike a balance between sustainability and social equity. In this regard,
the obligation for polluting industries to contribute to the costs of quaternary
treatment through an extended producer responsibility (EPR) scheme is a
necessary condition, which absolutely needs to be safeguarded in the legislation,
to maintain water affordability and ensure a level playing field.
Ensuring a fair distribution of costs
✓Article 9: It is crucial to implement a strong
EPR scheme, which should also cover the
costs of producing green energy to
compensate for the energy used to apply
quaternary treatment, and to remove
exonerations in §2 or at least clarify that
the 2 tonnes threshold is meant at EU
level.
The Commission should also be
empowered to adopt delegated acts in
order to be able to broaden the list of
products covered by the EPR scheme in
the future based on the latest scientific
knowledge.

HIGHLIGHTS
✓Articles 6-8: There should be a stronger emphasis on the need to adopt a
preventive approach favouring actions to reduce pollution at source in order to
reduce energy consumption and chemical use associated with treatment while
increasing the potentialities of the circular economy.
✓Article 8: We advocate for context-based flexibility mechanisms and for the
possibility of trade-offs to prioritise interventions where they are most useful,
notably between treatment requirements and energy neutrality (e.g., in relation
to ozone treatment), based on a thorough risk assessment.
✓Annex 1: Phosphorus and nitrogen reduction values should be adapted to local
environmental contexts to avoid unnecessary investment costs and energy
consumption while ensuring environmental benefits in line with the Water
Framework Directive’s objective.
There is a need for flexibility mechanisms in the implementation to prioritise
interventions and investments where they are most needed, given the multiple
objectives of the Directive and the potential trade-offs between some of them,
notably between treatment requirements and energy neutrality. In this respect, we
advocate for a stronger link with the Water Framework Directive to ensure
interventions are adapted to local ecological and chemical contexts of recipient
water bodies.
Prioritising investments where they are most needed
Storm water overflows
The proposal includes new requirements to limit and better monitor storm water
overflows. Whilst acknowledging the importance to reduce that source of pollution,
we draw attention to the significant economic impact that addressing this problem
may involve in its present form.
✓Article 5/Annex 5: To avoid disproportionate costs and prioritise investments
where they are most needed, we propose replacing the 1% Europe-wide
indicative reduction target, which doesn’t take into consideration local contexts,
with environmental objectives based on the Water Framework Directive, which
consider the environmental impact of the solutions introduced.
✓Annex 5: The 2035/2040 deadline should be extended to favour nature-based
solutions, which are more cost-effective and less energy-intensive but take
more time to implement, instead of grey infrastructure (e.g., water retention
tanks).

HIGHLIGHTS
The proposed revision is expected to have a stronger economic impact on rural
areas, notably in relation to collection systems and energy neutrality, which needs
to be financially mitigated to prevent territorial cleavage and exacerbating an
already unequal burden. That is why we call for a better consideration of local
contexts and for EU structural and investment funds to help water operators
implement key requirements of the new Directive. We finally draw attention to the
need to set up a regulatory framework that facilitates integrated water resources
management at river basin level, also as a way to ensure efficiency and solidarity in
the allocation of resources between urban areas and their rural surroundings.
✓Article 3: The obligation to connect to collecting systems should be based on a
thorough risk-benefit analysis by national authorities and sufficient resources
should be allocated through solidarity mechanisms at different spatial scales,
including at river basin level, to local authorities and water operators.
✓Article 11: We call for financial transfers under EU structural and investment
funds and mechanisms to speed up planning permits to facilitate the green
transition towards energy neutrality.
Adopting a realistic approach to energy neutrality
We welcome with caution the proposed objective to reach energy neutrality by
2040 at national level for all wastewater treatment plants above 10 000 p.e. Whilst
the target is commendable, it is challenging to reach in all contexts due to spatial
constraints and even more so as the requirements on tertiary and quaternary
treatment will increase energy needs. 
Article 11: Green energy bought from the electrical grid and sold to other
industries should be taken into account in the energy neutrality calculations.
There is otherwise a crucial need for clear indication on how to balance
treatment and energy neutrality requirements. We also advocate for a change
to the current wording, which suggests that green energy must be generated at
the wastewater treatment plant sites. This will force renewable generation on
these sites even where off-site generation could be more (cost)-effective and
will create significant planning as well as land use challenges on existing sites.

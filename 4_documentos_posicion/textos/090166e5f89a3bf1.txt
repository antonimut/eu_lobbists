Abstract 
 
Almost one year after the World Health Organisation (WHO) presented their new air quality 
guideline values, the EU Commission published a proposal for the revision of the European 
air quality directives on 26 October 2022. It contains updated limit values for air pollutants 
that are considered to be decisive for air pollution. Specifically, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), 
particulate matter (as PM2,5 and PM10), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO) and sulphur 
dioxide (SO2) limits are updated. 
 
Even before the publication of the Commission's proposal, VDMA took a clear position for 
Europe as an industrial location. Protecting the health of EU citizens is an important concern 
for the industry. However, the impact of new air quality limit values on Europe as an industrial 
location must not be underestimated under any circumstances. The technical feasibility as 
well as social, economic and political impacts of new limit values must be carefully assessed. 
The VDMA therefore expressly welcomes the fact that the EU Commission has reconsidered 
the WHO's guideline values in its proposal and has not adopted them lightly. 
 
The mere adoption of the new WHO air quality guideline values as part of the revision of the 
European air quality directives could have led to massive negative impacts on the industrial 
location. But the limit values for air pollutants now proposed by the EU Commission also 
have the potential to undermine the imperative transformation of the industry. 
 
VDMA therefore urges that the following points be considered when setting new air quality 
limit values and overall when revising the European air quality directives.  
 
» Transformation of industry is massively hindered! 
 
Industry is currently undergoing an urgently needed transformation towards green 
technologies, future-proofing and greater sustainability overall.  
 
With this in mind, the massive impediment to this transformation caused by 
overambitious air quality limit values or those that have to be implemented in the 
short term should not be underestimated. Many of today's large industrial areas and 
regions (e.g. the Ruhr region) could become exceedance areas as a result of the new 
limit values, making it much more difficult to obtain permits for new plants and thus 
undermining the transformation process.

» Timeline more than ambitious! 
 
The regulations on air quality are extraordinarily complex and at the same time they 
have a major impact on the everyday lives of many EU citizens. In this respect, it is 
advisable to follow the principle "quality before speed" in the further consultations on 
the revision of the European air quality directives. All EU institutions involved in the 
legislative process (EU Commission, European Parliament and Council) should deal 
with the submitted proposal in detail and evaluate possible positive and negative 
consequences. The technical expertise of the mechanical and plant engineering 
sector is available for discussions and technical input, especially on the topic of 
"technical realisation/feasibility".

» Data basis incomplete, comparability of data not given! 
 
The main basis for the assessment of air quality are the air quality measuring 
stations. These are necessary, among other things, to generate an in-depth 
understanding of the topic of air quality. In 2021, measurement data for nitrogen 
dioxide (NO2) was available from a total of 598 measuring stations in Germany, but 
for PM2,5 there were only 228 stations.3  Only 15 (!) of these stations for PM2,5 were 
assigned to the industrial sector for Germany as a whole.3 A comprehensive 
knowledge and new limit values derived from it with a great influence on the industrial 
location of Germany or Europe must not be derived on the ground of such a data 
basis under any circumstances.

Furthermore, the regulations and requirements for the measuring stations specified  
in Annex IV result overall in a low comparability of the measurement data itself. If, for 
example, a measuring station measures an idealised immission value of about 
40 µg/m³ originating from any emission source (e.g. vehicle, background pollution = 
0 µg/m³) at a distance of 2 m from the emission source, a measuring station at the 
permissible maximum distance of 10 m from the same emission source would, as a 
first approximation, only measure an immission value of about 8 µg/m³ (idealised 
behaviour ~1/r; for point sources, an idealised behaviour ~1/r² applies, which again 
significantly increases the difference). 
 
For the
 proposed limit values for air pollutants, it must also first be determined 
whether the existing measurement technology can represent sufficiently high 
measurement accuracies, especially in the area close to traffic. According to the 
current state of discussion, this is not undoubtedly the case.

At the same time, care must be taken when revising the European air quality direc-
tives that current positive developments are not counteracted by the revision of air 
quality limit values. For example, the tightening of particulate matter limits could slow 
down the positive development of the share of battery electric vehicles (BEV) in 
traffic. BEVs are often heavier than conventionally powered vehicles and therefore 
have increased tyre wear as well as possibly higher dust turbulence, which could be 
measurable in the form of particulate matter at a measuring station. 
 
Furthermore, we point out that in the area of fine dust, among other things, there are 
also natural emission sources (e.g. forests, meadows, ...). This irreducible contri-
bution must not be neglected in the current legislative debate, but - as already 
mentioned for the anthropogenic emission sources - it can also only be estimated at 
all by taking numerous assumptions into account. 
 
Conclusion and demands 
 
The VDMA expressly supports the efforts of the EU Commission to further improve air quality 
in the EU. 
Updating the EU air quality directives makes sense but must be carried out with the appro-
priate sense of proportion. Within the framework of this position paper, we have outlined 
various aspects and issues that must definitely be taken into account when drafting the 
legislative text.  
First of all, a comparable and reliable database on air quality in the EU must be created. An 
EU-wide comparability of measurement data is a basic prerequisite for effective measures to 
improve air quality. Furthermore, sufficient time must be allowed for the development of such 
a complex law with far-reaching effects. Likewise, the time until 2030 is too short to imple-
ment effective and efficient air pollution control plans at the national level. Finally, the 
relevance of this new regulation for subsequent source emission legislations should not be 
underestimated at all. These would presumably have to be massively tightened to avoid 
possible immission limit value exceedances, but without clear quantification of their 
respective contribution to the immission situation itself. 
 
6 
 
If the regulations now presented were to come into force unchanged, cities, municipalities 
and regions would be required to take appropriate measures within the shortest possible time 
in order to be able to comply with the new limit values. These would be, for example, stricter 
entry bans for motor vehicles, the reduction of opportunities to set up industrial production, 
as well as more extensive regulations for already existing production facilities and, associa-
ted with this, the real risk of production relocations to non-EU countries. 
In detail, it might be immensely difficult or impossible to decide which of the potential sources 
is actually responsible for identified limit value exceedances. 
 
 
The revision of the EU air quality directives should therefore be carefully considered and  
» reflect the technical-economic realisation/feasibility,  
» not slow down the transformation of industry through highly ambitious limit 
values, 
» build on a broad and comparable database (e.g. standardise locations of 
measuring stations across the EU, take topographies into account), 
» include the technical limitations of the measurement equipment (e.g. 
measurement uncertainties/drift), 
» take into account the scope and limits of the source emission regulations, 
» identify natural emission sources and their contribution to the immission situation 
and take this into account when designing limit values, and  
» identify and ensure fair burden sharing between the different emission sources.

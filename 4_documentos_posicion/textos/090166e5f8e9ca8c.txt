Concawe feedback to the EC proposal “Integrated water management – 
revised lists of surface and groundwater pollutants” 
Concawe supports the process aiming at regularly reviewing the lists of priority substances and 
groundwater pollutants and appreciates the possibility to share its views on the proposal recently 
adopted by the EC in that context. 
From an industrial perspective, the importance of harmonised EU-level regulations is well recognised 
and the harmonisation foreseen in the Commi ssion proposal well acknowledged: all  parties benefit 
when the process is less complex to manage. Moreover, although knowledge is good in the areas of 
the various ‘Water Directives’, the use of a framework which avoids duplication between directives or 
reduces the potential for confusion in trying to interpret which directive applies, and when, is highly 
desirable. Therefore, Concawe particularly welcomes the general objective of increasing the 
effectiveness and reduce the administrative burden of the legislation. 
Concawe recognizes that sound principles of science can be used to derive an actionable approach 
which can protect human health and the environment. We believe we have the capability to 
contribute to the general objective of regularly review ing the lists of priority substances and 
groundwater pollutants. Concawe had therefore engaged with the JRC and the EC through the process 
that led to the adoption of the so-called “Water Package”.

Deficiencies in the derivation of EQS for PFAS substances. 
 
What we object to: 
Procedural and scientific deficiencies in the derivation of Surface water EQS for 24 PFAS substances 
identified by members of the JRC EG and communicated to the EG and SCHEER committee have not 
been adequately considered. The PFAS Surface water EQS was subsequently used to propose PFAS 
Groundwater QS, thus we consider also the Groundwater PFAS QS has the same procedural and

Our Specific Concerns: 
1. The assumption of an “equivalent mechanism of action” for 24 PFAS substances based on 
limited and conflicting evidence of correlation for a small subset of PFAS (PFOA, PFNA, PFOS, 
PFHxS).  
▪ This includes a failure to properly consider the relevance and adequacy for EQS-
derivation of the selected endpoint as well as previous evidence and assessment by 
Health Canada (2018) which concluded “Although some effects on the antibody response 
have been observed, conflicting results were common in the dataset, which r emains relatively 
small. A low level of consistency was observed across studies, with variations between 
genders, specific microbial immunoglobins, infections, mother vs. child exposure, and child 
years, amongst other characteristics. Moreover, the risk of residual confounding, bias, and 
chance cannot be discarded. These flaws impede concluding on a causative mechanism, and 
the nature of the association remains unclear.”  
2. The application of relative potency factors (RPFs) derived from a single study using a 
biologically unrelated endpoint (liver toxicity) for 24 PFAS using the selected endpoint 
(vaccine response). 
3. The direct application of tissue-based RPF (and concentration addition of PFOA-equivalents) 
to water-based values for exposure via drinking water without consideration of relative 
biological partitioning affinity of PFAS compounds.

Detailed Discussion of Scientific Critiques: 
 
1. Assumption of “equivalent mechanism of action” for immune response endpoint for 
24 PFAS based on limited and conflicting scientific evidence. 
 
A tolerable weekly intake (TWI) value of 4.4 ng/kg bw-wk was used as the basis for the derivation 
of a tissue-based quality standard (QS) for fish consumption in the EQS dossier. This value had 
been previously published by EFSA (2020) and is derived based on a lowest benchmark dose level 
(BMDL10) of 17.5 ng/mL in human serum for the sum of four PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, PFHxS) 
from an epidemiological study of immune response to diphtheria vaccination in children in 
Germany (Abraham et al., 2020).  
There are significant concerns with extrapolating the outcome of this study to the derivation of 
population level EQS for 24 PFAS: 
A. As noted by the CONTAM panel (EFSA, 2020), the effect considered, “decreased vaccination 
response” is regarded as a “risk factor for disease” rather than a disease itself.  
 
o The study did not indicate whether the reduced vaccine response resulted in an adverse 
outcome (i.e., increased incidence of diphtheria in the vaccinated population) which was 
robust and statistically relevant. This consideration is further discussed in the Health 
Canada Technical Guidance Document for Derivation of Human Health Drinking Water 
Quality Standards (2018). The authors conclude that, “In humans, evidence of 
immunosuppression is inconsistent. Associations are observed between PFOS levels and decreases 
in antibodies against some (but not all) illnesses, and the influence of PFOS exposure on clinical 
immunosuppression (i.e. incidence of illnesses) appears to be more tenuous.”  Further, the 
authors specifically discuss the Grandjean (2012) study of immune response to DTAP 
vaccination by stating, “Of note for discussion of clinical importance in humans is the Grandjean 
et al. (2012) study, which demonstrated that despite decreased vaccine -specific immunoglobulin 
response in PFOS-exposed children, the number of children with immunoglobulin levels below the 
clinically-protective level was low.” 
 
o While the authors of the EFSA 2020 scientific opinion identify the non-adverse, non-
clinical nature of the selected endpoint and its implication for the application of further 
uncertainty factors in developing the TWI, these discussions, and their implications 
particularly their relevance to EQS derivation were not adequately addressed by the 
expert group, nor by the SCHEER committee.

B. As noted by the CONTAM panel, “findings [immune system effects] were considered robust 
since in animals they were consistently observed for the two studied PFASs (PFOS, PFOA) 
and also in humans”. 
a. This statement is somewhat misleading, as results between individual studies have 
shown various natures of effect (e.g., inverse, no-effect, non-linear), observed 
effects levels (several order of magnitude variation between mice and humans) as 
well as inconsistency between immune response as a function of vaccine target as 
well as PFAS compound. This is highlighted by the authors of the 2018 Canadian 
Drinking water report for PFOS, where they comment, “Immune system effects were 
excluded from the quantitative risk assessment due to inconsistencies in NOAELs and LOAELs 
among studies and uncertainty of the importance of observed effects to human health, both 
of which will be expanded upon in this section. Inconsistencies were observed in the effective 
PFOS doses for immune function endpoints: the suppression of SRBC-specific IgM in B6C3F1 
mice in Peden-Adams et al. (2008) was observed at ≥0.00166 mg/kg bw per day, whereas the 
LOAEL in C57Bl/6 mice was 0.0833 mg/kg bw per day, with no significant changes observed 
at 0.0083 mg/kg bw per day (Dong et al., 2009) or 0.0167 mg/kg bw per day (Dong et al., 
2011). Moreover, NK cell activity was actually increased at 0.0166 mg/kg bw per day in 
B6C3F1 mice (Peden-Adams et al., 2008); in C57Bl/6 mice, the effect was non-monotonic, 
with increased activity at low doses (significant at 0.0833 mg/kg bw per day), and significant 
decreases at higher doses (0.833 and 2.083 mg/kg bw per day) (Dong et al., 2009). An 
additional study identified increased mortality from influenza A infection in mice at 0.025 
mg/kg bw per day (Guruge et al., 2009); however, this effect was not studied in other 
species.”. The authors go on to highlight the inconsistency between animal and 
human results, stating, “Further explorations should be performed to address the nearly 
two orders of magnitude difference in LOAELs in the studies before these endpoints can be 
reliably considered as a basis for a risk assessment.” 
b. Further, it should be noted that in the Abraham et al. (2020) study, EFSA concluded 
that associations for PFOA were significant an inversely correlated for diphtheria, 
tetanus, and Hib, however associations for PFOS, PFHxS, and PFNA were not 
significant. In the study itself, Abraham further concludes, “The study revealed no 
influence of PFOA and PFOS on infections during the first year of life and on levels of 
cholesterol.” Additionally, the authors conclude that “… since most studies in this field 
are cross-sectional, data need to be interpreted with caution. More insight is needed into 
possible mechanisms of action, dose–response relationships and the clinical relevance. 
Therefore, further intensive laboratory and epidemiological research (including prospective 
studies) is necessary to improve the understanding.” This conclusion further supports the 
Health Canada position that observed serum level response does not necessarily 
support apical or clinical outcomes for immune response.  
c. Finally, there is no evidence of adversity provided for many of the PFAS within the 
dossier, highlighting a significant gap and uncertainty in the broad application of the 
immune response endpoint as the critical effect threshold for EQS derivation. This 
lack of evidence and the inconsistent and conflicting evidence for the PFAS 
considered in the EFSA 2020 opinion does not support the selection of the critical 
effect and the associated TWI for use in EQS derivation for the broad category of 
PFAS under consideration.

2. The application of relative potency factors (RPFs) derived from a single study using a 
biologically unrelated endpoint (liver toxicity) for 24 PFAS using the selected endpoint 
(vaccine response). 
 
A recent publication proposed a set of relative potency factors for 14 per‐ and polyfluoroalkyl 
acids (PFAA) and 2 PFAA precursors based on liver toxicity after oral exposure in the male rat as 
the common effect (Bil et al., 2021). Additionally, the JRC EG estimated RPFs for 7 additional 
PFAS by read‐across to provide RPFs for the 23 PFAS in the EQS dossier relative to PFOA as a 
reference compound. 
In the publication, Bil et al (2021) discuss the necessary information to derive relative potency 
factors. Specifically, “The RPF approach requires that a) chemicals contribute to a common effect, b) 
their dose–response curves are (approximately) parallel on log –dose scale, and c) chemicals do not 
interact.” The authors consider these conditions critical for the reliable and appropriate 
derivation of RPFs.  
As discussed previously, consistent observation of similar immune effects has NOT been 
established for the 24 PFAS in the EQS dossier. Further, robust, quantitative dose-response 
relationships have not been established for the 24 PFAS compounds. As such, the application of 
the Bil-derived RPF framework for the remaining PFAS substances not explicitly considered for 
immune effects by EFSA in the 2020 TWI derivation (e.g., PFOA, PFNA, PFOS, PFHxS) introduces 
a significant uncertainty and is not robustly supported, scientifically. 
While this point was raised by members of the EG during development of the draft EQS dossier, 
we didn’t see any evidence that these scientific criticisms were taken into account by the EG or 
the SCHEER committee.  
 
3. The direct application of tissue-based RPF (and concentration addition of PFOA-
equivalents) to water-based values for exposure via drinking water without 
consideration of relative biological partitioning affinity of PFAS compounds. 
 
Mammalian effects thresholds for PFAS compounds and most chemicals more generally, are 
derived on a tissue basis (e.g., mg/kg body weight or mg/kg-day) using oral or gavage dosing of 
the test animal. Typically, concentrations in target tissue (e.g., in the liver for a liver effect 
study) are analytically confirmed and reported (e.g., measured vs. nominal doses are derived for 
threshold effects such as a BMDL or NOAEL/LOAEL). In contrast, aquatic studies often rely on 
measurements in the water column (e.g., mg/L) rather than directly in the organism tissue. For 
the baseline narcosis mode of action the relationship between the water concentration and the 
tissue concentration is proportional to the relative affinity of the chemical for partitioning into 
organism lipid from the overlying water (e.g., the lipid-water partition coefficient, often 
represented by a surrogate organic phase, like octanol) (Di Toro 2000a,b). More recently, the 
principle of a generalized target-site critical body burden has been demonstrated for a large

number of mechanisms of action in aquatic and benthic organisms (Boone & Di Toro 2019a,b). 
For chemicals which affect an organism via a similar mechanism of action an identical water-
based concentration can result in a different tissue-based effects level and by extension a 
different relative potency.  
In the case of PFAS, whole body bioconcentration factors for aquatic as well terrestrial 
organisms/ plants are summarized by Condor et al., 2019. Figure 3 below illustrates the range 
of biological partitioning observed for a series of PFCA and PFSA molecules in fish: 
 
Figure 3. Recommended aquatic fish BCF values from Condor et al., 2019.  
The relative affinity for tissue varies by nearly 6 orders of magnitude over the series of C3 – C13 
PFCAs. Similar variability is observed for the sulfonic acids (~ 3.5 orders of magnitude). As a 
result, the application of the tissue-based RPFs without adjustment for their respective 
organism tissue-affinity may result in considerable uncertainty in the actual quantitative risk 
associated with exposure to the PFAS mixture in water. There are likely to be cases of under-
estimation (in the case where measured PFAS are predominantly > C8 chain length) as well as 
over estimation (in the case of predominantly low MW PFAS). 
Without a reliable, robust understanding of the relative biological availability and ADME 
properties of the individual PFAS substances, the efficacy in managing risk of the resulting 
water-based QS (relative to that of the tissue-based criteria) is likely to remain highly uncertain.

Background 
 
As a leading health company providing health with humanity we support the ambition behind the 
European Green Deal to be the first climate-neutral continent with the targets to have no net 
emissions of greenhouse gases by 2050, decouple economic growth from resource use and leave 
no person and no place behind.  
 
Our global position paper on Sustainable Packaging outlines the activities we are involved in to 
ensure the packaging solutions we use meet all safety, quality, and regulatory requirements to 
ensure product effectiveness for the full shelf life and at the same time create an inclusive user 
experience for our consumers.  
 
Across our packaging, we’re working to minimise waste and associated pollution by driving a 
circular model – so that the materials we use can stay in-use – while reducing our dependency on 
non-renewable sources. 
 
Introductory comments 
 
We welcome the European Commission’s proposal for a Regulation on Packaging and Packaging 
Waste, amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 and Directive (EU) 2019/904, and repealing 
Directive 94/62/EC.  
 
We particularly support the free movement within EU of all types of packaging materials and 
harmonised criteria for sorting packaging waste. In our opinion, truly harmonised legislation will 
create transparency and a level playing field for all stakeholders in the packaging value chain. 
Together with the establishment of a market for recycled materials, this will advance the circular 
packaging economy in EU.  
 
In the following are our detailed comments to individual provisions of the proposal.

Article 4 Free movement 
 
Under article 4 Member States may maintain and introduce national sustainability and 
information requirements that go beyond the requirements of the regulation. In a rather 
ambiguous way, the article states that Member States may not restrict or prohibit products 
which comply with the regulation, but not national requirements. The wording seems 
contradictory and will be a source of confusion and misinterpretation, which could restrict the 
free movement of goods.  
 
We strongly recommend the article is amended to ensure it is evident that national 
requirements, which go beyond the provisions of the proposed regulation, cannot be 
maintained or introduced. 
 
Member States are allowed to introduce labelling requirements aiming at identifying packaging 
subject to Extended Producer Responsibility-schemes or a Deposit Return Scheme other than 
those referred to in article 44, point 1. Since most packaging will be subject to extended 
producer responsibility, the article means specific national requirements may be imposed for 
most packaging materials placed in a Member State. We believe this will restrict the free 
movement of goods. In addition, such labelling has no relevance for consumers but could lead 
to confusion and result in lower recycling rates. For example, the “Der Grune Punkt” trademark 
symbol which was introduced following the 1992 EU Packaging Waste Directive to indicate 
payment into an EPR scheme causes confusion, as some people wrongly assume that it is a 
recycling symbol placed on packaging to indicate that the particular piece of packaging can be 
recycled1.  
 
Article 6 Recyclable packaging 
 
The regulation requires that packaging shall be recyclable by 2030, and medicines packaging by 
2035. Packaging is considered recyclable, if it meets specific performance grades for a design-for-
recycling (DfR). The recycling rate of the packaging must be above 70% (level A-D), packaging with 
performance grade E, where less than 70 percent of the packaging is recyclable, will be banned from 
2030.  
 
We support the overall ambitions of the proposal but have serious concerns about the timelines 
established in the proposal. In our opinion DfR guidelines must be based on science and 
established in close collaboration with relevant stakeholders. Regular reviews of the DfR 
guidelines must happen to ensure they consider scientific and societal developments. We believe 
connecting recyclability grades to proportionate EPR fees will be one of the main drivers for 
packaging recyclability. According to the proposal, the performance grades will be established by 
secondary legislation. This legislation must be in force by 2025 in order to meet the 2030/2035 
targets because takes 3-5 years to fully roll out a commercially available packaging solution.  
 
We strongly encourage the Commission to reconsider the timelines or alternatively specify them 
together with industry once the secondary legislation has been established, with a minimum of 5 
years to comply and 10 years for medicine packaging.

Where there is no technically recyclable solution commercially available at scale that ensures the 
quality, safety and efficacy of the product we encourage the EU help fund innovation to speed up the 
development of new solutions. For example, cold and flu medicine needs a high barrier. Today these 
are packaged in not-recyclable materials such as polyethylene (PE) with an aluminum barrier layer or 
PVC/PVdC. Alternative recyclable materials with an equivalent barrier are not commercially available 
today and it is expected to take 10-15 years to be in market for consumer healthcare products.  
 
A lot of Healthcare products are supplied in small size packaging such as tablet blisters and single dose 
sachets due to regulatory requirements. Even if these were made from recyclable materials this size 
packaging is not always collected, sorted and recycled across Member States. This translates into this 
type of packaging not being detected in sorting facilities and being counted as “non -recyclable”, 
despite being fully designed-for-recycling. Economic operators supplying products to the market 
can ensure they are recyclable but hav e no control over the collection, sorting and effective 
recycling of small healthcare packaging. This must be matched by innovation in the area of 
recyclability at scale so that for example small size packaging can be collected, sorted and 
recycled. 
 
 
Article 7 Minimum recycled content in plastic packaging 
 
Article 7 requires that a plastic packaging component contains a certain proportion of recycled 
plastic by 2030 and 2040. The requirement applies “per unit” of packaging.  
 
We support the proposal’s mandatory targets for recycled plastic in packaging, including contact 
sensitive packaging (with the exclusion of medicines and medical devices) but are concerned 
about the availability of recycled plastic materials at the specific time points specified in the 
proposal. Currently, the supply of recycled plastic suitable for contact sensitive packaging types 
of plastic other than PET is very limited in the EU. The situation is partly caused by sector 
specific legislation in areas like food and cosmetics ensuring the safety of these products.  
 
We encourage the Commission to ensure there is alignment between the sector specific 
legislative requirements when it comes to the use of recycled plastic material and the targets as 
well as timelines outlined in the proposal, especially collection and sorting targets. This could be 
done by amending the article so that the target for non-PET contact sensitive materials only 
enters into force, if recycling processes have been authorized by the relevant authorities by a 
certain date. We also strongly recommend the Commission to allow non-mechanical (Advanced 
/ chemical) recycled material for the achievement of this target. 
 
Article 7.1d also references a higher target of 35% post-consumer plastic waste in packaging 
other than those listed in points a, b and c. HDPE is the second most commonly recycled 
material but has much more limited availability that PET in grades approved for food contact 
(Healthcare packaging such as toothpaste, uses food grade plastics). PP has even more limited 
capacity in food contact grades. Therefore, it would be more appropriate for the target in Article 
7.1d(35%) to be aligned to Article 7.1b (10%). 
 
We are concerned about the proposal specifying the recycled content target:  
1. explicitly referencing post-consumer recycled (PCR) content – this implies exclusion of post-
industrial recycled content which is an important and valuable source of clean recycled 
content given the shortage of supply of PCR.

Article 9 Packaging minimisation 
 
We support the Commission’s efforts to minimise packaging. The principles of the proposed 
methodology for packaging minimization are understandable but we are not sure if the criteria in 
Annex IV are adequate. The criteria do not, for example, include “consumer acceptance” and the 
wording used to explain “Information requirements” seems open to interpretation. For example, 
it is unclear if non-mandatory label information can justify the size of packaging materials or 
multi-lingual labelling can justify a certain packaging size. 
 
The obligation for producers to provide proof of conformity for each individual package is 
excessive. The effort to prove and document, with legal certainty, that packaging cannot be 
smaller or lighter is disproportionate to what is intended to be achieved.  
 
We are not supporting standard documentation to demonstrate packaging minimization. We 
believe competent authorities should be empowered to request justification from companies on a 
packaging solution, based on a risk-based control approach.  
 
 
Articles 11 & 12 Labelling of packaging and waste receptacles for the 
collection of packaging waste 
 
According to the proposal the material fraction of packaging must be labelled. Waste containers 
must be labelled in a similar way to help consumers to sort packaging waste correctly. In our 
opinion it is essential for the free movement of goods in EU that the labelling of the material 
fraction on the packaging only consist of a harmonized pictogram and no text. Allowing national 
pictograms or texts would be a restriction on the free movement of goods and would most likely 
increase the size of the packaging if the product is in market in several Member States. To 
ensure that consumers quickly become familiar with the harmonized pictograms, national text 
could be displayed on the waste containers in connection with the pictograms. 
 
We believe all labelling requirements under the proposal must be relevant and helpful to 
consumers allowing them to sort and recycle packaging waste correctly. The proposal should 
not introduce labelling requirements for other purposes like identification of national EPR 
schemes. 
 
We strongly recommend the Commission to take the opportunity to introduce digital labelling as 
way to reduce the amount of materials used and to convey labelling information concerned with 
packaging, its sorting and reuse or recycling.

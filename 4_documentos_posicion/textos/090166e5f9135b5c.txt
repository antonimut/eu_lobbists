The UWWTD was adopted in 1991 and its evaluation in 2019 confirmed that the implementation of the 
directive has led to significant reduction of pollutants in effluents from cities, leading to improvements in 
the quality of EU’s water basins. Since then, new contaminants of concern have emerged, notably micro -
pollutants, mainly due to steep demographic increase in urban areas, scientific progress achieved by the 
pharmaceutical and overall chemical industries which leads to longer and better quality of lives in the 
Community, as well as more sensitive analytical detection techniques. To remove a major part of these 
contaminants in urban wastewater, the revised UWWTD proposal suggests applying EPR exclusively to 
two industrial sectors, quoted to be the major source of “hazardous micro-pollutants”, namely manufactur-
ers of human medicines and personal care (cosmetic) products.   
 
As the European voice for non -prescription medicines manufacturers, AESGP is supporting the objective 
of UWWTD – to protect the environment and human health from the adverse effects of urban wastewater 
discharges – and is prepared to correspondingly participate in attaining this objective. AESGP believes 
that, to achieve this goal, every stakeholder concerned must play their fair part, starting from the people 
generating discharges to those responsible for treating urban wastewater – a view reflected in the specific 
points detailed below. When it comes to its responsibility as a manufacturer of medicines that are amongst 
sources of micro-pollutants, AESGP calls for a proportionate and fair implementation of EPR. AESGP 
does not believe that the proposal of the European Commission is balanced and cost-efficient.   
 
The proposal should consider that the pharmaceutical industry provides medicines in the overall 
public interest, and should therefore: 
 
• Apply EPR to manufacturers of micro -pollutants based on the risk they pose to the environ-
ment and/or human health (1),  
 
• Recognise all sources of hazardous micro-pollutants irrespective of sector, (2;3),  
 
• Avoid administrative burden where it is not justified and ensure that funds collected through 
EPR schemes are used in the most cost-effective manner (4;5) 
 
• Acknowledge that environmental policies promoting the development and use of products 
with negligible potential environmental impacts must be considered alongside the societal 
and health benefits of pharmaceuticals (6) 
 
• And, ultimately, not jeopardize access to any category of medicines for people that need 
them, particularly those in smaller markets (7).  
 
Introduction 
 
This paper concerns the position of the European Self -Care Industry (AESGP) on the pro-
posed revision of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive (UWWTD) 2022/0345 (COD). 
Its main purpose is to provide co-legislators with AESGP’s views on the pragmatic, propor-
tionate and science -based application and implementation of an Extended Producer Re-
sponsibility (EPR) within the framework of UWWTD.

While AESGP welcomes the overall objective of the revision – to improve surface and ground water quali-
ty in the community, it has a number of concerns regarding the draft proposal and some suggestions for 
improvement:  
 
1. The Directive for the first time in EU law defines a micro-pollutant as  
 
a substance including its breakdown products, that is usually present in the environment and urban 
wastewaters in concentrations below milligrams per litre and which can be considered hazardous to 
human health or the environment based on any of the criteria set out in Annex one part 3 (health 
hazards) and 4 (environmental hazards) of the Regulation EC 1272/2008 of the European Parlia-
ment and of the Council on classification, labelling and packaging of substances and mixtures. 
 
AESGP believes that the proposed definition of micro -pollutant, with the consecutive hazard identification 
scheme based on the mere presence of a substance in effluents, while not considering its concentration, 
and the negative impact on living organisms it may entail, is disproportionate. This definition fails to as-
sess the risk and does NOT provide a good reference for identification of actual micro-pollutants having an 
impact on the environment or human health via the aqueous route.  
 
AESGP acknowledges the political choice made by the European Commission for an EPR system. For 
such a system to be feasible, it needs to operate by using manageable data bases and rules. However, to 
fulfil the requirements for an impact -based system, any hazard-based approach should be augmented by 
easily implemented risk-based rules, which will account for degree of hazard and exposure potential (1). 
 
Therefore, AESGP proposes that the definition and identification of micro-pollutants covered by an 
EPR system should be based on well -established risk-based principles, amended by certain ha-
zard properties , as adopted by regulatory authorities in Europe under several regulations, such as for 
human and veterinary medicinal products (2), agrochemicals (3), biocides (4), and general chemicals (5). 
The system should also ensure that compounds having no impact, or the impact of which being reduced 
during its lifespan, can be delisted from any EPR system (see section 3).

2. The Impact Assessment and proposal for the revision fails to provide so-
lid evidence that pharmaceuticals are among the leading contributors of 
micro-pollutants. Therefore, AESGP feels that the pharmaceutical sector 
would be unfairly burdened with the total cost of the system, while some 
other Industries, which are also contributors to the micro -pollutant load, 
are not identified for financial obligations under EPR.  
 
The Impact Assessment fails to recognize several evidence -based studies led by independent organisa-
tions, academia, regulators, and the pharmaceutical industry, suggesting that only 10% of the active phar-
maceutical ingredients (APIs) could pose a potential risk to the environment (6) and that most medicines 
(> 80%) indicated a low environmental risk (7).  
 
The Impact Assessment is exclusively focused on two sources of micro -pollutants (medicines and perso-
nal care products) which were narrowed down from a relatively small list of chemicals that only represent 
a small portion (around 1,200 chemicals) of the chemical sphere. The narrow selection of chemicals is 
mainly driven by those that are the focus of academic and regulatory monitoring. Recent work by Meent et 
al. (2020) (8) took an exposure modelling-based approach, assessing the exposure to around 10,000 che-
micals. Although still only a small percentage of the ca. 150,000 chemicals on the EU market, this paper 
demonstrates potential approaches which could be taken to assess the micro-pollutant contribution of che-
micals that have yet to emerge as a concern because they have not been the focus of monitoring cam-
paigns.   
 
The Impact Assessment fails to address other sources of micro -pollutants such as metals, metal oxides, 
non-metals (9), forms of carbon, and other industrial chemicals (as registered under REACH) (10) that can 
be found in many widely used consumer goods. Manufacturers of products containing hazardous micro -
pollutants that may pose a risk to the environment and/or human health would benefit from the implemen-
tation of a quaternary wastewater treatment directly, without being subject to the polluter pays principle 
proposed EPR scheme, as it would remove other micro -pollutants not originating from use of medicines 
and personal care products. AESGP, therefore, calls for a non -discriminatory extension of EPR to any in-
dustry sector that places on the market products that contain micro -pollutants that pose a risk to the envi-
ronment and/or human health, and that would require quaternary treatment for their removal.

3. The current plan disincentivizes industries not covered by the EPR sys-
tem from developing new technologies to treat and/or replace micro -
pollutants, since only the industries producing treatable micro-pollutants 
are targeted for this EPR initiative; untreatable micro-pollutant emissions 
are free to continue unabated as long as no treatment technology is avai-
lable.  
 
The draft specifically names metals and microplastics as micro -pollutants but excludes these substances 
only on a pragmatic basis (i.e., because treatment technology is not available). This leads to arbitrary 
inequity among industries emitting the substances identified as micro -pollutants of concern. By their na-
ture as micro-pollutants, none (or very few) pose a risk on their own, but the combination could ostensibly 
lead to ecosystem stress under certain circumstances. In such cases, should they occur, all micro -
pollutants together would be contributing to that stress, and any amelioration achieved by removing orga-
nic micro-pollutants would make the stress contributed by the untreatable micro-pollutants more tolerable.  
 
The simple lack of treatment technology does not absolve other industries of responsibility for contributing 
to ecosystem stress and, in fact, is more concerning because there is no mitigation possible. At the least, 
industries contributing to untreatable micro -pollutant emissions should have financial responsibility follo-
wing the same logic as for industries responsible for organic micro -pollutants, with funds used either for 
tertiary treatment that mitigates the multiple -stressor scenario or for research to develop appropriate 
treatment technologies.  It would be in line with a general principle of Community Law that requires similar 
situations not to be treated differently unless such differentiation is objectively justified (11). The lack of 
inclusion of other industry sectors into the EPR system will constitute unequal treatment of the pharma-
ceutical and cosmetics industries, since the EPR proposal does not provide an objective reason which 
would justify it. 
 
4. The only country in Europe that has already started systemically remo-
ving micro-pollutants from urban wastewater is Switzerland.  
 
It is calculated that, to upgrade the most impactful water treatment facilities to quaternary water treatment 
level in Switzerland (130 out of 715), it will cost 1.2 billion CHF francs in total over a period of 24 years 
(2016-2040). Additional recurring costs (e.g., additional energy consumption, the selected process mainte-
nance, such as filter consumables, waste management etc.) will depend strongly on the size of UWWTP, 
technology of choice, etc., but this cost is always significantly lower than the implementation costs of a 
quaternary treatment system. Since 2016, 14 WWTPs have implemented the necessary measures in 
Switzerland. Of these, 12 have built an elimination stage for micro -pollutants; while the other two WWTPs 
were closed and connected to another WWTP – for this, a sewer line was built instead of the measure re-
quired at the WWTP.  
 
The Impact Assessment estimates that costs of EPR at EU level will be around 1.18 billion EUR annually 
from 2040 onwards. By that time, UWWTP should have achieved energy neutrality, increased re -use of 
treated water and sludge and, as a result, reduced operating costs and generated additional profits. Based 
on the Swiss experience and logic, AESGP argues that EPR should, therefore, be used only for the 
upgrade of the urban wastewater treatment from tertiary to quaternary level. Then, the quaternary 
water treatment running costs should be borne by operators of urban wastewater treatment plants

5. It is suggested that producers of products containing hazardous micro -
pollutants exercise EPR via Producer Responsibility Organisations. The 
proposed stakeholder-based model governance will undoubtedly create 
high administrative costs associated with establishing, managing, and 
supervising such organisations.  
 
Experience gained by the pharmaceutical industry from the implementation of the Falsified Medicines Di-
rective (12) and the establishment of European and National Medicines Verification Organisation (13)  
suggests that this may not be the most cost -effective or efficient governance model. The governance 
costs of European Medicines Verification System (EMVS) have, by far, exceeded projected costs in the 
initial impact assessment by the European Commission.  
 
EPR has already been applied in the case of medicines in the form of take-back schemes for expired and/
or unused medicines in EU Member States to avoid inappropriate household disposal through solid waste 
or water effluents. Some of these take -back schemes are established and financed by the different stake-
holders of the medicines value chain – manufacturers, wholesalers, and pharmacies – under their social 
and environmental responsibility programs, by setting up Producer Responsibility Organizations (PROs) 
(14).  
 
To reduce administrative costs of EPR, AESGP suggests using existing PROs for medicines and/
or exploiting other existing Industrial levy collecting schemes at Member State level that would al-
low exercising EPR efficiently. Furthermore, it is important that industry not only pays the bill is-
sued by wastewater treatment facilities but has an active role in deciding on the practicalities of 
quaternary water treatment such as choice of technology and other modalities.

Initial statement 
The European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA) and the Association 
of the European Self -Care Industry (AESGP) welcome the opportunity to submit feedback to the 
Commission Proposal for a Directive on Urban Waste Water Treatmen t COM(2022)541 (UWWTD) 1 
and the accompanying Annex, impact assessment.  Due to the direct implications on the 
pharmaceutical industry with the inclusion of the proposed Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), 
we take the opportunity to also comment in detail on the related Feasibility Study which formed the 
basis for the inclusion of our sector. 
The pharmaceutical industry is aware that substances contained in medicinal products have been 
detected in trace amounts in European Surface Waters. We are supportive of the Urban Wastewater 
Treatment Directive Proposal’s objective to protect the environment and human health from the 
adverse effects of urban wastewater discharges and are committed to play our part and work with all 
stakeholders in achieving this objective in a fair and equitable way. 
We believe that, to achieve this obj ective, every stakeholder concerned must play its fair part, 
including the people generating discharges to urban wastewater treatment facilities. The 
responsibility lies not only with the pharmaceutical manufacturing industry, which is already driving 
initiatives to evaluate and reduce the environmental impact using well established risk -based 
methodologies  
 
Overview 
Human medicines enter the environment predominantly through individual patient use (via excretion 
or wash off), while smaller proportions are due to improper disposal  associated with expired or 
unused product, and even less through manufacturing2. Due to the mechanism of action of the active 
substances in medicines, which have been intentionally designed to interact with biological systems, 
some have been identified as presenting a potential environmental concern. 
The pharmaceutical industry has put in place an extensive Eco -Pharmaco-Stewardship2 programme 
which over the past 15 years has made great progress in minimising the effects of pharmaceuticals in 
the environment. Components in the programme include scientific research projects under the 
Innovative Medicines Initiative (IMI), guidelines for the management of manufacturing effluents 3,

We have serious concerns that the EPR scheme for micro-pollutants proposed in the revised UWWTD

Any regulatory measures taken to reduce environmental emissions of micro -pollutants under the

contributing to micropollut ants in the environment .5 Focus should be substance specific and not 
sector specific. It should also consider the healthcare and social benefits from the use of products that 
are currently targeted as containing substances of emergent concern for the environment . By doing 
so, all stakeholders responsible for micro-pollution will be part of the solution to ensure higher water 
quality and address the overall micro-pollutant footprint in a holistic manner. This is reasonable and 
proportionate, given that advanced waste water technolog y employed to remove pharmaceuticals 
from wastewater will also remove many other types of chemicals.

Summary of comments on the Commission Proposal and the Feasibility Study 
The three pharmaceutical associations would like to comment on the Feasibility Study of an Extended 
Producer Responsibility (EPR) program - the study conducted on behalf of the European Commission 
for their impact assessment leading to the inclusion in the Commission proposal. 
 
Absence of consideration of patient access: 
• The Feasibility Study lacks an impact assessment of the consequences for patient access to 
medicines. Any measures to reduce the environmental impact of pharmaceuticals must 
consider and differentiate pharmaceuticals as essential and critical public goods. Patient 
access to medicines which have been deemed safe and effective by the regulators should 
not be hindered in any way.   
• Under the polluter -pays principle, pollution control is assigned to the polluters, who can 
absorb costs without excessive setback to their own or other actors’ interests. It is unclear 
that, having medicines manufacturers pay,  meets this condition because these provisions 
will induce external costs with indirect burden on health systems and patients.  
• Having medicines manufacturers pay for WWTP upgrades will incentivize the cost recoup 
through higher drug prices, aggravating th e national pharmaceutical budget. It could also 
result in manufacturers leaving markets  where such requests are made, or in which a 
product would no longer be economically viable (Nijsingh et al., 201910).  
• Patients who have to pay out of pocket for certain classes of medicine or who are financially 
insecure would have reduced accessibility to needed medicine if prices were increased. 
Some examples of more vulnerable categories of patients potentially affected would include, 
for instance, chronic pain patients reliant on pain medication, mental health patients reliant 
on anti -depressants, female patients reliant on hormonal treatments, and children and 
hospital patients receiving anti-infective treatments. Given the clinical importance of these 
medicines, diminished access to them will have a real health impact. 
• We are concerned that if not applied proportionally and fairly, the proposed EPR system will 
translate into a de facto tax which will impact those patients using the medic ines affected 
by the measure, contradicting the European Pharmaceutical Strategy goal of reducing access 
inequalities. It would also have a negative impact on the EU’s goal to enhance the resilience 
of pharmaceutical supply, a key asset for Europe’s compet itiveness, health and 
preparedness for future health crises and likely affect availability on smaller markets first.   
 
Inconsistency with other EU rules and strategies:  
• The proposal could be inconsistent with the Pharmaceuticals Strategy in terms of ensu ring 
continued patient access to efficacious medicines. We also express further concerns over 
the pharmaceutical legislative review taking place in parallel.

sludge. Quaternary treatment costs should be linked to substances that actually require 
quaternary treatment to be removed and not those adequately removed from wastewater at 
properly functioning tertiary treatment plants. 
• To a large extent pharmaceuticals are already efficiently managed by UWWTP tec hnologies. 
Pharmaceutical residues are only a fraction of the micropollutants that an improved 
wastewater treatment would control. Other toxic, persistent or hazardous substances coming 
from other manufacturers, which are harder to identify, would benefit without any cost from 
any additional investment into UWWTP infrastructure and technology. 
• EPR, if applied to a tiny subset of industry, as currently proposed by the UWWTD, would be 
inadequate and disproportionate. Two industry sectors would be responsible for removing all 
micro-pollutants, including the large number from non-pharmaceutical and non-personal care 
products11. Thus, the proposed EPR system fails to properly apply the Polluter Pays Principle 
to arrive at fair funding options for quaternary treatment. It is clearly stated in the Feasibility 
Study that substances of various other sectors can be treated with quater nary treatment, 
therefore the polluters for these substances will be also be benefitting from tertiary and 
quaternary treatment.

• The proposal lacks many details and definitions that will lead to overuse of quaternary 
treatment. For example, it is not clear how surface waters “at risk" will be determined and 
whether risk is limited to micropollutants from the pharmaceuticals and pers onal care 
products or to other substances as well.

Summary of proposals and solutions 
• There is a need for consistency with the Pharmaceutical Strategy in terms of ensuring 
continued patient accessibility to efficacious medicines. 
• Any EPR scheme should be applied to micropollutants in urban waste water using a risk-based 
approach versus a hazard-based approach, regardless of the volume of the load being treated 
(i.e. focus should be on micropollutants that pose a risk to the environment and human health, 
based on well-established environmental risk assessment methodologies, like the PEC/PNEC 
ratio.) 
• Any mechanism to implement the polluter pays principle should include all manufacturers of 
potential micropollutants to provide an equitable and cost -effective approach, given that 
many micropollutants that do not originate from the use of medicines will be removed by 
additional quaternary treatment. 
• The Dire ctive should prioritize treatment at the source (concentrated sources), where 
possible, to remove micropollutants before they enter urban waste water treatment systems, 
as a primary means to protect water ways from pollution. Note: As written in the UWWT 
Directive Proposal, “most stakeholders” consulted during the OPC in 2021 agreed that this 
was an important consideration for the Commission. 
• The Swiss model for reducing micro pollution should be considered as an equitable and cost -
effective solution and represents a good starting position for managing pollution.

Furthermore, the proposal significantly overestimates  the amounts of pharmaceuticals that could

Concluding remark on the analysis of the Feasibility study 
Thus, on many aspects, the EPR Feasibility Study lacks clarity and appears not to be sufficiently 
transparent, causing a bias of opinio n. Subsequently, we challenge the conclusions of the report as 
the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industries should not be the only sectors considered for EPR based 
on the data presented. 
The quaternary treatment proposed in the revised UWWTD is designed to remove all organic carbon

for harm to humans and environment. Based on the above discussion, there is potentially a much

effluents.  Therefore, the application of EPR to only two sectors is inequitable and unjustified.

3. The proposed treatment standards for COD, TOC and TSS will cause operational 
problems for ozonation or granular activated carbon used for quaternary treatment.  
The Proposal for a Directive in Table 1 of Annex 1 ( Part B – Discharge from urban wastewater 
treatment plants to receiving waters) characterizes the level of treatment as tertiary treatment when 
combined with the limits on phosphorus and nitrogen in Table 2. “Tertiary treatment,” as the term 
widely understood in the wastewater treatment  industry, includes achieving low concentrations of 
carbonaceous compounds and total suspended solids in addition to nutrient control. The Organisation 
for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) defines tertiary treatment as follows:  
“Tertiary treatment is the advanced treatment process, following secondary treatment of 
waste water, that produces high -quality water. Tertiary treatment includes removal of 
nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen and practically all suspended and organic matter 
from waste water.” 
For example, in the United States wastewater treatment plants are required to achieve maximum 30-
day averages of total phosphorus and total nitrogen of 0.5 mg/L and 6 mg/L, respectively, would also 
have 30-day maximum limits of 10 mg/L for carbonaceous oxygen demand (CBOD5) and 15 mg/L TSS. 
This level of treatment will also result in effluent concentrations of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) of 
10-20 mg/L and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) of 30-60 mg/L. Note that these limits are applied as 
maximum 30-day averages – annual averages as required by Annex 1 and Annex 2 are approximately 
67% of the maximum 30-day averages. This degree of BOD/COD/TSS control is considered mandatory 
when adding a quaternary treatment step of activated carbon and/or ozonation. 
Therefore, the limits for Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) and Total Suspended Solids (TSS) detailed in 
Table 1 of Annex I of the Directive Proposal for the requirements for discharges from urban 
wastewater treatment plants subject to Article 6 of the Di rective Proposal will cause operational 
problems for quaternary treatment controls. The proposed TSS limit of 35 mg/L will quickly cause 
fouling in granular activated columns and will also cause operation issues for ozonation systems. The 
125 mg/L limit fo r COD will cause granular activated carbon systems to have shortened periods of 
adequate performance and will require excessive amounts of carbon regeneration to keep adsorption

capacity. For ozonation, the 125 mg/L COD will require higher amounts of ozone needed for treatment 
and longer contact time. 
For ozonation and granular activated carbon to be effectively used as quaternary treatment, urban 
wastewater treatment plants will need to install additional filtration steps (i.e., sand filtration or 
membrane filtration) to control solids before using these systems. They will have to achieve much 
higher removal of CBOD/COD/TOC than specified in Annex 1. The additional cost of these systems 
could be an additional cost of 30-40 % of the cost of the quaternary treatment systems and would also 
generate more solids for disposal. It should also be noted that these types of added filtration steps 
could remove several “micro -pollutants” leading to issues in demonstrating the treatment 
performance of the ozonation and granular activated carbon systems. 
The Feasibility Study states that tertiary treatment should provide an effluent containing 5 -10 mg/L 
Dissolved Organic Carbon (DOC) and that total micropollutants constitute at most 1 mg/L DOC. 
Because both activat ed carbon and ozone are non -selective technologies in terms of DOC removal, 
this means that 80-90% of the DOC removed, and thus 80-90% of the cost, of the proposed quaternary 
treatment will be for chemicals that the Directive does not categorize as micropollutants. Thus, even 
if pharmaceuticals and personal care products made up 100% of the toxicity load on WWTPs, which 
they do not, they would be bearing 100% of the EPR burden while contributing only 10 -20% of the 
DOC burden. This outcome is in direct conflict with the concept of the polluter pays principle.

7. The proposal has not done an accurate inventory and assessment of Greenhouse Gas 
(GHG) emissions associated with quaternary treatment.  
One goal of the UWWTD is to reduce GHG em issions. The impact assessment estimated that there 
would be a decrease of 4.86 million tonnes of GHG emissions (37.32 % of the sector). The estimates 
do not appear to consider the GHG emissions associated with energy use by the ozonation process 
and GHG emissions associated with generating, re-generating, transporting and disposing of activated 
carbon.  
For example, based on our projected amount of potential granular activated carbon treatment 
systems needed, it is estimated that approximately 1,251,986 kg of activated carbon may need to be 
disposed of every 2 years if all 4630 Agglomerations serving 10,000 pe or more and all Agglomerations 
serving more than 100,000 p.e are required to install granular activated carbon treatment systems. 
These estimates ass ume that the 2 -year mass of carbon needed is based on 20 g GAC/m 3/day of 
wastewater flow and that the granular activated carbon cannot be reactivated after two years of use. 
If granular activated carbon cannot be reclaimed after 2 years of use, we estimate that approximately 
4,588 tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO 2) will be emitted to the atmosphere every 2 years from 
incinerating this material as waste, unless if used for the production of electricity instead of using fossil 
fuels.

The carbon footprint for regenerating granular activated carbon has been estimated to be 0.32 kg of

The overall GHG emissions from quaternary treatment, should be part of a cost-benefit analyses that 
should support the treatment of micropollutants on the basis of risk to the environment in a holistic 
view.    
 
8. An alternative approach 
As an alternative to the proposed EPR mechanism, the pharmaceutical industry is in favour of a Swiss- 
like model. The way it is designed brings all sources and sectors of micro-pollution to be covered and 
ensures the transparency of all costs to stakeholders, while not pre-selecting or limiting the scope of 
polluters to pharmaceutical and cosmetic products. In the Swiss model, the confederation supports 
WWTPs by financing measures to minimize/eliminate organic trace su bstances. This includes the 
implementation costs, and it is funded through the user-pays principle. The confederation also levies 
a fee on all holders of central WWTPs to finance measures against organic trace substances including 
the implementation costs of the confederation. The amount of the levy is based on the number of 
inhabitants connected to the WWTP and amounts to CHF 9 per inhabitant. Holders of central WWTPs 
shall be exempted from the levy if they take measures to eliminate organic trace substances. 
The upgrade of the most impactful water treatment facilities to quaternary water treatment level in 
Switzerland (130 out of 715) will cost in total 1.2 billion CHF francs (similar to 1.2 billion Euros) over a 
period of 24 years (2016-2040). Additional re-occurring costs (e.g. additional energy consumption, the 
selected process maintenance (filer replacement, etc.) depends strongly on the size of UWWTP, 
technology of choice, etc., but is always significantly lower than quaternary treatment system 
implementation costs. Since 2016, 14 WWTPs have implemented the necessary measures in 
Switzerland. Of these, 12 have built an elimination stage for micropollutants; two WWTPs were closed 
and connected to another WWTP – for this, a sewer line was built instead of the measure required for 
at the WWTP.

9. Further development of WWTPs as resource hubs integral to resource recovery, e.g., on 
water reuse, nutrient recovery, energy generation may interfere with the two industrial 
sectors obligated to pay for establishment of the quaternary treatment. 
Currently, there is varying discussion in the EU on how to improve WWTPs. These plans may interfere 
with the two industrial sectors obligated to pay for establishment of the quaternary treatment, as 
conversions of the plants may be used for other purposes than removal of micro-pollutants only. The 
Feasibility Study however does not address such activities and resulting funding obligations.  
Hence, there may be an insufficient scope in the Feasibility Study and two industrial sectors are at risk 
to be asked to financially compensate for third -party interests and business for resource recovery 
activities that should be outside the scope of this proposal. We therefore would highly appreciate if 
such possible interferences would be addressed and either transparently excluded or if all 
stakeholders responsible as “pol luters” just as beneficiaries would be considered in financing 
improved wastewater treatment. 
The Feasibility Study (page 72, marginal cost analysis) acknowledges that its implementation of 
quaternary treatment for micropollutant removal will require upgra ding treatment to tertiary 
treatment (defined as nitrogen removal) at all WWTPs within its scope, regardless of whether or not 
nutrient removal is required to achieve water quality objectives. It proposes that the costs (capital and 
operation) for upgrading WWTPs to tertiary treatment will “… need to be reflected in water tariffs or 
passed on to the entities responsible for nitrogen pollution via different policy options.”  Given that 
the current directive specifies the addition of tertiary treatment at exi sting WWTPs is required only 
on a receiving water specific basis, i.e., when there is a risk of eutrophication, this proposed blanket 
requirement for upgrading all WWTPs within the scope of the proposed rule to tertiary treatment is a 
direct cost attributable to the proposal and must be considered in the overall cost-benefit evaluation 
of the proposal. Furthermore, as discussed elsewhere in these comments, the necessary upgrading to 
tertiary treatment must also include upgrading removal of BOD, TSS and DOC in order to assure that 
quaternary treatment successfully achieves the planned reductions in micropollutants.

11. The proposal could be inconsistent with the Pharmaceuticals Strategy in terms of 
ensuring continued patient accessibility to efficacious medicines.  
The proposal could be inconsistent with the Pharmaceuticals Strategy in terms of ensuring continued 
patient accessibility to efficacious medicines. It is critically important that design and implementation 
of a robust and meaningful EPR policy is not only proportional and has a holistic approach to all 
micropollutants but does not inadvertently result in impeding patient access to medicines.  
Similarly, the impact assessment lacks an assessment of the increased Greenhouse Gas emissions due 
to quaternary treatment. This should be an essential part of the cost-benefit analyses, and should be 
considered against other objectives of the EU Green Deal.  
The proposed UWWTD EPR Strategy as it is planned by the Commission is a threat to the strategic 
autonomy of the EU in the field of research and supply. For example, increasing price pressure would 
be caused by the Commission’s proposal especially in co nnection with underestimated costs on 
product expenses and profit margins as noted in earlier comments. This is especially important in 
these times of growing global economic competition, inflation, high energy prices and general 
economic uncertainty even in the far future.

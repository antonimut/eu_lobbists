NICOLE Opinion  – Integrated water management – revised lists of surface and groundwater 
pollutants 
14 March 2023 
 
 
 
NICOLE is the only European network combining industry, service providers and academics, formed in 1996 
with an aim to bring together professionals involved in sustainable risk-based management of land and 
water in Europe – www.nicole.org. NICOLE has drafted this paper to reflect its opinion based on its long-
term experience in the field of industrial land management, in Europe and internationally, and to provide 
its practical experiences regarding sustainable land management and PFAS contaminated soil and 
groundwater. 
 
Since its inception NICOLE and its members have been focused on sustainable land management of 
industrial sites. Over the years, our focus and insight have evolved, culminating in several publications on 
Land Stewardship (booklet) and associated initiatives, which explain how to create value by connecting 
industrial land management to societal, ecological and economic challenges. Another recent example is a 
joint position paper with Common Forum on how to include sustainability in contaminated land 
management (position paper). 
 
NICOLE welcomes the recognition of the need to establish EQS for PFAS, however, there are a number of 
issues of concern that would be useful to be clarified before defining appropriate EQS values. NICOLE’s 
opinion is that the proposed EQS are unfeasibly low, often below ambient levels in surface and 
groundwater, and are based on science whose robustness and relevance is being questioned by 
authoritative bodies around the world (SCHEER opinion (2022), UK CoT statement (2022), WHO statement 
(2022)). The available data on ambient levels are scarce but worrisome and we expect the adverse impacts 
of trying to regulate to such low values to be numerous. Below, we have summarized some of our concerns 
for your consideration. We would be happy to be part of any conversation to try to resolve these issues.

Our concerns center around three topics: 
 
1. Potential (unintended) policy consequences 
• All indications are that PFAS impacts to the environment are ubiquitous, leading to 
concentrated and diffuse contamination around the globe. In many cases it may be impossible 
to apply the polluter pays principle, due to the absence of a singular polluter or a polluter not 
having or being able to have knowledge of a product containing PFAS (for instance firefighters 
using PFAS containing foams).  
• We believe land stewardship is an important principle for the sustainable management of 
industrial and other land. We therefore advocate for the application of sustainable remediation 
practices as outlined in the ISO 18054 guideline for the sustainable remediation of 
contaminated sites. For many PFAS, treatments to remove them to the proposed EQS are likely 
to require intense and costly treatment, potentially involving a combination of technologies 
and resulting in hazardous waste streams that require additional treatment or disposal 
exceeding the principles of sustainable management and the objectives described in the EU 
Circular Economy Action Plan. 
• The low EQS may become a significant barrier in the redevelopment/reuse of land, not only 
industrial land but also agricultural land and other land uses dealing with diffuse PFAS impacts. 
This likely results in an increase in derelict land. 
 
 
2. Proportionality: 
• It is our understanding that the proposed EQS are based on food consumption. While most 
human exposure to PFAS appears to be related to food consumption, the water sources the 
EQS apply to are generally not directly consumed by or come into contact with humans. In 
addition, the proposed EQS are now significantly lower than drinking water standards.  
 
 
 
- 3 - 
• The proposed EQS, even when human or ecological risk is not evident, is likely to generate and 
divert environmental impact, considering the resulting water treatment, emissions to water 
and air, consumption of energy, and production of PFAS waste that will need further treatment 
or disposal to a different location. 
• As PFAS are already present in the environment, we would favor a risk-based approach where 
the EQS is divided into categories based on the vulnerability (degree of protection) and the 
sensitivity (based on the use) of the aquifer. This would limit exposure risk for recipients, 
analogous to the risk-based approach common in remediation projects.  Suggestions include: 
o Groundwater which is flowing towards surface water within a certain distance 
o Groundwater which is used or could reasonably be used as drinking water 
o Groundwater which is not directly going to surface water and is also not likely to be 
used as drinking water or for irrigation and not lead to exposure, whereas when 
remediated and treated there could be exposure 
 
3. Technical Feasibility 
• The chemical analysis of PFBA and PFBS (and others of the less-well studied 24 PFAS 
compounds) is often unreliable due to incomplete recovery during laboratory analysis and it is 
challenging to reach the detection levels required to meet these EQS.  
• PFAS is often not the only driver in determining the limit of quantification (LOQ) when water 
samples are analyzed. In our experience complex water matrix interference often occurs, 
leading to detection limits higher than the proposed EQS. This is likely to lead to false positives 
and/or uncertainty in determining the presence and level of PFAS when performing laboratory 
analysis. 
• Directly related to the bullet above analytical results for PFAS require extreme caution on 
sampling in the field to analysis in the laboratory and the need for applying strict QA/QC 
protocols due to the high risk of cross contamination at such low levels. The risk of false 
positives is a substantial concern.

The current Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive (UWWTD) was adopted on 21 May 
1991, aimed primarily at protecting the environment. Following three decades of its 
implementation, the European Commission impact assessment identified a need for the text 
to be updated to improve, among other things, aspects included therein relating to the 
following objectives: 
 Reducing the pollution load discharged into the environment. The proposal 
addresses topics such as waste water treatment processes in small agglomerations, 
individual treatment systems, stormwater and urban runoff overflows, industrial 
discharges or tighter regulations regarding the removal of nutrients and 
micropollutants. 
 Aligning the directive with new challenges arising from regulatory changes in 
Europe, such as the European Green Deal, by including aspects relating to the 
circular economy, energy neutrality, control of greenhouse gas emissions or 
wastewater-based epidemiological surveillance. 
 Improving governance. By introducing extended producer responsibility schemes, 
investment planning, monitoring and reporting or obligations to provide information to 
the public or ensure access to sanitation. 
The Proposal for a revised Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive was published on 26 
October 2022; the full text of the directive, its annexes and the impact assessment reports 
are available on the European Commission website at this link.

Waste Water Treatment Directive and agrees with the European Commission that further

We agree that the objectives of this new draft directive (hereinafter the Proposal) are aimed 
at achieving greater protection for EU citizens and ecosystems from sources of pollution, as 
well as improving transparency and governance within the framework of digital 
transformation and aligning the industry with the European Green Deal. However, AEAS 
believes that the Proposal comprises some aspects that shall be discussed before its 
adoption due to their implications so that new legislation is effectively implemented: 
 Given its great economic impact, the Proposal should promote a sound financing 
strategy, while ensuring the affordability of water tariffs and better application of the 
Polluter Pays Principle, in relation to both users and those directly concerned with the 
principle of extended producer responsibility. 
 A deadline extension is needed to meet many of the requirements, thus making 
them feasible. Otherwise, the collapse of the proposed measures is certain. 
 A more decisive commitment should be made to implement control at source 
measures for pollutants, and not to place the solution to issues created earlier in the 
process in the hands of urban WWTPs. Measures should be taken to identify and 
reduce the sources of pollution when pollutants that pose a risk to treatment at 
WWTPs, the health of sanitation workers or the receiving environment are identified 
at the inlet or outlet of WWTPs. 
 In order to prioritise necessary actions and increase efficiency, the Proposal should 
focus on an approach based on risk management and the protection of 
receiving water bodies, to achieve good ecological status, rather than on 
establishing overly strict emission limit values for water quality parameters.  
Suggested improvements 
1. In relation to the articles on tertiary and quaternary treatment processes 
The new limit values for Total N and Total P are extremely demanding, making major 
investments in existing WWTPs necessary to comply with the proposed limits, which would 
result in a very high economic and technical impact, without providing any real 
environmental benefit in many cases. These limit values are very costly, due to new 
construction and renewal of existing infrastructure, and also greatly increase the 
consumption of electricity and other resources, such as reagents, which are necessary to 
adequately treat waste water to achieve the new targets.  
According to the article 4 of the Directive 2000/40/EC, to ensure a good ecological status of 
receiving waters with the highest effiency of WWTP, two different thresholds are 
proposed depending on the size (one regarding WWTP bigger than 100,000 p.e. and 
another corresponding to WWTP for 10,000 – 100,000 p.e.) with different limit values for 
each threshold. Besides, we appeal for a reduction of 20% of the current concentration 
limits specified in Directive 91/271. However, we encourage excluding WWTP discharging 
into open coastal waters with no risk of eutrophication because it does not guaranty any 
further environmental improvement. Finally, AEAS considers that urban waste water 
intended for reuse purposes as irrigation in agriculture should be exempted from compliance 
with the new nutrient limit values set for tertiary treatment processes.

Regarding micropollutants, while recognising the benefits of reducing their concentration, 
AEAS is of the view that implementing control-at-source measures is much more efficient 
than introducing high-cost treatment processes at WWTPs to reduce their concentration in 
treated effluent.  
Besides, the proposed timeframe for ensuring the compliance of existing facilities is very 
tight, in view that current advanced treatment processes are unreasonably costly, both from 
an economic and an environmental perspective. It must be taken into account that these 
result in a great deal of waste and high energy and resource consumption.  
In terms of tertiary and quaternary treatment processes taking place at newly constructed 
facilities or those at the tendering stage, design requirements should be maintained, until 
a minimum amortisation period has elapsed. 
When implementing quaternary treatment processes at WWTPs, AEAS believes a risk 
management approach to be necessary, i.e., by prioritising taking action in those facilities 
that will yield the greatest environmental benefit, rather than doing so based on size or other 
features. 
In relation to the assessment of treatment quality, micropollutant concentration levels 
should be included as an alternative parameter to the proposed percentage removal of 
micropollutants. Concentration levels for some substances to be removed may be so low at 
the inlet of the facilities that reaching the 80 % removal target would be incredibly difficult and 
costly, without any real environmental benefit. Moreover, an assessment based on 
percentages requires a double monitoring system and affects all parameters equally, since 
no reference to any substance prioritisation is made in the text, thus driving up costs. 
Finally, the list of micropollutants to be monitored should be consistent with priority 
hazardous substances with associated environmental quality standard limit values, in 
addition to other additional pollutants to be determined by each Member State based on their 
specific environmental conditions.  
2. Energy neutrality 
AEAS finds the objectives of the Proposal to be too ambitious. The association is of the view 
that the alternatives necessary to achieve energy neutrality should be adjusted to the 
situation and possibilities of each territory. Sludge digestion and cogeneration-based 
energy neutrality in WWTPs bigger than 100,000 p.e. can only be fulfilled in those cases 
where anaerobic digestion on a larger scale enables co-digestion to take place. Most 
WWTPs for 10,000-100,000 p.e. can only reach 25-50% neutrality, making it difficult to use 
anaerobic digestion on the basis of technical feasibility and economical profitability; this 
added, on many occasions, to the lack of space for the installation of photovoltaic plants, as 
an alternative and complementary technology. 
Likewise, AEAS deems it essential to be able to include any renewable energy used in 
the plants, regardless of its source (produced on site or purchased from the grid), as well 
as that supplied by them to external distribution networks (thermal, electric, gas, etc.), within 
the computation of the total amount. In addition, the possibility of establishing energy 
communities among different plants should be considered.

and entry into force of the directive. The reference year should be 2050 (that of the

3. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) 
The high cost of implementing quaternary treatment processes in WWTPs, to ensure 
compliance with the requirements established in Article 8 and Annex I, makes it necessary to 
design and enforce EPR schemes establishing that total costs shall be borne by 
producers, which should be in place before the expiry of the first deadlines set for 
compliance with quaternary treatment requirements. 
Likewise, it is also important that the manner in which the money raised shall be passed on 
to operators for the implementation of new treatments, as well as whether coverage shall 
also include operating costs, be described in greater detail. 
4. Combined sewer overflows 
The Proposal sets an "indicative" target for stormwater discharges from overflows at 1% 
of the annual urban waste water load within the agglomeration concerned, as calculated 
under dry weather conditions. Under the current climate change scenario, and particularly in 
Mediterranean Europe, fulfilling this target is considered to be almost impossible, and would 
for sure entail significant investments. In addition, under the Proposal, deadlines for 
compliance with the aforementioned target are established within Annex 5, thus making it 
compulsory; consequently, this inconsistency within the text should be addressed and 
corrected. 
AEAS proposes that the objective be set as part of the Integrated Management Plans, 
dependent on local context and legislation and tied to the achievement of previous 
environmental objectives. 
Finally, in order to control pollution caused by rainwater and urban runoff, what parameters 
are representative and feasible to conduct monitoring tasks should be defined, together 
with the possibility of using sanitation network modelling tools.  
5. Invesment planning 
AEAS finds it particularly positive that the directive lays down the need to identify and plan 
all necessary investments for both the carrying out of actions focusing on and leading to 
compliance with the obligations outlined above and the renewal of existing urban waste 
water treatment infrastructure, and defines potential sources of financing. 
However, it is very important that EU structural and investment funds provide economic 
support, by means of a clear regulatory framework, so that those facilities making it possible 
to meet key requirements set forth in the new directive can be built.  
It is also important that investment planning, as well as deadlines, be aligned with River 
Basin Management Plans. The time horizon for investments envisaged by plans recently 
adopted in Spain is 2027, thus making it difficult to undertake new investments if not 
currently projected. Regarding the transposition of the directive now under review into the 
national law, 2026 could be a reasonable forecast. 
Finally, AEAS considers it necessary to further define the obligation on the part of 
polluting industries to contribute to the costs of quaternary treatment processes by

6. Monitoring and control 
The proposal establishes a very demanding system for the control and monitoring of a 
series of pollutants, at the inlets and outlets of WWTPs servicing all agglomerations bigger 
than 10,000 p.e. The list of pollutants should be clear and concise, avoiding duplication. 
Furthermore, some of these pollutants pose problems relating to the lack of well-tested, 
validated, or even defined, techniques; such is the case of microplastics, both in water and 
sludge; this, in addition to the high cost of these techniques.  
AEAS is alarmed by the fact that loads and concentrations of pollutants from storm water 
and urban runoff overflows in all agglomerations bigger than 10,000 e.p. are to be 
monitored. In the association's view, this is impossible, unless these agglomerations are 
provided with the necessary economic resources to sensor-equip the sanitation network, and 
the impact on receiving water bodies is modelled and verified, which would also be aligned 
with the Water Framework Directive (WFD).  
Regarding the annual minimum number of samples required to verify compliance with 
discharge limit values for secondary, tertiary and quaternary treatment parameters, the 
Proposal establishes these are to be conducted too often, something disproportionate to 
the benefit likely to accrue from this increased sampling frequency, which represents a 
threefold, sixfold, or even twentyfold increase, depending on WWTP size, in comparison to 
the existing directive. AEAS is of the opinion that full advantage should be taken of the 
opportunities provided by digitalisation and online instruments. This would be a suitable and 
complementary alternative to laboratory analyses, thus reducing the annual number of 
samples and, consequently, the consumption of polluting reagents generated during 
laboratory tests. The costs associated with the proposed increased sampling will lead to 
an increased of a hundredfold, in the case of WWTPs bigger than 100,000 p.e.  
7. Small agglomerations and individual systems 
AEAS does not agree with setting requirements for individual treatment systems as stringent 
as those applicable to secondary or tertiary treatment processes at WWTPs. Treatment 
options providing an equivalent level of environmental protection of the receiving 
environment should be allowed, based on the aforementioned risk-based approach. 
8. Sludge 
Sludge valorisation for agricultural use is clearly and necessarily beneficial to the 
environment; thus, it should be considered the main option for the recovery and reuse of 
nutrients. However, this sludge destination requires implementing control of pollution at 
source measures, in order to be able to manage it sustainably. 
Nitrogen inputs to soils from the recycling of sludge and/or struvite formation should be 
considered as reused nutrients rather than nutrient loads discharged into the 
receiving environment.  
Likewise, AEAS is of the opinion that the possible future delegated act provided for in the text 
should be linked to the revised Sewage Sludge Directive, aimed at encouraging the correct 
use of sludge in agriculture.

9. Urban waste water surveillance 
Waste water operators are responsible for ensuring the proper functioning of sanitation 
systems (WWTPs and/or collection networks); however, the monitoring of health parameters 
is beyond the scope of their activity. Epidemiological surveillance programmes are part of 
health surveillance operations, falling under the competence of healthcare authorities.  
Although the urban water sector is willing to collaborate with the competent authority, the 
workload involved in devising such surveillance processes shall be reasonable for 
operators. The planning, coordination and assumption of the cost of these tasks 
should be incumbent on healthcare authorities. In order to cover the real technical cost and 
financial costs of these operations, there would be a requirement for additional allocations, 
which are currently not being taken into account.  
The fact that sampling points and sampling frequency can be decided at the national 
level is welcomed.

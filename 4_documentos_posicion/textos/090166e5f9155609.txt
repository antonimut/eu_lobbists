Executive Summary 
 
Medicines for Europe represents generic, biosimilar and value-added medicines industries across Europe, 
which supply 70% of dispensed medicines in Europe and cover 80% of the therapeutic areas.  
 
Our sector is engaged in efforts to reduce the environmental and climate footprint of the pharmaceutical 
industry through a science-based approach covering the entire lifecycle of medicine while ensuring equitable 
patient access to safe, effective and high-quality treatments.  
European Union policies to address pharmaceutical residues in the environment should be proportionate, 
based on scientific evidence, consider the public health impact and otherwise comply with European laws and 
principles. The recent proposal to revise the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive (UWWTD) fails to meet 
these requirements given that:  
• the proposed extended producer responsibility (EPR) system is inconsistent with the established legal 
principles of conferral, subsidiarity and proportionality and further it does not address the established 
position that pharmaceutical legislation has prevalence over general legislation to ensure a high level of 
public health protection and the quality, safety and efficacy of authorised medicines. 
• proportionality has not been properly assessed, since the scope of medicines that will be required to 
contribute to the EPR and the financial contribution to the water industry are undefined. This uncertainty 
makes the measure impossible to quantify. 
• it does not cover other industrial sectors, despite acknowledgement within the feasibility study that 
other sectors also contribute to the presence of micro-pollutants in wastewater. The responsibility and 
financial burden for establishing additional wastewater treatment (quaternary treatment) and to 
stimulate an environmentally friendly environment has to be distributed between all relevant actors. The 
proposal should not have been based on a discriminatory and arbitrary selection of sectors. 
• it is based on a feasibility study and impact assessment with clear methodological flaws, due to  
o the inaccurate and limited scientific evidence of environmental impact from the pharmaceutical 
sector and other industrial sectors 
o the absence of an assessment on the feasibility of replacing current medicines with alternative 
molecules. No evidence is presented to demonstrate that there are alternatives available to replace 
active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) without compromising safety and efficacy. 
o the vastly underestimated effect on the off-patent pharmaceutical industry, which supplies life-
saving products in high volumes and at low-profit margins. As medicine prices are strictly regulated 
on the Member State level in the EU, manufacturers are not at liberty of introducing price increases 
even if the cost of manufacturing rises. Therefore, the proposal’s suggestion for the recuperation of 
EPR costs through increasing the product price is irrelevant.   
• it will jeopardise patient access to critical, safe and affordable medicines by threatening the viability of 
manufacturing several generic molecules, which can potentially lead to withdrawals of products from the 
market. 
 
Therefore, Medicines for Europe calls on the EU Council and Parliament to re-evaluate the legal basis of the 
proposal and the associated economic and public health impact before moving forward with any 
discussions. All alternative options for supporting the set-up and the operations of the quaternary treatment 
by distributing the responsibility and financial burden among all relevant actors should be explored and 
measures that consider the specificities of the pharmaceutical sector, particularly off-patent manufacturing, 
should be applied to avoid unintended consequences on patient access to safe and affordable medicines.

Background 
  
On 26 October 2022, the European Commission proposed a recast of the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive 
to tackle pollution from urban sources and align the wastewater sector with the European Green Deal. 
 
The proposal introduces an extended producer responsibility (EPR) system that will require companies placing 
medicines for human use and cosmetics on the market of Member States (in practice only Marketing 
Authorisation Holders (MAH) can do this for medicines) to finance the additional quaternary treatment required 
to remove micro-pollutants from urban wastewater, if their products contain substances which, at the end of 
their life, are found as micro-pollutants in urban wastewater. The relative financial contribution of the companies 
will be based on the quantities and hazardousness of the products placed on the market. 
 
The off-patent sector is fully engaged in efforts to address the presence of pharmaceutical residues in the 
environment through a science-based approach covering the entire lifecycle of a medicine (Eco-Pharmaco 
Stewardship). These efforts include developing and implementing strict standards for the management of 
manufacturing effluents for antimicrobials (AMR Industry Alliance) and other products that could pose a risk to 
the environment, as well as by raising awareness on the proper disposal of unused medicines (Meds Disposal).  
 
However, Medicines for Europe strongly opposes the proposed EPR system, since it: 
• undermines Member State competence to raise levies and it contradicts the EU Pharmaceutical Directive.  
• is based on a feasibility study and impact assessment with methodological flaws that: 
o do not demonstrate the impact of pharmaceutical residues on the environment; 
o fail to define the scope of products; 
o focus on two sectors only rather than all relevant sectors and without providing any objective 
justification; and 
o do not accurately report the financial burden of the EPR.  
• primarily impacts the off-patent pharmaceutical sector due to the focus on medicines volumes and in turn 
jeopardises patient access to safe and affordable medicines. 
• does not consider the impact on public health that reduced uptakes of medicines or reformulation could 
have.  
 
Legal concerns on the EU’s legitimacy of introducing the proposed EPR system 
 
The proposal seems to breach several EU Treaty principles: 
• Conferral and subsidiarity (art. 5 Treaty on the European Union, TEU): Since the measure is in practice a levy, 
it would interfere with the levy-related autonomy of Member States and potentially transgress national 
taxation competences. EPR fees seek to steer the industry toward the design of “greener” products, but the 
proposal goes beyond that mandate and instead penalizes an industry and products in which, as 
acknowledged in the underlying impact assessment and feasibility study, there is no realistic possibility of 
substituting most medicines used today by alternative medicines that do not leave micro-substance traces 
elements in wastewater. Moreover, it would not be permitted within the laws governing medicinal products 
to redesign a pharmaceutical compound for environmental reasons if that change were to reduce the 
pharmacological efficacy or increase the safety risks of the medicine. 
• Proportionality principle (art. 5 TEU): The proposed EPR system is based on a feasibility study that has 
significant data gaps and methodological flaws and does not consider the serious consequences on public 
health that this scheme would have. Indeed, as the off-patent pharmaceutical industry provides most 
medicines to patients, the disproportionate impact on the sector, due to its low profit margins and high 
volumes, would have serious consequences on the access and availability of medicines (see next sections).  
By only seeking to address the presence of pharmaceutical residues in wastewater due to physiological 
excretion by patients, the scheme aims to rectify pollution at source by reducing the uptake of medicines or 
changing their formulation (and therefore their efficacy) which would have severe consequences on public

Methodological flaws in the feasibility study and impact assessment 
 
The study “Feasibility of an EPR system for micro-pollutants”1, which formed the basis for the current proposal, 
contains methodological flaws and fails to establish the necessary evidence to justify the proposal.  
In particular, the study, the impact assessment and the final proposal: 
• Are based on very limited and/or inaccurately characterised scientific evidence.  
o The study does not provide the methodology and sources for the estimate that pharmaceuticals 
contribute up to 66% of the total toxic load in wastewaters2. 
o The data used to estimate the presence of harmful substances in waterbodies refers only to the 
German Land of North-Rhine Westphalia3 and the extrapolation cannot be considered representative 
of the whole European Union as it ignores the variability of medicines consumption across Member 
States – or in other words, making assumptions of the entire European Union population based on the 
sample of a part of Germany is inherently flawed and misleading.  
o The forecasting of masses of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) placed on the market was 
incorrect as it relied on the estimation of one country, Denmark, extrapolated to all Member States 
without considering epidemiological data (incidence and prevalence of diseases) and the variability of 
consumption of each individual country or external factors such as high veterinary medicines 
consumption. For instance, two of the antibiotics considered, dicloxacillin and piperacillin-tazobactam, 
are not as widely used in the whole Europe. Moreover, the authors extrapolated this data to project 
sales for 2050 of APIs that will reach water treatment plants using the same methodology, without 
considering this epidemiological data and external factors that may influence the prescription of these 
medicines – for example, vaccination can reduce the need for some medicines, or substitution with 
alternative or biotechnological products.  
o The methodology for  the calculation/definition of the most environmentally critical micro-pollutants 
is not given and is not accessible in the articles referred to in the study and cannot therefore be verified.   
o It is not acceptable that the feasibility study covered only pharmaceuticals and cosmetics without 
analysing other sectors significantly contributing to the presence of micro-pollutants, including micro-
plastics, in wastewater. According to the study, 5 out of the top 10 most harmful substances detected 
are not APIs. There are many industrial sectors producing substances potentially containing micro-
pollutants beyond pharmaceuticals and cosmetics. Literature and monitoring data demonstrate that 
compounds from chemical/heavy industry, food, plant protection products and the biocides industry 
contribute higher amounts and diversity to the presence of micro-pollutants into the environment.4 
Some of these compounds have higher wastewater and environmental concentrations and are equally 
or even more ecotoxic and persistent than APIs. The automatic inclusion of pharmaceuticals was justified 
by a research publication which looked at the presence of API in surface water and specifically 
recommended that the same research could be applied to other classes of pollutants, including

• Vastly underestimate the impact on the off-patent pharmaceutical sector.  
o The proposal assumes that the pharmaceutical industry will need to contribute an amount of more than 
€1 billion annually to finance the EPR for quaternary wastewater treatment and this will cost the industry 
an average maximum of 0.7% of profits6. However, this is almost certainly an underestimation of the 
true cost.  
o If a 0.7% EPR levy were applied on all generic prescription medicines by sales value, which is many 
multiples higher than the profit margins for generics, the system would only collect €300 million. That is 
only one third of the amount to be collected, whilst generic medicines account for 70% of prescription 
medicine volumes. Since many medicines have no impact on wastewater residues, they should also be 
excluded from the EPR. This clearly shows that the calculations behind the cost impact of this measure 
are flawed and that the true impact on the off-patent pharmaceutical sector would be much greater 
than is currently being represented to legislators.7 This should take into account the fact that under 
national pricing regulations, companies manufacturing generic medicines will not be able to increase 
their prices to limit the impact on their profit margins. Using the IQVIA database, Medicines for Europe 
has applied the quaternary treatment cost (€ 1.213 billion/year) to the 12 most sold pharmaceuticals 
cited in the feasibility study8 and found that on average their MA holders would need to contribute over 
10% of their sales value to pay for 80% of the funds to be collected by the pharmaceutical sector. 
Moreover, for some of those molecules, the EPR fee would exceed the total annual sales revenue of the 
product in question. 
o The estimates of pharmaceutical industry profits in the feasibility study were based on non-specialised 
Reuters data on 21 high-profit originators and just 1 company active in the off-patent sector (see table 
35 of the feasibility study)9. Incidentally, the former is the only one showing negative profit margins. 
Some of the companies cited in this article are not even pharmaceutical manufacturers since they market 
medical devices.  This clearly shows an underestimate of the cost of the EPR for medicine manufacturers 
and its impact on specific low margin medicines.  
o Although not cited in the impact assessment, the feasibility study acknowledges that the impact on 
margins “would be significant for some substances that have a relatively lower selling price, such as 
generic pharmaceuticals” and for this reason recommended “not to use quantities solely as a basis”10. 
This recommendation was disregarded in the final proposal of the Commission.  
o The proposal foresees that EPR fees will be collected based on the volume/weight of the products 
marketed and their (undefined) hazardousness. This volume criterion places a disproportionate burden 
on the off-patent sector, which accounts for 70% of prescription medicine volumes, enabling access to 
affordable, safe, effective, and life-saving medicines for patients, whilst having much lower profit 
margins relative to originator products, and may result in the withdrawal of products from the market.

• Do not assess the feasibility of replacing APIs with “greener” alternatives.  
o Contrary to most of the other industrial sectors, pharmaceutical APIs are intensively researched over a 
lengthy development phase: physical-chemical (solubility, degradation), in vitro/in vivo mechanism of 
action in different species, pharmacokinetics (metabolism, excretion in different species), 
toxicology/ecotoxicology in different species as well as fate in WWTP and in the environment are all 
investigated. The lifecycle and fate of APIs are well-defined due to strict regulations which are not 
applicable for the majority of other industrial sectors, therefore micro-pollutants from other industrial 
sectors are more difficult to define and to monitor in the environment and the risk of unknown pollutants 
from other industrial sectors remains even higher. 
o Directives 2001/83/EC and 2001/82/EC require an Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA) as part of the 
dossier for marketing authorization applications for new pharmaceutical products. Most ERA 
conclusions are that APIs cause no, or an acceptable, risk for the environment when used by patients 
according to the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), which is evaluated through performing a 
battery of phys-chem, environmental effect and environmental fate studies. Such a comprehensive ERA 
is not available for most of substances/micro-polluters from other industrial sectors. 
o The feasibility study presents no evidence to demonstrate that there are alternatives available to 
replace active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) without compromising patient safety and efficacy. The 
study recognizes that for a “number of products, there is no green alternative”.11 
o The Commission should have assessed with the EMA, national regulators and relevant stakeholders, the 
scientific feasibility of replacing affected molecules with “greener” alternatives.  
o The proposal fails therefore to consider the essential role of medicines for public health and the need 
for companies and regulators to prioritise the safety, quality and efficacy of medicines, which is the 
fundamental purpose of the EU pharmaceutical legislation. 
o Moreover, the proposal does not consider the improper disposal of unused and expired medicines, 
which can importantly increase the level of any sort of medicines in urban wastewater. Medicines for 
Europe, together with other healthcare stakeholders, has been engaged since 2015 in a campaign to  
raise awareness on how to dispose of unused or expired medicines appropriately in Europe.  
 
Consequences for patient access to safe and affordable medicines 
 
The failure to consider the unique features of medicinal products and the particular impact on the off-patent 
pharmaceutical sector, which supplies 70% of prescribed medicines in Europe covering 80% of therapeutic areas, 
contradicts the goal of the Pharmaceutical Strategy to improve patient access to safe and affordable medicines. 
 
Given they are subject to national pricing regulations, companies manufacturing generic medicines will not be 
able  to increase prices and will therefore experience a significant reduction in profit margins. This threatens 
the viability of the production of the affected molecules. A clear example would be metformin, which is on the 
WHO List of Essential Medicines and is used by 30 million patients suffering from diabetes every day in Europe. 
Despite its critical importance for public health, it is cited in the feasibility study and listed as one of the molecules 
for which the EPR fees could exceed the profit margins (see table 39). The public health implications of applying 
the EPR to such molecules would be huge.  
 
The off-patent pharmaceutical industry is fully committed to maintaining the supply of medicines to Europe, but 
the introduction of the EPR levy would undermine the viability of many essential medicines.   
 
The sector is fully engaged in efforts to reduce the pharmaceutical industry environmental footprint, but believes 
that any measure taken in this regard must ensure patient access to safe, effective, high-quality affordable 
medicines, in line with the EU Strategic Approach to Pharmaceuticals in the Environment.

Conclusions 
 
Medicines for Europe calls on the EU Council and Parliament to thoroughly re-evaluate the legal basis of the 
proposal and the associated economic and public health impact before moving forward with further 
discussions.  
Scientific literature demonstrates that various industrial sectors contribute significantly to the presence of micro-
pollutants in urban wastewater. All alternative options of supporting the setting up and operations of the 
quaternary treatment by distributing the responsibility and financial burden between all relevant actors should 
be explored and measures that consider the specificities of the pharmaceutical sector, particularly off-patent 
manufacturing, to avoid jeopardizing patient access to safe and affordable medicines should be applied.

ANNEX - Economic assessment of the study “Feasibility of an EPR system for micro-pollutants” 
 
The European Commission estimates that the new EPR fees will have a limited impact on pharmaceutical 
companies, which will have the choice of either passing these new costs on in the price of their products, with a 
maximum increase of 0.59% or reducing the profit margin on them with a maximum impact of 0.7%. Moreover, 
some products may be exempted, for instance those products placed on the market in small quantities (i.e. less 
than 2 tonnes of products) or if the producer demonstrates that no micro-pollutants are generated at the end 
life of a product, or substances that biodegrade in a few hours before reaching WWTP. 
 
The proposal estimates the total EPR fee collection would amount to € 1.213 billion/year, € 1.186 billion/year 
for all micro-pollutants + administrative cost (PRO set-up). This collection would be distributed between eligible 
sectors, where the pharmaceutical industry will bear 74% to 81% of the total EPR costs while the cosmetic sector 
will bear 10% to 26% of the costs, depending on the selected allocation method. 
 
If all generic, prescription-bound medicines, sold on the EU markets in 2021, were to pay an EPR contribution to 
the amount of 0.7% of the total sales value12, we would collect € 326 Million Eur.13 Hence, it can be assumed that 
the impact on the prices of the pharmaceutical products affected by the new ERP system would be much greater 
than that estimated in the impact assessment, or that the contribution would not meet the objective of covering 
the cost of the water treatment as there are pharmaceutical products that are not causing micro-pollutants and 
not all medicines will meet the 2 tonnes/year criteria proposed by the Commission.  
 
The EPR  feasibility study14 that is cited in the impact assessment for the proposal, illustrates the influence of EPR 
on pharmaceutical prices based on the 12 most sold pharmaceutical substances that represent 80% of the sold 
volume of pharmaceuticals altogether. These molecules are paracetamol, metformin, lactulose, ibuprofen, 
phenoxymethylpenicillin, glucosamine, gabapentin, mesalazine, dicloxacillin, piperacillin and beta-lactamase 
inhibitor, acyclovir and amoxicillin.

Considering the sold volumes of pharmaceuticals suggested in the feasibility study and considering the total sales 
value in 2021 of these products15 , the average EPR contribution as a percentage of the total revenue per product 
would be 10% and, in some cases, it could go up to 138% (as in the case of phenoxymethylpenicillin – penicillin 
V). This clearly shows that with the current volume condition, the EPR contribution can be even higher than the 
actual revenue of some products. It is worthwhile noting that some of these products are essential medicines, 
such as antibiotics, that cannot be substituted with alternatives.  
 
This proposal will disproportionally affect the generic medicines industry as generic medicines are typically sold 
in higher volumes at a lower cost. It is unclear how the fee would be calculated for multisource products, and 
how it would disadvantage the producer supplying higher volumes on the market over the producer who does 
not meet the threshold of placing over 2T of product per year on the market. This could also negatively impact 
the supply of high-volume, low-cost products.  
 
If we analyse the weight of the medicines mentioned above, sold on all the EU markets, on average 1/3 of the 
manufacturers exceed the 2T threshold16 proposed in the feasibility study. This means that market leaders would 
be disproportionally affected and could create disincentive to supply the higher volumes.  Five medicines on the 
list are antibiotics, where we already notice a very fragile and consolidated market. There would be a high risk 
of worsening the current antibiotic shortage crisis that Europe is facing17. Therefore, essential medicines would 
be directly affected and the sustainability of the market would be threatened as patients would have a significant 
reduced access to these pharmaceuticals.

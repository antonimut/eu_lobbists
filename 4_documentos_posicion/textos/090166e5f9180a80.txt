Position of the Polish Union of the Cosmetics Industry  
on the proposal for a revised Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive  
(Commission’s proposal of 26 October 2022)1 
14th March 2023 
 
On 26 October 2022, the European Commission presented, within the framework of the Zero Pollution 
Action Plan policy, being a part the European Green Deal, the proposal for a revised Urban Wastewater 
Treatment Directive (UWWTD). The scope of the Directive is wide and it covers a number of horizontal 
issues. The position presented below refers only to the elements of the directive proposal that have  
a direct and significant impact on the cosmetics industry , namely the scope of the extended 
responsibility of the producer for micro-pollutants and the list of mictopollutants covered by the proposal. 
 
General remarks 
The proposal introduces a system of the extended producer responsibility (EPR), to finance the 
modernisation of the wastewater treatment plant. The purpose of the proposal is to ensure that micro -
pollutants are eliminated from wastewater that otherwise would be discharged to the water environment.  
The cosmetics industry is willing to cover the costs of urban wastewater treatment from pollu tants 
generated by this industry. Consequently, we support the principle of extended producer responsibility. 
However, the cosmetics and pharmaceuticals industries are indicated as the only sectors financing the 
EPR system. The Polish Union of the Cosmetic s Industry is in a position that the proposal is 
disproportionate and has the form of a tax, rather than ensuring the implementation of the “true polluter 
pays” principle. Indicating the two industries only as polluters has no substantive justification. The 
methodology and justification presented by the Commission represents significant shortcomings, and 
do not provide any justification for indicating the two industries as the only payers in the EPR system for 
micro-pollutants. Assumption that personal care  products are one of two main sources of micro -
pollutants, requires the cosmetics industry to incur significantly higher fees that arising from the actual 
share in the emissions released. The Union is of the position that this assumption is disproporitonate and 
not supported by the analyses of the micro-pollutants identified in the effluents from the exosting III-stage 
urban wastewater treatment. 
We call the European Commission to rewiew the proposed system of EPR . To gain the environmental 
goals, tt should cover all sources of the emissions of the micro-pollutants identified in the wastewater 
treatment systems, and not only cosmetics and medicinal products. It should be therefore based on the 
regulation of the substances but not the sectors . The EPR system for micro -pollutants should be 
developed in accordance with the “true polluter pays” principle , where costs of treatment of the 
environment are covered by all industries that are the source of micro -pollutants, proportionally to the 
pollutants they generate. There is no reason for two industries to cover the cost of all emissions of 
pollutants from all industries. 
 
Moreover, the directive proposal introduces a very broad definition of micro -pollutant that will cover 
more, than 190 000 of chemical substances, including substances that do not pose a risk to the 
environment and are not a problem from the perspective of wastewater treatment. It is neither 
reasonable nor feasible. We call for reconsidering the definition of micro-pollutant.

The definition of micro-pollutants should be precise, explicit and applied in a harmonised way across the 
EU. It should also address these substances that pose a risk to the environment and are not eliminated 
in the urban wastewater treatment processes. 
 
The directive proposal also introduces the definition of a producer, as a result of which the EPR fee for 
the same batch of substance will be collected many times in the value chain in different EU countries, 
even if the product an d substance are not released to wastewater. It must be clarified that the EPR 
contribution should only be paid in the country where the product that releases the identified micro -
pollutant into the wastewater is used/sold to the end user. 
 
Detailed comments 
The cosmetics industry is aware of the importance of the modernisation of wastewater treatment 
methods by investing in quaternary wastewater treatment. The industry understands that the financing 
should be provided through the EPR system, in accordance with the “true polluter pays” principle.  
 
EPR system – in line with the “true polluter pays” principle 
Each enterpreneur covered by the EPR system for micro -pollutants should be required to cover only 
those financial costs that result from the emiss ions of “true micro-pollutants” coming from its activities 
and products. The EPR system should not exceed the justified objectives of the directive, so it should 
cover only “true pollutants,” i.e. substances that are not currently effectively removed in wa stewater 
treatment plants and therefore require modernisation of wastewater treatment plants and treatment 
methods. 
The cosmetics and pharmaceutical industries are treated disproportionately in the proposal. These two 
industries are designated as the only source of micro-pollutants emissions into the environment. This 
approach is inconsistent with the actual knowledge and is not justified by any scientific data , including 
the reports of the Joint Research Center2. As a result, in the directive proposal, the EPR system covers 
only the entities that place on the market medicinal products for human use falling within the scope of 
Directive 2001/83/EC, and cosmetic products falling within the scope of Regulation No. 1223/2009/EC. 
In accordance with the extended producer responsibility principle, the producers of medicinal products 
and cosmetic products will cover the full costs of:  
• quaternary treatment of urban wastewater to remove micro -pollutants resulting from the 
products and their residues they place on the market, 
• monitoring of micro-pollutants, 
• gathering and verifying data on products placed on the market. 
The cosmetics industry accepts the application of the extended producer responsibility system to cover 
the full costs of polluting the environment with emissions from this industry, including waste, packaging 
waste, greenhouse gas emissions and other pollutants. However, in accordance with the “polluter pays” 
principle, the cosmetics industry should cover only th e costs of its own emissions, and not those 
generated by all other sectors. The “polluter pays” principle applies in all chemical regulations in the EU 
(e.g. REACH Regulation (1907/2006/EC), and each enterpreneur pays for the pollution it generates.

Likewise, the same principle is applied in the packaging waste management system, where the EPR 
system has been functioning in the EU for many years. If the objective of the directive is to improve the 
quality of urban wastewater treatment, adequate financing for the entire system should be ensured. At 
the same time, the financing mechanism will not be effective and sufficient if it covers only two industries.  
 
The cosmetics industry is ready to pay its fair share for financing the upgrading of the urban wastewater 
treatment plants (UWWTPs) through the EPR scheme. However, it has to be noted that cosmetics or 
pharma are very often not the only users of a substance, which can end up in wastewater from a variety 
of different sources. For example, for caffeine (which is used in few cosmetic products and in very low 
concentrations) cosmetics industry would have to cover the costs of the removal from urban wastewater, 
wheras cosmetic products are a negligible source, and most caffeine comes from the consumption of 
coffee and other beverages. What is more, different products can contain very different amounts of the 
same micro-pollutant, any cost allocation must be based on the substance (micro-pollutant), but notthe 
volume of product c ontaining it. The micro-pollutant is the substance but not a products, and specific 
substances could be present in the product in various concentrations. Therefore the amount of the 
product is not representative for the level of the substances emmission to the environment. 
 
Definition of micro-pollutants  
The Commission provided the following definition of the micro-pollutant:  
‘micro-pollutant’ means a substance, including its breakdown products, that is usually present in the 
environment and urban wastewaters in concentrations below milligrams per litre and which can be  
considered hazardous to human health or the environment based on any of the criteria set out in Part 3 
and Part 4 of Annex I to Regulation EC, which refers to the CLP regulation. 
This definition covers more, than 190 000 substances, as it includes all chemical substances classified 
as hazardous to health or the environment under the chemicals legislation (CLP), including substances 
with harmonized classification in annex VI of CLP and self-classified chemicals. EPR system based on 
such broad list of the substances would not be workable , as it is technically imposible to monitor and 
remove from the urban wastewater. Such big number of chemical substances in the EPR system does 
not reflect the environmental problem of the micro-pollutants identified in the wastewater. 
The group of substances classified as hazardous in accordance with CLP includes substances that do 
not have a harmonised classification, but have been categorised in such a way by one or more suppliers, 
based on the classification followed by other suppliers  (self-classification). For instance, if one of the 
suppliers categorises pure water as a corrosive substance 3, this would lead to an absurd situation in 
which it would be necessary to pay for minitoring and removal of water from urban wastewater.  
Moreover, the definition also covers many substances that, despite the harmonised classification under 
the CLP Regulation, are easily biodegradable and do not require modernisation of urban wastewater 
treatment plants. As an example, pure acetic acid 4 is classified with the chemical hazard of ‘Causes 
severe skin burns and eye damage’, but the same acetic acid when diluted – could be found in food 
(vinegar) or cosmetics and it is fully biodegradable and easily removed in the second stage of WWTP.

This broad approach by the Commission does not consider whether these substances are still present 
in the wastewater treatment plants ( WWTP) effluent5 after treatment. From the environmental point of 
view, only if a substance is still present in the wastewater treatment plants it would mean that the current 
3-stage treatments are not effective  in its removal, and it could pollute the environment when being 
released with the WWTP effluent back into water streams. This should be the aim of UWWTD revision – 
to address those substances which actually remain in WWTP after 3-stage treatments, not all classified 
and self-classified substances. Because despite their classification, many substances  (including most 
cosmetics ingredients) are effectively removed in existing secondary and tertiary wastewater treatments. 
In the opinion of the cosmetics industry, only the substances, the presence of which in wastewater 
requires the modernisation of the existing treatment plants and treatment processes, should be covered 
by the definition of micro-pollutants and the EPR system. 
Taking into account the proposed definition of micro -pollutant, the current requirements should be 
considered unfeasible. They would result in the need to monitor more, than 190 000 substances, for the 
majority of which developed analytical methods for environmental monitoring are missing, and which are 
not pollutants and/or pose no risk to the environment. At the same time, substances that are effectively 
removed in secondary and tertiary wastewater treatments, and are not present in wastewater 
discharged from the treatment plant should not be covered by the definition of micro -pollutants and 
should not be covered by the EPR system.  
List of micro-pollutants – Joint Research Centre’s approach 
To ensure a harmonised application and enforcement of the EPR scheme across the EU internal market 
there is a need for legal certainty and clarity over the substances in  the scope of the EPR. The Joint 
Research Centre (JRC) study 2 from November 2022, used by the European Commission in it s own 
Impact Assessment for the proposal, provides a better suited basis for a harmonised implementation of 
the EPR, based on a clear methodology for identification of potential micro-pollutants. The JRC list is 
based on a clearly identifiable list of substances, based on the problem they actually cause in wastewater 
and WWTPs . Depending on the criteria for choice of the substances, the exact list and n umber of 
substances may be differ. The JRC preliminary list includes 1,337 “total pollution proxy substances” 
(TPPS).  
This list could be further discussed, however, it is a first indication of the chemicals commonly found in 
wastewater effluents from current 3 -stage treatment process. Therefore, these substances trigger the 
need for implementation of a fourth treatment stage. Out of the preliminary JRC list, 114 substances are 
also listed in COSING 3 as potential cosmetic ingredients . This list includes a number of potential 
cosmetic ingredients with the ones actually used by the industry being mainly UV filters and 
preservatives. The cosmetics industry is ready to pay its fair share for financing  the upgrading of the 
UWWTPs through the EPR scheme. 
It is important that EPR system should be based on regulation of the substances and should not regulate 
sectors (it should be „sector agnostic”). Each company should pay a share of the total cost that is 
calculated from its proportional contribution to this overall micro-pollutant load, in accordance with the 
“true polluter pays” principle.

This principle says that costs of treatment of the environment should be covered by all industries that 
are the source of particular micro-pollutants, proportionally to the amount they generate. Such fair and 
proportionate cost sharing has been successfully implemented in other EPR schemes , such as for 
packaging.  
Other definitions 
Additionally, considering the present definitions of “producer” and “placing on the market” fees due to 
EPR will have to be paid many times in the supply chain for the same product, each time the product is 
moved inside the Community to another EU country, even if it is not placed on the market and used, and 
thus not released into wastewater. This issue seems to be a formal mistake, but it should be clarified as 
soon as possible and the provisions of the directive adjusted accordingly. 
 
Summary of the position 
The presented legislative proposal results in groundless discrimination against the cosmetics industry 
and is not justified by any scientific facts and the structure of micro-pollutant emissions into wastewater.  
The extended producer responsibility system for micro-pollutants should be applied in accordance with 
the “true polluter pays” principle, proportionally to the emission of substances into the environment 
generated by the industry. To ensure harmonised application and enforcement of EPR schemes across 
the EU internal market there is a need for legal certainty and clarity over the substances in the scope of 
the EPR. Such list should cover the sub stances which are found in the effluents of WWTP operating 
according to third stage technology and therefore trigger the need for a fourth treatment stage.  
The cosmetics industry accepts the application of the extended producer responsibility system to cover 
the full costs of polluting the environment with emissions from this industry. But it seems to be unfair to 
cover the costs of all industries in the EU by only two sectors, including cosmetics sector. 
The EPR system should clearly identify the list of substances in the scope of the EPR system. This list 
should be based on the existing data on the micro-pollutants identified in the wastewater, according to 
methodology applied by the JRC. Such list should be developed as an annex to the directive and should 
be updated accordint to the state-of-the-art.

develops and implements solutions that lead to the development of the Polish cosmetics market, which is now fifth 
in the European Union.

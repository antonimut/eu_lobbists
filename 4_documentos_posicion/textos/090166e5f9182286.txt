On 30 November, the European Commission presented a proposal for a regulation to revise the 
European legislative framework on packaging and packaging waste. This proposal raises a lot of concern 
among the economic actors involved.  
 
Firstly, the choice of a Regulation rather than a Directive  leaves little room for Member States to 
define the models and instruments to be adopted to achieve the objectives. A choice that, paradoxically, 
risks penalizing the most virtuous countries in terms of packaging management. 
 
Secondly, concerns are also high on content. In fact, without a thorough impact assessment, the draft 
regulation envisages the imposition of certain restrictions and bans on certain forms of packaging, the 
setting of design criteria and the imposition of binding deposit and return systems. 
 
The intention is to push as much as possible towards prevention and reuse, an objective that we could 
agree with in general terms. However, we should pursue it with models consistent with the 
characteristics and needs of the business system operating in the packaging chain.  
 
Moreover, prevention and reuse cannot be the only op tion. On the contrary, it must be added, albeit 
within the waste management hierarchy, to the positive recycling models  that have developed in 
recent years, also driven by the previous regulatory framework. 
 
These are very impactful measures for companies that produce packaging and many sectors that use it. 
In light of the above, we present our comments in the context of the discussions within the European 
Parliament and the Council. 
 
The proposed regulation raises strong concerns among companies operating in the retail, wholesale, 
tourism, catering, entertainment, and service sectors. 
 
Confcommercio has always supported the proactive role played by the European Union in orienting 
Member States' policies towards ecological transition objectives. These policies, so far, have resulted in 
the determination of general objectives and principles to which Member States have had to adapt, but 
with wide margins of discretion as to the adoption of the specific measures deemed necessary to achieve 
these objectives. This approach has led to the definition, in Member States, of a regulatory framework 
that is functional to the specificities of each country's economic, productive, and social situations.  
 
Instead, the proposal for a regulation threatens a risky reversal of the trend, which risks penalizing the 
countries that have already made significant efforts in recent years to adopt models capable of 
guaranteeing effective management of packaging waste and fostering the achievement of recycling 
objectives. 
 
In particular, the proposal focuses on the reduction and elimination of packaging, the valorization of 
loose products, on containers to reuse through the binding and restrictive introduction of systems of 
deposit. These aspects are not necessarily mor e effective and sustainable than the national models 
already in operation, as the Italian one, which has led to excellent results in achieving the separate 
collection targets set at the EU level.  
 
Generally speaking, this proposal risks penalizing the most virtuous realities at the European level, 
such as Italy, which is the first among the largest Member States in terms of per capita recycling of 
packaging materials and waste recovery. 
 
Among the targets set by the directives contained in the circular eco nomy package adopted at the 
European level is that of recycling 65% of packaging waste by 2025 and 70% by 2030, with different 
targets per material, as illustrated in the table:

responsibility of EPRs for achieving correct management objectives. It represents an excellence in the 
European panorama. Primacy is confirmed both by recent Eurostat data on recycling and recovery of

Moreover, the European Paper Packaging Alliance (Eppa) commissioned a Life Cycle Analysis (LCA) 
study of single-use paper-based packaging - a renewable, certified, and fully recyclable raw material that 
is recycled - which shows that switching to reusable alternatives would generate 48% more CO2 
emissions, consume 39% more potable water and require 82% more mineral and resource extraction.  
 
Analysis of the results shows that this is due to the return transport of packaging after its use and 
washing and drying, which consumes more energy, water, and resources than the production and use 
of single-use paper packaging. 
 
Moreover, selling loose food and banning single-use products to encourage reusable packaging is 
contrary to food and consumer health protection.  
 
Furthermore, food packaging is a crucial element in protecting the integrity of the food,  resulting in 
the reduction of waste of contained products.  
 
The proposal for a regulation is ideological. Among the restrictions and constraints that are likely to do 
great harm, we highlight  the restrictions on the use of certain packaging formats . With this 
approach, the damage would fall, for example, on the food chain as food packaging is fundamental for 
the protection and preservation of food, consumer information, traceability and hygien e of products, 
also enabling their marketing and export. 
 
 4 
Another highly critical element concerns the implementation of deposit and return systems . That 
risks harming organized distribution in all its articulations, restauran ts, public -related activities , 
hospitality businesses, and other sectors such as food retail. These businesses' structures cannot handle 
the required compliance as they do not have sufficient space to accommodate the packaging returned 
by consumers and staff to manage the flows generated by such operations.  
 
In addition, data on market dynamics should be the base of the shift toward systems to promote reuse. 
Indeed, such a systemic change requires the evolution of business models. That starts with technological 
innovations to introduce at the level of plant transformation, changes in the supply cha in, presence of 
adequate on -premises infrastructure (including packaging waste collection/separation systems), 
efficient cleaning facilities, and storage capacities in hospitality businesses, with due regard to local and 
national specificities.  
 
According to recent estimates compiled by the Italian National Packaging Consortium (Conai), the  
proposal would negatively impact more than 300,000 industrial and artisanal producers and users and 
more than 400,000 trade, tourism, restaurant and entertainment, and  service businesses  that 
collectively go on to generate a turnover of more than 1.8 trillion with an employment force of more 
than 7 million workers. 
 
Furthermore, the proposal lacks a scientific basis when it directs the market toward technological or 
production choices. Lacking, then, are timely references to LCA (Life Cycle Assessment) that give 
evidence of the economic, social, and environmental impacts resulting from the application of the new 
EU framework. Instead, the impact on businesses should be c onsidered through rigorous cost-benefit 
analysis and life -cycle assessments . In addition, support measures, financial and regulatory 
incentives, and adequate transition periods should counterbalance negative externalities.  
 
This regulatory approach generates uncertainty about what packaging may eventually be released and 
what may be banned. That will disincentivize businesses from investment, undermining overall growth 
objectives, with possible waste of resources and job losses. 
 
Thus, the proposal risks h arming the entire packaging production and management chain  
throughout its lifecycle.  
In light of these considerations, Confcommercio believes that a review of the overall structure of the text 
under discussion at the European level is necessary.

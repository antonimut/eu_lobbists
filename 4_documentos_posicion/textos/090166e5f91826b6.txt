Response to the public consultation of  
the Revision of the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive 
 
 
The Danish Association of Cosmetics and Detergents welcomes the opportunity to comment on the public 
consultation of the Revision of the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive. 
The Danish Association of Cosmetics and Detergents is the leading Danish industry association and 
represents more than 80 Danish and international companies, which predominantly consists of SMEs. The 
proposed changes to the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive will impose an additional financial burden 
to companies manufacturing and selling cosmetic products. Therefore, it is essential that the new 
requirements will be implemented transparently. 
 
Overall, we support the European Commission in their strategic focus to ensure that we protect our 
environment. However, after having read the proposal, we have some concerns about how the different 
initiatives will be implemented in practice so that the EPR of the cosmetic industry rests on the right terms 
and conditions and that the cosmetic industry is held responsible only for its own discharge of 
micropollutants.  
 
It is therefore essential that the financial model for the EPR-fee scheme is proportional to the contribution to 
the overall micropollutant load originating from the cosmetic industry. Introducing the “polluter pays 
principle” would mean, that the companies should only pay for micropollutants that are not sufficiently 
removed in wastewater treatment plants and therefore trigger the need for upgrading from third to fourth 
treatment stage. Thus the scheme must only include micropollutants, that require a fourth treatment stage 
and not substances removed by the third treatment stage or can be proven biodegradable.  
 
The main concerns and comments to the revision are listed below.  
 
The definition of micropollutant is too generic and broad.  
In article 2 (16), the European Commission defines a micropollutant as “a substance, including its breakdown 
products, that is usually present in the environment and urban wastewaters in concentrations below 
milligrams per liter and which can be considered hazardous to human health, or the environment based on 
any of the criteria set out in Part 3 and Part 4 of Annex I to Regulation EC” which refers to the CLP regulation.  
• Applying the definition above, any substance with a harmonized classification or a self-applied 
classification as hazardous to human health or the environment will be considered as a 
micropollutant. The purpose and focus of hazard classification under CLP is to allow their safe 
transport, handling and use in their pure form or in mixtures, while protecting the user and the 
environment. Therefore, the classification of a substance is not necessarily relevant in terms of the 
substance’s behavior in wastewater treatment plants and whether it requires a quaternary 
treatment 
• The definition of a micropollutant is too broad and will therefore automatically include many 
substances that do not actually pose a problem in current wastewater treatment systems. It is 
essential that the EPR-fee is consistent with the amount of micropollutants the individual company 
discharges, and which also trigger the need for a fourth treatment stage 
• Clear definitions and an unambiguous identification of micropollutants (in an Annex to the Directive) 
must ensure that the EPR can be applied in a harmonized manner across all Member States and 
across distribution chains at Member State level. Therefore, a defined list of micropollutants needs 
to be included in the Directive.

• The CLP-regulation requires companies to self-classify all the substances that do not have a 
harmonized classification. By applying a definition based on a self-applied CLP-classification, there is 
a risk that different manufacturers and suppliers can provide the same substance, but with different 
hazard-classification which in turn leads to a disharmony in the market 
The definition of ”placing on the market” is incompatible with the freedom of movement for goods.  
In article 2 (24) the European Commission defines placing on the market as “‘placing on the market’ means 
the first making available of a product on the market of a Member State”. In article 2(18) a producer is 
defined as “‘producer’ means any manufacturer, importer, or distributor that on a professional basis places 
products on the market of a Member State, including by means of distance contracts as defined in Article 2(7) 
of Directive 2011/83/EU means;”. This means, that an EPR-fee for the same product will be charged several 
times if different operators in the distribution chain located in different EU member states. 
 
The approach taken in this proposal is not aligned with the single market by allowing for repeated EPR 
charges every time a product is placed on the market in a different Member State. The proposed definition 
of placing on the market is inconsistent with the European Commission Blue Guide on the implementation of 
EU products rules and therefore with existing EU legislations in which placing on the market is defined as the 
first making available of a product on the Union market 
 
It is also important, that online market platforms selling to EU fulfill the same requirements as European 
companies regarding being charged the EPR-fee 
 
The contribution should be based on the dry weight of the substances 
The EPR contributions of companies should be based on the amount of dry weight micropollutants they 
release and not the amount of the product  
• The EPR contribution must be calculated based on the quantity of micropollutant. For example, if we 
have two products that both weigh 1 kg. Product A contains 0.1% of a micropollutant and therefore 
releases 1 g per kilo product. Product B contains 1.2% of the same micropollutant and therefore 
releases 12 g per kilo product. So even though both product A and B have the same weight, product 
B releases 12 times as much of the micropollutant compared to product A  
Finally, it is important to ensure a harmonization of the MS implementation of the proposal, in particular the 
implementation of EPR. It is crucial that member states do not introduce other or additional requirements 
than those presented in the directive.

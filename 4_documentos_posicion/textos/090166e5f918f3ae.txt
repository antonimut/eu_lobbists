 
 
 
 
Statement on the proposal for a directive on ambient air quality and clean air for Europe Deutsche Umwelthilfe e.V. 
Statement by Deutsche Umwelthilfe e.V. on the proposal for a directive of 
the European Parliament and of the Council on ambient air quality and 
cleaner air for Europe 
 
A. Pollutants and limit values  
 
A.1. Limit values are too weak and come too late 
DUH welcomes the fact that the EU Commission intends to tighten the limit values for air pollutants in 
ambient air, thus recognizing that the current standards are insufficient and do not adequately protect 
the health of citizens. Likewise, it is to be supported that the revised recommendations for air quality limit 
values of the World Health Organisation (WHO) are explicitly named as a benchmark for clean air and are 
used as comparative values. However, it is incomprehensible why the Commission has introdu ced air 
quality limit values into the legislative process that are significantly weaker than the recommendations of 
the WHO. For the pollutant nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and for fine particulate matter PM2.5, the annual mean 
limit values proposed by the Commission are twice as high as recommended by the WHO, and for partic-
ulate matter PM10 they are one third above the WHO guideline value.  
It is positive that the Commission takes into account the danger of short -term exposure to air pollutants 
through partly ne w or tightened daily mean  limit values. Unfortunately, the recommendations of the 
WHO are not adopted here either and thus the necessary protect ion is not sufficiently granted . This is 
especially relevant with regard to seasonal sources such as wood combustion/household heating, whose 
air pollution is particularly visible in the number of days on which the limit values are exceeded. While the 
WHO considers an exceedance of the proposed daily mean limit values on only 3-4 days per calendar year 
to be acceptable, the Commission generously grants 18 permitted exceedance days in combination with 
weaker limit values for NO2, PM2.5 as well as PM10. The combination of weaker limit values and a higher 
number of permitted exceedance days leads to a significantly weak er level of protection, which we illus-
trate with an example below:  
At the official monitoring station Friedberger Landstraße in Frankfurt am Main in Germany, the 24 -hour 
limit value for NO 2 of 50 µg/m³ proposed by the Commission was exceeded on 15 days in  2021. With 18 
permitted days of exceedance, the air quality at this traffic hotspot already meets the legal requirements 
planned for 2030. Further improvements in air quality would not have to be achieved. However, the 24 -
hour guideline value of 25 µg/m³ recommended by the WHO was exceeded on 248 days in 2021, although 
only 3 days of exceedance are assumed to be tolerable by the WHO. The number of days with demonstra-
bly harmful air quality is thus 82 times higher (!) than recommended by the WHO, without th ere being 
any need for action under the proposed directive. 
An implementation of the Commission's proposal would thus lead to stricter limit values, but an end to 
the significant exceedance of the WHO guideline values relevant for health protection would s till not be 
specified and foreseeable.  
Based on the current status of air quality and the health burdens in the EU determined by the European 
Environment Agency, exceeding the WHO guideline values leads to 238,000 premature deaths due to 
exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and 49,000 due to NO 2 concentrations above the WHO rec-
ommendations. Another 24,000 premature deaths are due to ozone concentrations above 20  µg/m³ as 
an annual average. These premature deaths and immense societal costs due to diseases caused by air

pollutants could be avoided if the WHO guideline values were adhered to. This must be the standard for 
setting limit values in Europe.  
The fact that, according to the draft law, the limit values are only to apply from 2030 is clearl y too late. 
This will result in a de facto moratorium on action for air pollution control measures in the next seven 
years in many member states that now comply with the currently valid limit values. Without strict require-
ments by the European Union, no voluntary progress by the member states can be expected. We call for 
a much earlier implementation of the new limit values by 2025 at the latest. Due to the current legislative 
process, the member states are sufficiently prepared to initiate appropriate meas ures for implementa-
tion.  
Furthermore, it is to be welcomed that the target values of the existing directive are all (with the exception 
of ozone) to be converted into legally binding limit values. However, this is only a first step and cannot 
hide the fact, that stricter limit values are also needed here. For example, an annual average limit value 
of 1.0 ng/m3 for benzo(a)pyrene was included in the draft. For the protection of human health, however, 
the European Environment Agency states a guideline value of 0.12 ng/m 3 in its publications, i.e. almost 
one tenth of what the Commission has now proposed. This clearly shows the discrepancy between sci-
ence-based recommendations and political limit values. We demand that the limit values be adjusted 
according to scientific findings and thus in the interest of people and the environment:  
» The current air quality limits must be lowered to the WHO recommendations and implemented and made 
legally binding as soon as possible, by 2025 at the latest.  
» For particulate matter PM2,5 an annual mean value of 5 µg/m3 and a daily mean value of 15 µg/m3, 
which may not be exceeded on more than 3 days per calendar year.   
» For particulate matter PM10 an annual mean value of 15 µg/m3 and a daily mean value of 45 µg/m3, 
which may not be exceeded on more than 3 days per calendar year.   
» For nitrogen dioxide NO2 an annual mean value of 10 µg/m3 and a daily mean value of 25 µg/m3, which 
may not be exceeded on more than 3 days per calendar year.   
» The target values previously contained in the Air Quality Directive must be adopted as ambitious and bind-
ing limit values. For benzo(a)pyrene, the current target value of 1.0 ng/m3 must be significantly tightened 
and adapted as a limit value of 0.12 ng/m3 (recommended guideline value cited by the European Environ-
ment Agency).  
 
A.2. Too many exceptions  
Although the limit values for air pollutants and the target value for ozone are mandatory under Art. 13 for 
all member states from 2030, Art. 18 offers the possibility to extend the deadline for compliance with 
certain limit values. Due to unfavourable conditions or transboundary pollution, it is possible for member 
states to obtain a deferral of up to five years for compliance with the limit values. However, the conditions 
are not precisely defined, which will lead to different interpretations and thus probably also to legal dis-
putes. Experience with the implementation of the current Air Quality Directive 2008/50/EC has shown 
that possibilities to extend the deadline are actually used by the member states. Thus, Art. 18 introduces 
a loophole for postponing compliance with limit values and thus a de facto postponement of air quality 
improvement in Europe until 2035.  
Article 16 of the Commission's draft also offers the risk of undermining the limit values set out in Annex I, 
Section 1. Article 16 regulates that exceedances of the limit values that are causally attributable to natural 
sources of air pollutants are not to be considered as limit value exceedances within the meaning of th e 
Air Quality Directive. This means that air pollutant concentrations proven harmful to health  will remain 
legally untouched. Especially since the Commission wants to anchor limit values above the WHO guideline 
values, such an extensive exemption is neither comprehensible nor justifiable. Even people who live in

» In their current form, Articles 16 and 18 offer a high potential for abuse and are likely to under-

only permitted if exclusively or predominantly natural sources lead to a limit value being ex-
ceeded. The ridge extensions provided for in Article 18 must be deleted altogether.  
 
A.3. Insufficient new regulations for further pollutants (UFP, black carbon and ammonia)  
As the Commission's draft states, limit values are an important prerequisite for the reduction of air pollu-
tant concentrations. It is therefore incomprehensible why air pollutants that have long been known and 
proven to be harmful to health, such as black carbon, ultra -fine particles or ammonia as a precurs or of 
fine particulate matter, are still not considered with corresponding limit values in the draft revision of the 
Ambient Air Quality Directive.  
The inclusion of relevant pollutants such as black carbon and ultrafine particles in the directive is an im-
portant step. Unfortunately, the specifications do not go beyond insufficient monitoring requirements. 
There is still a lack of a legal limit on pollutant concentrations in the air.  
» All relevant air pollutants must be included in the directive. In particular, limit values and extended monitor-
ing requirements for the pollutants black carbon, ultrafine particles and ammonia are indispensable for effec-
tive air quality improvement. 
 
B. Monitoring / surveillance 
 
B.1. Supersites 
It is a step forward that ammonia, black carbon and ultrafine particles have been made mandatory in the 
measurement specifications, but only at the so-called supersites. The concept of supersites is a good idea 
in itself, as it provides for mandatory, comparable, standardized measurements of the  most important 
pollutants (NO2, PM10, PM2.5, O3, BC, NH3 and UFP) for all member states. However, the concrete im-
plementation specifications are also deficient here. Contrary to the Commission draft, the supersites 
measurements should not only concentrate on urban and rural background pollution, but should also be 
carried out at hotspots with particularly high pollution from industry, wood combustion or traffic. The aim 
of the supersites  is to establish additional sampling points for unregulated air pollut ants that are of in-
creasing concern. In order not to produce a glossed -over picture of the pollution with ammonia, black 
carbon and ultrafine particles, their concentration must therefore also be determined at hotspots. 
Especially with regard to ultrafine particles, which show a strongly fluctuating spatial distribution, the 
concept of a few supersites does not seem well thought -out. The various sources of ultrafine particles 
(traffic, industry and wood combustion) cause UFP emissions at very different locations. A few supersites 
de not ensure adequate coverage of the UFP load. 
The absolute number of measuring points for supersites is insufficient. For example, only one supersite 
per 10 million inhabitants is to be obligatory for the urban background; for the rural background, only one 
monitoring station per 100,000 km2 is necessary. For Germany, the largest EU member state in terms of 
area and population, this would mean that only 8 monitoring stations would be obligatory for the urban 
background and 3-4 for the rural background. It is obvious that this would not be sufficient to assess the 
ammonia or black carbon pollution of more than 80 million people.

B.2. Modelling  
The inclusion of modelling in the assessment of air quality makes sense in order to be able to assess the distribu-
tion of air pollutant concentrations in addition to the local assessment through measurement results. However, it 
is not sufficient to make modelling mandatory only in case the newly defined, far too weak limit values for air pol-
lutants have already been exceeded. Modelling should already become a mandatory part of air quality monitoring 
in advance when the assessment thresholds defined in Annex II are exceeded, in order to ensure an assessment 
of the actual air pollution.  
In addition to the necessity of obligatory macroscale, area-wide modelling, prior modelling is required especially 
for the determination of measuring points at hotspots. A screening modelling of the entire urban main road net-
work should be obligatory, especially for the determination of monitoring stations close to traffic. This is the only 
way to ensure that the selected sampling points reliably cover the highest loads. Therefore, screening modelling 
along the main road network for the most important traffic-related air pollutants – as has been the state of the 
art for many years – should become a binding requirement. 
Modelling is also helpful and useful for evaluating the effectiveness of measures taken in the context of air quality 
plans. The use of modelling as an assessment method makes sense, regardless of whether air pollutant limit val-
ues are exceeded, in order to enable a fully comprehensive picture of air quality in Europe.  
Many indicative measurements, for example in the context of citizen science projects, and modelling, for example 
in the context of infrastructure planning, have revealed limit value exceedances at particularly highly polluted lo-
cations that have not been covered by official monitoring stations until now. The directive should contain an obli-
gation for the authorities to follow up on these findings with official measurements. Furthermore, it would make 
sense for the EU Commission to provide a list of measuring methods and instruments that can be used in addition 
to the official monitoring stations in the context of citizen science and by local authorities. This list should be up-
dated regularly. 
  
B.3. Monitoring stations/ number of sampling points  
The increase in the minimum number of sampling points for fixed measurements of particulate matter is a step in 
the right direction, but only affects assessment zones with a small population. The specified minimum number of 
PM10 and PM2.5 monitoring stations is to be welcomed, as it obliges the systematic and comprehensive monitor-
ing of PM2.5 pollution. The obligation to cover certain components of fine particulate matter by a minimum num-
ber of monitoring stations is equally sensible. However, the minimum number of sampling points for stationary 
measurements generally remains significantly too low.  
Furthermore, it is wrong to maintain that mandatory fixed monitoring stations are to be set up only if assessment 
values are exceeded. Only through extensive obligations to model air quality and the general obligation to take 
measurements independent of defined assessment thresholds a comprehensive picture of pollution can be estab-
lished. Article 9, paragraph 3 on the possible reduction of the minimum number of measuring points by 50 per-
cent under certain conditions should therefore be deleted. The minimum requirements for measuring points 
should not be reduced.  
Extending the monitoring of ultrafine particles beyond supersites is particularly important to provide a better 
data basis for UFP pollution. However, the weak minimum requirements (one measurement point per 5 million 
inhabitants) and the very imprecise definition of locations where high UFP concentrations are likely to occur (air-
ports, ports, roads and certain spots of wood combustion are mentioned) open a wide door for arbitrary compli-
ance with these requirements without actually checking the main locations with high UFP pollution. In Germany 
alone there are 38 airports, but according to the minimum requirement based on the number of inhabitants, only 
16 UFP measuring points are prescribed, along with the supersites in the background. The number of UFP meas-
urements is therefore not nearly sufficient to obtain an overview of the concentration at highly polluted sites. 
» The requirements for monitoring air pollution must be expanded. More measurements of all relevant air 
pollutants in different pollution environments (background pollution in rural and urban areas, traffic-related 
pollution, industry and residential areas with high amount of solid fuel combustion) as well as supplementing

B.4. Measurement of mass and composition of PM2.5  
In particular, for further understanding of the effect and actual exposure to particulate matter, the mandatory 
requirement to also measure mass and composition of PM2.5 is indispensable. The current specifications for de-
termining the composition of PM2.5 may have limited suitability with regard to source attribution, which is neces-
sary for targeted measures. In the case of wood combustion, for example, levoglucosan would have to be ana-
lysed as a tracer in order to determine the contribution of this source to the PM2.5 pollution.  
 
B.5. Assessment units / NUTS1 
The draft of the new directive provides under Article 6 that the member states themselves determine the zones in 
their territories in which air quality is to be assessed. How this is to be done and on the basis of which criteria is 
not specified.  
The situation is different for the determination of assessment areas for the control of average exposure. In the 
new directive, NUTS level 1 is used as the assessment basis for all average exposure targets. NUTS level 1 corre-
sponds to the federal states in Germany according to Regulation (EC) No 1059/2003. This assessment unit for 
monitoring station specifications and target exceedances is extremely unsuitable because the federal states have 
very different sizes, both in terms of area and population. For example, a city state like Berlin with about 4000 
inhabitants per km2 is difficult to compare with a territorial state like Bavaria with about 185 inhabitants per km2. 
This disparity is not only present within Member States in the basis of assessment, but of course also in the com-
parison of exposure calculations between Member States.  
 
C. Average Exposure Index / Average Exposure Reduction Obligation 
 
The Commission's draft envisages a central role for the Average Exposure Index (AEI). It makes sense to 
anchor a reduction target in the Ambient Air Quality Directive in addition to the limit values in order to 
encourage member states to make continuous ef forts in air quality. In this way, the responsible authori-
ties are obliged to further improve air quality even if the limit values are complied with, and the achieve-
ment of the WHO limit value recommendations is at least anchored as a goal in the directive. Despite the 
positive intention, the AEI in this regulation has only little benefit for the reduction of air pollutants and 
is not suitable for achieving the stated goal.  
The AEI serves as the basis for a reduction path, the Average Exposure Reduction Ob ligation. This stipu-
lates that from 2030 onwards, it must be demonstrated that the AEI value has been reduced by 25 per 
cent within a ten -year period. The exposure in 2020 would thus be the reference value, regardless of 
whether the existing air quality li mit values are met or not. Areas where pollution levels are very high 
must therefore continue to meet lower air quality standards in the future. Since only a relative reduction 
is defined, but the baseline is not linked to limit values and can be very high according to the actual expo-
sure, it is not regulated when the WHO recommendation is to be fulfilled. 
Irrespective of the fact that the reduction targets do not apply as soon as possible, but only from 2030, 
the AEI only refers to the average exposure of all background monitoring stations in a certain assessment 
area. The index is therefore not suitable for making a statement about the exposure of people at specific 
places of residence and stay. At these locations, the exposure of people in the same assessment area can

be significantly higher and even increase locally. However, in order to provide sufficient health protection, 
the WHO limit value recommendations must be complied with at all locations in the EU. An average value 
over large assessment areas is unsuitable for this assessment.  
Although the target values of the Average Exposure Reduction Obligation correspond to the WHO recom-
mendations for the annual average of PM2.5 (5 µg/m 3) and NO 2 (10 µg/m3), they are not to be met at 
each individual sampling point and not within a specific time. This means that significantly higher pollution 
levels will continue to be permissible, especially at hotspots and even in urban or rural background. As a 
result, pollution levels above the WHO recommendations must be accepted also in the long term. Beyond 
that, a further reduction below the WHO guideline values is not envisaged  although there is no known 
threshold for the health effects of air pollutant concentrations. Negative health effects have also been 
proven below the WHO guideline values, which is why the Commission should demand further improve-
ments of air quality – in the sense of the European Commission's zero-pollution ambition.  
The instrument of the reduction path based on the AEI also motivates to impleme nt as few preventive 
measures as possible before the reduction targets for air quality improvement enters into force. The 
higher the AEI in the reference year, the less ambitious the target. Assuming that the limit values for 
PM2.5 and NO2 are met in 2030, pollution will still be above the WHO recommendation in 2050. Compli-
ance with the WHO guideline values is therefore not mandatory until 2060. If the limit values are exceeded 
by 5 µg/m³ each in 2030, a member state is rewarded and is not obliged to comply with the WHO guideline 
values until 2070.  
The last point of criticism is that the AEI only applies to the annual mean values of NO2 and PM2.5. Other 
pollutants are not included. In summary, it can be said that although the idea of the red uction path is 
comprehensible, the planned implementation of the instrument is not suitable  for meeting the stated 
goal of zero pollutions. Under the following conditions, the reduction obligation could have a better ef-
fect: 
» Inclusion of hotspots in the reduction obligation, not only consideration of the background load 
» Reduction obligation for every single sampling point, not an average exposure 
» No reward for exceeding limit values: In the case of limit value exceedances, the reduction target 
should relate to the limit value. 
» Reduction obligation also below the WHO guideline values 
» Specify a deadline in which the air quality level of the WHO guideline values must be achieved. 
 
D. Enforcement of air quality targets / air quality plans  
 
D.1. Air quality plans  
The requirements in Article 19 on air quality plans are much more concrete than before and contain, concrete 
requirements as to when air quality plans must be submitted. It is also specified that the period of exceedance 
must be kept as short as possible, in any case below three years. If this is not achieved, however, the Commis-
sion's draft does not provide for any consequences, except that the air quality plan must be updated and supple-
mented with further measures if the limit values are still not met three years later due to the measures. Clear 
sanction options are needed here. 
The information specified in Annex VIII, which is to be taken into account in air quality plans, is welcome. Above 
all, the analysis of the effect of the measures to achieve the limit values within 3 years, the necessary considera-
tion of the expected quantified concentration reduction and the estimation of the year in which the limit values 
can probably be complied with are necessary components.

D.2. Legal claims / enforcement  
» The entry into force of the new air quality limit values must be brought forward at the European level from 
2030, as currently envisaged, to 2025. At the same time, consequences must be drawn from the previous 
problems of non-compliance in the EU member states over many years. Additional controls, the need for the 
early development of binding proposals for measures and evaluations must be recognised and their binding 
stipulation must take place. Compliance with the limit values from the first day of validity must be guaran-
teed.  
» The enforceability and implementation of the legal requirements must be ensured. The Commission's moni-
toring and sanctioning possibilities in the event of non-compliance with the requirements must be sharpened 
and, above all, enable the rapid initiation and implementation of sanctions. It must be avoided that another 
decade can pass before corresponding infringement procedures and financial sanctions take effect.  
» In addition, civil society's access to legal enforcement must be guaranteed throughout Europe (in accord-
ance with the standards of the Aarhus Convention) in order to enable control of those responsible at national 
level. This includes the right of access to environmental information, participation in decision-making pro-
cesses and access to justice to enforce the right to a healthy environment.  
» For the implementation of the new air quality standards, the Ambient Air Quality Directive must define 
measures in all relevant sectors (agriculture, wood combustion, transport and energy/industry) that con-
tribute to the efficient reduction of air pollutants. The framework conditions for the implementation of 
these measures must be created. The Commission should support the implementation of measures from all 
relevant sectors. In addition to technological solutions, measures that promote behavioral change must also 
be included. The highest priority must be given to preventing emissions at the source. The best protection 
against air pollutants is to avoid their generation.    
» All complementary regulations at European and, if necessary, pre-compliant at national level that contrib-
ute to emission reduction and air pollution control must be aligned according to the WHO limit value recom-
mendations. These include the directive on National Emission Ceilings for certain air pollutants (NEC di-
rective), emission limits in the ecodesign regulations for local space heaters and solid fuel boilers, the EURO 7 
exhaust emission standard and many more.  
» In the event of implementation of the WHO values later than 2025, the Commission must encourage the 
member states to make use of the possibility of early compliance and implementation of stricter envi-
ronmental and health requirements at EU member state level, as affirmed by the ECJ.

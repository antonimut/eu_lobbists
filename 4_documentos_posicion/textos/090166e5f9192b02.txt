EBF POSITION ON THE DIRECTIVE HARMONIZING CERTAIN 
ASPECTS OF INSOLVENCY LAW  
 
 
Introduction: 
On 7.12.2022 the European Commission put forward a proposal for a Directive 
harmonising certain aspects of insolvency law COM (2022) 702  (the “Proposal”) . The 
initiative is part of the Commission´s efforts to advance the Capital Market Union. The 
Commission believes that the lack of harmonised insolvency regimes is one of the key 
obstacles to greater integration of the EU´s capital markets and that efficie nt and better 
aligned insolvency rules in the EU can also increase the absorptive capacity of the economy 
to manage sizeable economic shocks and distress such as the energy crisis.  
 
While the 2019 Directive focuses on pre -insolvency proceeding, t he proposed Directive 
seeks to harmonise certain key areas of insolvency law and aims to maximise the recovery 
of assets from the liquidated insolvency estate (s. below A) -D)), the efficiency of 
proceedings ((E), (G)) and the fair distribution of recovered  value amongst creditors (F). 
It lays down common rules on: 
 
I. avoidance actions; 
II. the tracing of assets belonging to the insolvency estate through improved 
access to asset registers; 
III. pre-pack proceedings; 
IV. the duty of directors to submit a request for the ope ning of insolvency 
proceedings; 
V. simplified winding-up proceedings for microenterprises; 
VI. creditors’ committees; 
VII. the drawing-up of a key information factsheet by Member States to enhance 
transparency for creditors / investors. 
 
It is questionable if the prop osal can really achieve its aim of promoting capital markets 
or at least improve recovery rates. A direct impact of the harmonization efforts on the 
investment decisions of investors cannot be proved right by accepted evidence or 
investigations and in some  respects even seems unlikely (e.g. simplified winding -up 
proceedings for microenterprises). 
 
In particular, the below concerns are raised by the proposal and need to be addressed: 
➢ A clarification of the position of secured creditors. Any undermining of their position 
(due to increased uncertainty and/or reduced recovery rate and/or additional 
delays) will have an adverse impact on the granting of credit and hence the 
safeguarding of economic activities.   
➢ The Commission´s pre-pack proposal is neither balanced nor clear: 
o In general, Member States need to have clear and predictable rules for the 
debtor and the creditors. Therefore, it will be convenient to have a clear 
definition of what prepack should mean.

➢ In the field of avoidance actions, the various national legal regimes show significant 
differences. These concern: first, the identity of the party entitled to take avoidance 
action; second, the time limits and the onus of proof applicable to avoidance 
actions; and third, the scope of the legal acts that could be challenged. Therefore, 
the Proposal should provide minimum harmonisation rules that addresses the 
fundamental questions of what, who and when can be challenged. 
➢ If there is a need for simplified winding -up proceedings for microenterprises in 
some MS in order to provide a functioning system to take unprofitable 
microenterprises out of business , it should, however be taken into consideration 
that by far the largest part of corporate insol vency proceedings   relates to 
microenterprises with less than 10 employees and with an annual turnover or a 
total balance sheet of less than 2 million Euros. The implementation of a new 
winding-up regime for microenterprises would have a material effect on  the 
established insolvency systems in many MS and would replace it in many respects.  
Moreover, fundamental pillars of business life (e.g. , accounting, tax obligations, 
social security contributions, file -keeping) are devalued in the interest of a quick 
proceedings. It is questionable if an orderly winding -up can be achieved by the 
proposed “quick and dirty” approach. From the perspective of banks insolvency 
administrators and/or courts are the first choice for the leading part in small 
insolvency proceedings. There is no reason to move the winding -up process from 
insolvency courts and administrators to creditors and the debtor itself. As said, it 
is questionable if simplified winding-up proceedings will contribute to the objectives 
of the Proposal. It is s uggested that t he introduction of simplified proceedings 
should be left to the discretion of MS.  Especially because in some Member States 
valuable and better alternatives might already exist.

EBF POSITION 
 
I. GENERAL PROVISIONS  
 
o A definition of what is insolvency is missing. This definition should include 
situations where a debtor is unable to fulfil obligations and also (if so considered 
by Member States) if its liabilities are clearly greater than its assets, valued in 
accordance with the applicable accounting standards. 
 
o In the definition of art. 2 (g) of “executory contracts” it should in any event be 
made clear that any agreement in relation to financing does not qualify as an 
“executory contract”. 
 
o The definition of ‘ best-interest-of-creditors test ’ in art. 2(h) should not only 
refer to a piecemeal liquidation but also to a sale going concern in a liquidation 
as this is the default in many MS. 
 
o The definition of “party closely related to the debtor” under article 2 (q) is, in 
some cases, too wide and not specified ; therefore, it should be better defined 
and specified in order to avoid (or to be aware of) any possible risk of avoidance. 
 
 
II. PUBLIC CREDIT  
 
At the moment, in some member states, public credit has a privileged position both 
in the pre-bankruptcy and bankruptcy. It should be noted that the privileged status 
of public creditors is detrimental to other creditors and to the debtor itself, to the 
point that some debtors are forced into liquidation precisely because of the public 
credit. 
 
This privileged position should be removed, and the public credit should be able to 
be dragged to the reductions and waivers agreed by the majority of creditors 
(including those under public law) both in the pre -bankruptcy and bankruptcy

III. AVOIDANCE ACTIONS  
 
o We overall support that the Proposal regarding avoidance actions is based on a 
harmonisation approach due to the diffe rences in the legal regimes in the 
Member States. 
 
o Some national regimes include a clear list of acts considered to be contrary to 
the interests of the insolvency estate / general body of creditors, therefore 
allowing for all parties to be aware of their c onsequences. It is suggested that 
a similar approach/regime were to be included in the Proposal. This could bring 
benefits, namely by allowing parties to know, in advance, which acts would be 
subject to this sanction.  
 
o It is important to highlight the suc cessful exemption granted for new and 
interim financing and for security granted to secure such financing (Whereas 
10). 
 
o How is ‘unable to pay mature debts’ to be understood? Does one debt suffice?  
What if a payment delay is granted? Could that imply that certain acts might 
be declared void/unenforceable whilst in some jurisdictions, according to local 
law, the debtor might not even be in a bankruptcy situation. 
 
a) Preferences (Article 6) 
Moreover, in the case of due claim (paragraph 2), the proposal should specify that 
the burden of proof of knowledge of creditors should be on the insolvency practitioner.  
The declaration of insolvency by the court, and not the simple request for the opening of 
insolvency, should probably be a better starting point for two reasons: i) a simple request 
may not be accepted; and ii) there are different publicity requirements for Member States 
in relation to such items.  
 
Moreover, paragraph 3 provides that, by way of derogation from paragraphs 1 and 
2, Member States shall be able to allow that the certain legal acts cannot be declared void. 
An exhaustive listing of these acts prevents Member States from taking into consideration 
certain aspects which specifically concern the legislation of their own legal systems. 
Therefore, Member States should be free to provide for further exemptions in their national 
frameworks. In Italy, just as an example, the rules on avoidance actions do not  apply to 
“credito fondiario” (residential mortgage loan) and some exceptions to these rules are 
contained in Law n. 130 of 1999 on securitisations and covered bonds.  
 
In any case, it is important to ensure that legal instruments, commonly used by 
creditors, such as datio pro solutum and datio pro solvendo (e.g. real estate assets that 
are transferred to the mortgage secured creditors upon appraisal) are clearly excluded 
from avoidanc e actions under article 6.  These legal institutes are instrumental to the 
resolution of NPLs by the banking system and represent, to a great extent, an anticipation 
of the results of a winding -up of the debtor, concerning the secured creditor, therefore

b) Legal acts against no or a manifestly inadequate consideration (Article 
7) 
There is a need to explain the concept of “manifestly inadequate consideration”, 
and, preferably provide relevant examples, in view of national jurisprudence from MS that 
already considers this requirement to be verified in cases of marginal/not significant 
differences compared to market values. 
 
c) Legal acts intentionally detrimental to creditors (Article 8)  
The extension of deadlines proposed in Article 8, which may affect acts carried out 
up to four years before the filing of the insolvency proceeding, may generate great legal 
uncertainty and turn against the debtor itself. Financial institutions may be more reluctant 
to refinance debt in view of  the danger that the operation may be subject to a 
reinstatement action. 
 
If a several legal acts are part of a package, the package as a whole (rather than 
the single acts) should be judged.

d) General consequences (Article 9) 
It should be made clear that the debtor will also not be able to rely on the «  legal 
act » in question. In particular, we are thinking of the case where contractual clauses exist 
in the act which is the subject of the invalidity, which provide that the payment made will 
extinguish the claim arising from an earlier act (i.e. that appears to be dealt with by Article 
10(1). That is to say that the creditor affected by the action for a declaration of invalidity 
must regain his rights as they existed before the act for which the declaration of invalidity 
was signed. 
 
Indirect damage (such as all the consequences of bankruptcy that some creditors 
might wish to attribute to the creditor who has benefited from the act of nullity) should be 
excluded. 
 
Moreover, under paragraph 2, due to the wide definition related to the statement 
(“the party …. Is obliged to compensate …”), the real perimeter of this provision should be 
clarified, in order to limit - without any doubt - its effect only to the available avoidance 
without any other disbursement or other compensation due. 
 
In paragraph 4, it is s tated that a claim to obtain full compensation pursuant to 
paragraph 4 may be assigned. In some Member States these claims are unassignable due 
to the specific nature of the claim and the fact that any proceeding in ration to these claims 
is subject to prior approval of the bankruptcy court. It is suggested to amend this clause 
and make clear that a claim can only be assigned provided that the avoidability is accepted 
by the counterparty, or determined by a court. In any event prior approval of the 
bankruptcy court to assign should be required. 
 
e) Liability of third parties (Article 11) 
In the paragraph 2) it should be made clear that there cannot be any automatic 
effect against a third party who acts in good faith as a consequence of the avoidance action 
against the beneficiary of the legal act that has been declared void. In other words, the 
rights of the third party acting in good faith should be guaranteed.  
 
Moreover, it should be provided that the knowledge (paragraph 2, point b) should 
be proved by the insolvency practitioner. 
 
f) Undefined legal concepts 
It should be highlighted that, due to the implications that this type of actions may 
have, it is necessary to clearly define legal concepts such as "legal acts of the debtor 
against no or manifestly inadequate consideration" (article 7) and "which the debtor has 
intentionally caused a detriment to the general body of creditors" (abovementioned article 
8).

IV. TRACING ASSETS BELONGING TO THE INSOLVENCY ESTATE 
 
In general, EBF would like to say that it is very supportive to asset tracing.  
However, we would like to suggest the below improvement: 
 
o Which tools can be used by creditors in order to improve their debt 
recovery? A first consideration is that the national beneficial owner   registers 
and, especially, their interconnectivity currently fail. The legal basis for these 
registers was set out in the Directive (EU) 2015/849 but, unfortunately, it still 
doesn´t have a real effect and has not truly been used by th e Member States. 
In order to meet the goal of asset tracing, Member States would need to make 
interconnectivity work.    
 
o Another point is about national insolvency registers. Focusing on the source of 
the problem, we consider that a n approximation of nati onal insolvency 
registers regimen is required . Member States should have clear and 
harmonized rules related to what has to be registered and when . That means 
that those rules will need to focus on the procedure, content and publicity of 
the insolvency registers. 
 
o Digital assets tracing should also be considered important for value recovery. 
Nowadays it is not easy to identify the owner of such assets and registers have 
not been adapted yet to this new reality.    
 
o We agree on providing a key role to the ins olvency practitioner (and not, 
as currently proposed in art. 13 to and including 16, designated courts) on asset 
tracing, but subject to  three conditions that attend to creditors concerns: 
1) certain powers and rights of the insolvency practitioner should be 
exercised under judicial supervision ; and 2) Member States also need a 
proper and harmonized regulation on the insolvency practitioner retribution, 
duties and liability and 3) banks should be compensated for additional 
costs of an inquiry.   
 
o It has to be ensured that the foreign insolvency administrator can only exercise 
its right to access registries in compliance with the national banking secrecy  
and data protection rules. 
 
o It is questionable whether it will work in practice to give the insolvency 
administrators direct access to the information in the registries (article 18).  
 
o Finally, relevant assets information should not be limited to bank accounts, 
since other financial entities, such as insurance companies and fund managers, 
may also hold relevant funds of debtors, and therefore should also be 
considered for the purposes of article 13 et seq. of the proposal. Assets search 
should also expressly extend to an adeq uate period of time before the 
insolvency was declared, namely the previous 3 or 4 years, to prevent wrongful 
asset depletion by debtors.

V. PRE-PACK PROCEEDINGS 
 
o Following the definition in the article 2 paragraph (p), in particular the reference 
to the “liquidation of the assets of the debtor as a result of the established 
insolvency of the debtor” it should be clarified that: 
a. also for the appointment of the monitor an insolvency is required (it does 
not appear so, if we consider the article 23 which refers not only to 
insolvency but also to the likelihood of insolvency) 
b. a judicial statement is necessary to prove the status, as above 
mentioned. 
 
o Creditors play a vital role and are frequently the first interested in keeping a 
business viable as a going concern, which is the best to recover their credits. 
The Proposal does not seem to recognize this in full, namely because it does 
not include the possibility of creditors taking the initiative to  start pre -pack 
proceedings, which could be a relevant alternative, especially in situations 
where the debtor is mainly considering its own interest and is not interested in 
transferring its business to third parties. It seems also important to ensure a 
higher degree of participation of relevant creditors in the sale process, namely 
through individual rights of creditors and/or a creditors committee with 
adequate powers to control the “Monitor” actions.  Creditors should have the 
power to reject a monitor th at does not provide the guarantee that the sale 
process will be fair (e.g. lack of independence or qualification).  
 
o Secured creditors should have a say in the sale of the secured assets.  
Favourable voting by secured creditors should be clearly set as a co ndition 
precedent to the approval of the sale of the debtor’s business or part thereof. 
 
o Most pre-packed sales require a silent and confidential preparation phase. The 
concept of a public auction contradicts the economic goals of pre -pack 
proceedings. It i s questionable if an attractive price can be achieved if the 
liquidation takes place through an auction. The public auction should be only 
one alternative to be considered along with others that are in place in each MS 
and could be more advantageous. 
 
o A st ay of enforcement actions significant ly affects the creditors´ rights that 
requires justification. Given that there are no criteria for the access to the 
proceedings, no participation of creditors in the preparatory phase and no 
means to avoid abuse such justification is missing. Moreover, it is questionable 
if a pre-pack sale is still feasible when the company faces enforcement actions 
of creditors.  A stay of enforcement actions should be limited, covering only 
individual creditors disturbing an economically viable going-concern sale. 
 
o In this vein, there should be a time limit in place for the preparation phase, for 
instance, 2/3 months with a possible extension to an additional month. 
 
o The unvoluntary change of the contract partner means a severe interfer ence 
with the principle of the contractual freedom to the detriment of the creditor. 
In its current definition of executory contracts, it is unclear if typical banking 
arrangements (accounts, loan agreements, netting arrangements) qualify as

a) Stay of individual enforcement actions (Article 23) 
Creditors will need more safeguards to ensure that this type of proceeding will not 
be abused by the debtor, so the existence of an insolvency situation that justifies the use

likelihood of insolvency and that the debtor will be given a different and additional stay to

negotiate a restructuring plan). The benefit of a new stay implies an unjustified restriction 
of the creditor´s rights.  So, it is recommended to balance these rig hts of the creditors, 
who appear in lack of any protection, and it should be provided an intervention and 
opposition before any stay in favour of the debtor. 
 
If the goal is to conduct the preparation phase as confidential (as explained in the 
Proposal Memo), it will be very difficult for creditors to be able to respect the benefit of a 
stay. We also consider that Member States should limit the duration of such stay and, for

Moreover, it is vital that a stay (if introduced despite the above concerns) does not 
have the effect of suspending termination rights in respect of financial contracts and, in 
particular, netting agreements. This should be addressed by a clear exemption f or 
termination rights regrading financial contracts, especially netting agreements. A 
reference to the Financial Collateral Directive (FCD) would not be sufficient this context.  
 
b) The monitor (Article 22) 
Taking into account the importance of proving the e xistence of an insolvency 
situation (as mentioned above), this should be included as one of the monitor duties.  
 
Moreover, the rights and duties of the monitor and its formal qualification should 
be more specifically defined also to better clarify the dif ferences with the insolvency 
practitioner.

c) Assignment or termination of executory contracts (Article 27) 
According to this article the assignment shall not require the consent of the debtor’s 
counterparty. A debtor should however not be substituted without the consent of a 
creditor. In our opinion a creditor must be able to choose the debtors. The creditor’s 
consent must therefore be required even in a pre-pack process. 
 
Moreover, it is important in article 27, that banks are not obligated to transfer credit 
contracts to a new entity that takes over the debtor’s business. Credit contracts are subject 
to strict regulatory requirements and that an individual credit assessment of the borrower 
is mandatory. Moreover, the lending bank has to ensure that it is compliant with anti-
money laundering law and other mandatory legal requirements (e.g. supply chain acts) . 
Therefore, it has to be clearly represented that there cannot be any mandatory 
commitment for the banks to maintain any of the assigned contract even in this 
proceeding. 
 
Furthermore, the exact meaning of the provisions of this article is unclear regarding     
a) the limits and the extent of the “debtor business” which is not defined, and 
it appears too wide and may include any asset o r activity related to his 
position, also, what in case activities are split?; 
b) the definition of “ executory contracts which are necessary for the 
continuation of the debtor business “.  
 
With regard to the possibility to assign or transfer assets and liabilities to another 
entity it also has to be noted that this possibility could also invalidate the effectiveness of 
netting agreements as risk mitigation instrument where such assignment or transfer would 
allow for the transfer of some but not all of the assets and liabilities under a netting 
agreement. This is because such a partial transfer would effectively undermine the single 
agreement concept of netting agreements whereby all transactions, claims, rights and 
liabilities under a netting agreements are combined to a single agreement. The single 
agreement concept is an essential element of all netting agreements and undermining it 
would have the same serious and far -reaching effects as a suspension of the termination 
rights in case of an insolvency. 
 
To ensure the necessary protection it should ther efore be expressly clarified that 
any application of the assignment or transfer tool in respect of assets and liabilities under 
a netting agreement can only be applied to the netting agreement as a whole (and of 
course only to the extent the netting agreement as not already been terminated).     
 
d) Debts and liabilities of the business acquired via the pre -pack 
proceedings (Article 28) 
It should be also expressly stated that the reference “free of debts and liabilities” also 
covers public credits (eg tax, soc ial security, etc), leaving no room for divergent national 
transpositions, as well as adding “without encumbrances or charges”.

The appellant will need to provide an adequate security covering potential damages

In particular, in providing that the grantors of interim financing are entitled to

proposal should clarify that such priority does not operate on  the proceeds from the 
liquidation of the assets subject to pledge or  mortgage, for the part intended for pledgee 
or mortgage creditors. 
 
In any case, for the interim financing the proposal should also provide for the 
protection offered by art. 17, paragraph 1, of Directive (EU) 2019/1023 (“ Member States 
shall ensure that new financing and interim financing are adequately protected. As a 
minimum, in the case of any subsequent insolvency of the debtor: (a) new financing and 
interim financing shall not be declared void, voidable or unenforceable; and b) the grantors 
of such financing shall not incur civil, administrative or criminal liability, on the ground 
that such financing is detrimental to the general body of creditors, unless other additional 
grounds laid down by national law are present”). 
 
Moreover, Articles 33.1(c), which contemplates the granting of security interest to 
interim lenders, does not address issues (such as ranking) relating to the granting of such 
new security interest against any existing security interest. Under the collater al law of 
most EU jurisdictions, the granting of new security interests (over the same assets) is 
subject to the consent of the existing secured creditors. In giving such consent, existing 
secured creditors will typically require that new security interest be of a lower ranking than 
those of existing security interest, and that any rights under those new security interest 
(such as enforcement etc) should be freezed until the claims under the existing security 
interest are discharged. 
 
Finally, s ection 33. 1(d) refer to be eligibility of set -off of interim financing by 
interested bidders. This set-off should not be possible to the extent interested bidders bid 
on secured assets if these interested bidders are not the creditors having the relevant 
security. The proceeds for those assets should be available to the secured creditors. 
 
g) Limitation of credit bidding (Article 33.3 and preamble (30)) 
In this section it is stipulated that Member States shall ensure that, where security 
interests encumber the business subject to the pre -pack proceedings, creditors who are 
the beneficiaries of those security interests may offset their claims in their bid only 
provided that the value of those claims is significantly below market value of the business. 
The position of a secured creditor should be based on the value of its security, not on the

VI. DIRECTORS’ DUTY TO REQUEST THE OPENING OF INSOLVENCY 
PROCEEDINGS AND CIVIL LIABILITY 
 
o There is no evidence that it is always in the best interests of the creditors that 
an insolvency proceeding is started. An out of court restructuring or pre -
insolvency proceedings often results in a better outcome. It is preferable to take 
the approach as laid down in article 19 of the European Preventive Restructuring 
Directive and to stipulate the duties of directors in a more general manner.

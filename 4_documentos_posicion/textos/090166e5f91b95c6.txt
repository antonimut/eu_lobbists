Eli Lilly and Company (Lilly) is an innovative R&D-driven pharmaceutical company that unites caring with 
discovery to create medicines that make life better for people around the world. We welcome the 
opportunity to provide feedback to the Commission Proposal for a Directive on Urban Waste Water 
Treatment COM(2022)541 (UWWTD) and the accompanying Annex, impact assessment. 
 
Below we set out the main principles we believe are needed to underpin the UWWTD, building on the 
consultation submission by our trade association, EFPIA. We also include an annex setting out why the 
data referenced in the Impact Assessment is flawed. We believe that a revised approach is urgently 
required to ensure the global competitiveness of the biopharmaceutical industry in Europe, based on  
clear verifiable and predictable shared financial responsibility. 
 
To be able to assess any potential financial responsibility for micropollutant removal, Lilly calls for the 
following principles to be adopted : 1) the financial responsibility is shared and distributed fairly 
amongst all industry sectors that make products w ith potential micropollutants as well as users of the 
products and the public that benefits from the products; and 2) there is a mechanism to cap the scope 
and cost of the EPR scheme . Both the formula for calculating financial payment and the use of funds 
needs to be transparent and agreed to in advance. A cap for the scope and cost of the scheme is 
necessary to reduce uncertainty and to allow financially responsible parties (identified in a fair and 
proportionate way to include producers and polluters) to forecast impact. Lilly supports the suggestion in 
EFPIA comments to conduct accurate impact assessments of the implementation of an EPR scheme for 
quaternary treatment. This is an essential first step. Once accurate impact assessments are in place Lilly 
agrees with EFPIA that producers and users need to be included in the determination and negotiations 
on details of an EPR scheme. 
 
Lilly is very concerned that the basis of the proposed EPR scheme is neither transparent nor 
supported by available data. We co nsider the proposal to be flawed in both the identification of 
the pharmaceutical and cosmetics sectors as being responsible for 92% of micropollutant toxic 
load and those two sectors being assigned sole financial responsibility for quaternary treatment. 
Lilly has provided detailed comments on this issue in an attached annex.

Annex 
The 92% contribution of pharmaceuticals and cosmetics of total toxic load is overstated 
and should not be taken at face value to make impactful policy decisions. 
 
The basis for the conclusion given in the 2022 EPR Feasibility study that pharmaceuticals and cosmetics 
contribute 92% of the toxic load that enters wastewater treatment is not provided and cannot be verified 
or reproduced. In order to verify the accuracy of that conclusion, the detailed methodology and data for 
substance inputs and PNEC values of the substances needs to be provided. Additionally, the 
methodology that is presented is flawed because it does not use refinement of the input by human 
metabolism and does not consider that wastewater treatment can significantly reduce toxic load to 
surface waters. These refinements are recognized by other EU regulatory authorities (i.e., the European 
Medicines Agency and the European Chemicals Agency).  
 
There are  two sources that also tried to measure the contribution of individual micropollutants to 
environmental toxic loads. Both sources [Water Solutions (2020) and Pistocchi et al (2022)] were cited 
in the 2022 EPR Feasibility study and are referenced below. The se two sources considered toxic loads 
in surface water and the resulting assignments of toxic load contributions are different from that of the 
EPR feasibility study. Water Solutions assigned 61% of the toxic load to pharmaceuticals while Pistocchi 
et al a ssigned 35% to pharmaceuticals. Both sources did account for removal of pharmaceuticals via 
human metabolism and wastewater treatment.

The Water Solutions (2020) did not provide data for all substances evaluated for environmental toxic 
loads. The Pistocchi, et. al. (2022) publication provided its full dataset as supplementary data. Using this

data only 29.7% of pharmaceuticals and their metabolites and cosmetics had toxic load contributions 
greater than 0.01% and these substances contributed a total of 35.3% of the toxic load in the surface 
water. Because the data was provided, it was apparent that the PNEC values used for Telmisartan and 
Candersartan [ranked #1 and #6, respectively, for toxic load contributions] were very low due to derivation 
by in silico modelling. Both of these pharmaceuticals have empiric al chronic data available for algae, 
daphnia and fish, and this data should be used to derive more accurate PNEC values using methods in 
the Water Framework Directive. The PNEC value for Telmisartan should be 49,000 ng/L, not 0.55 ng/L. 
The PNEC value for Candersartan should be 100,000 ng/L, not 3.1 ng/L. Applying these two recalculated 
PNEC values to the Pistocchi dataset results in Telmisartan and Candersartan dropping out of the top 
300 contributors and results in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics only contributing 13.7% of the toxic load.  
 
These two sources (Water Solutions and Pistocchi) contradict the conclusion of the 2022 EPR 
Feasibility Study on the contribution to the toxic load by pharmaceuticals and cosmetics. These 
sources suggest that the 92% contribution is overstated and should not be taken at face value to 
make impactful policy decisions.

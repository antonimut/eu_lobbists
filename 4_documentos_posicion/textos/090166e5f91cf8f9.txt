VCI POSITION  | 13 MARCH 202 3 
  
VCI Position on the Commission proposal for 
integrated water management – revised lists of 
pollutants in surface and groundwater 
The VCI welcomes the opportunity to take position on the Commission proposal amending 
Directive 2000/60/EC establishing a framework for Community action in the field of water policy, 
Directive 2006/118/EC on the protection of groundwater against pollution and deterioration and 
Directive 2008/105/EC on environmental quality standards in the field of water policy. 
 
We support the intention to make prioritisation processes, the setting of quality standards and 
threshold values more transparent and efficient and to align them with other principles, in order 
to make the best use possible of available expertise and data. However, we would take this 
opportunity to express our concern about the system change with regard to river basin specific 
pollutants, the extension of the Commission's competences and the lack of transparency that 
comes with this exercise. 
 
The chemical industry uses water, in particular, for cooling and production purposes and needs 
permits, approvals as well as official notices on taxes, charges and levies. Therefore, fast and 
legally sound licensing procedures under water law are of special importance for German 
industry. The industry must carry out comprehensive restructuring to achieve the climate 
targets, which includes numerous additional permits. 
 
I Extension of the Commission’s competence 
In future, the EU Commission is to have the possibility to set EU-wide harmonised threshold 
values for pollutants/indicators, as specified in Annex II Part B for which Member States have to 
consider setting national threshold values. The brief and barely comprehensible justification for 
this reads as follows: "grant the Commission delegated powers to set, where necessary and even 
for pollutants or groups of pollutants which are not of EU-wide concern, threshold values at EU 
level, to enhance the level of human health and environmental protection and achieve a more 
harmonised implementation". 
 
This means that the Commission should be empowered to set EU-wide harmonised EQS for river 
specific pollutants including for certain (national) river specific pollutants, i.e. also for pollutants 
that are not or not yet of EU-wide significance. This is a clear extension of the Commission’s 
competences. The justification provided by the Commission is not convincing: If pollutants are 
not of Europe-wide relevance, it is doubtful whether or to what extent the Commission should 
have any competence at all to set EU-wide threshold values. 
 
In the time to come, the Commission will be able to exert much more influence on the 
parameters through "delegated acts" . In future, decisions on the revision of Annexes I and II will 
not be made in the co-decision procedure but by way of such delegated acts (see Article 8). This

In various legislative procedures, the Commission has shown a clear tendency to prefer

can only prevent the project altogether. Therefore, the question arises as to why these important 
decisions are not taken at least by means of "implementing acts" where a committee composed 
of Member States’ delegates has a say. More concretely, where the committee with its counter-
positions can force the Commission to amend or revise the original text, if necessary. 
 
II Amendment of the Water Framework Directive (WFD) 2000/60/EC 
WFD rules are becoming increasingly important. According to experience so far, the European 
Union needs solutions to the following aspects: 
 
 It is not clearly regulated how to continue with the WFD objectives and measures after 2027. 
Yet such clear regulation is necessary to ensure legal certainty. The WFD in its current form 
will remain in force after 2027. Therefore, the Member States are obliged to update the 
relevant planning documents every 6 years, also following 2027. However, deadline 
extensions are no longer possible after 2027, except for natural conditions. The MS will have 
to decide whether they can realistically expect to achieve good status/potential for the 
respective water bodies within the last cycle to 2027. This causes much legal uncertainty for 
public authorities in Germany. 
 
 Currently, the status of a water body is determined by its lowest quality factor. However, this 
distorts the assessment of the actual water quality. Water quality may have improved 
significantly, even if this improvement cannot be shown because of only one parameter. 
Improvements could be achieved by creating concrete development assessments in the 
Member States' implementation reports. Therefore, the "one-out-all-out" principle should be 
changed. 
 
 The central legal term of "deterioration" must be defined in a workable and proportionate 
manner. The principle of non-deterioration has led to a high degree of uncertainty in 
investment and on the legal side, all the more so after the Weser ruling of 2015. The strict 
interpretation of the principle that ensues from this sentence is a step backwards, as 
compared to the original aim of the directive. 
 
 Furthermore, in view of the far-reaching planned changes regarding chemistry overall 
through the inclusion of many new substances which extends and thus tightens the 
deterioration ban, German industry takes the stance that it would be appropriate to demand 
at least an exemption regime adapted to this.

1. Review of the interplay between objectives and exemptions (Article 4(5) and (7)) 
 
The chemical industry supports the objectives of the Water Framework Directive (WFD) and 
considers itself obliged to make an intensive contribution to achieving them. However, in view of 
the planned further shaping of the existing WFD, a large proportion of water uses rely on the 
exemption regime under Article 4(7) WFD. This is irrespective of the fact that Article 4(5) of the 
WFD provides for situations where, as a matter of principle, a discretionary approach to water 
management is possible. Ultimately, the exception is made the rule. However, the rule-
exemption-ratio should not be reversed, especially against the backdrop that not all industrial 
activities with water uses can be exempted. 
 
2. Design of exemption in Article 4(7) WFD 
 
Firstly, the current text formulation of the exemption pursuant to Article 4(7) WFD is problematic 
for industrial permit procedures. It should be made clear that the facts for exemption under 
Article 4(7) WFD must be open to all industrial activities, as a matter of principle. In particular, 
this means that not only changes in physical properties in the sense of purely hydro-
morphological properties should be the object of exemption. Rather, all changes in properties – 
i.e. also chemical, physico-chemical and biological changes in the ecological status (Annex V 
WFD) and changes of properties in the chemical status (environmental quality standards of 
Directive 2008/105/EC) of surface water bodies as well as all changes relating to substances in 
groundwater bodies (inter alia, as a result of the release of substances) – should be eligible for 
exemption in principle under Article 4(7) WFD. Especially against the backdrop of the intended 
extension of Annex I to Directive 2008/105/EC by many new substances, it must be ensured that 
exemptions can be allowed also in the event of deterioration in chemical status, in order not to 
put at stake important planned projects. 
 
Secondly, the exemption regime in Article 4(7) WFD should be designed in such a way that an 
exemption can also be allowed for economic reasons. From German industry’s perspective, 
granting exemptions only due to overriding interest would not be in line with the principle of 
proportionality. 
 
3. Introduction of three more management cycles 
 
Introducing at least three more management cycles within the existing WFD requirements will 
contribute significantly to more legal certainty and thus to speeding up procedures.  
 
Meanwhile, WFD implementation in permit procedures takes very lengthy preparations and 
studies by the those planning and realising the projects. There is much uncertainty about 
achieving the 2027 objectives – also and in particular in respect of the deviating management 
objectives and exemptions provided for in the WFD. 
 
Many water bodies will fail to achieve the WFD objectives within the given time frame. Especially 
in view of the fact that many new substances with threshold values are now to be included, this

management plans and programmes of measures would also need to be adapted accordingly.

III. Amendments of the Groundwater Directive (GWD) 2006/118/EC 
Annex I GWD: incorporation of new substances to assess the chemical status of groundwater 
bodies 
 
The list of groundwater quality standards in Annex I to the Groundwater Directive (2006/118/EC) 
for certain substances that must be complied with for good chemical status is supplemented by 
new substances with threshold values, which means a tightening of the legal act. It is of great 
importance, inter alia, that a groundwater quality standard for “non-relevant metabolites of 
pesticides” is introduced. So far, these have been excluded explicitly. Until now, the existing 
Annex I GWD only contains threshold values for “Active substances in pesticides, including their 
relevant metabolites” . 
 
Non-relevant metabolites undergo a rigorous EU assessment procedure based on a wealth of 
data that allows a risk assessment to be carried out. The threshold value of 0.1 µg/L proposed by 
the Commission for non-relevant metabolites of pesticides is an unsuitable theoretical 
precautionary value, even more so as it is thus being given equality to the existing threshold 
value for pesticides and their relevant metabolites. The threshold value of 2.5 µg/L is unclear in 
respect of the existing data situation for non-relevant metabolites, and it is unnecessary. 
 
Setting artificial and low threshold values (e.g. 0.1 or 1 µg/L) where a higher threshold value can 
be derived from existing data based on common EU methods creates a false perception of risk 
without improving safety for consumers or the environment. Non-relevant metabolites undergo 
a rigorous risk assessment in the common EU assessment procedure regarding human and 
animal health and the environment. Therefore, setting artificial threshold values must be 
deemed non-scientific in this context. 
 
Moreover, EU Member States should also be able to use the existing data on substances as a kind 
of "higher-tier option" (based on CIS Guidance No. 27) to set individual threshold values at 
national level on a scientific basis for a limited number of non-relevant metabolites that are of 
national concern. Annex II GWD would be the right place to do so. 
 
Regarding Annex III Table Quality Standards of the Groundwater Directive 
 
Number 6: Pharmaceutical agents in total are indicated with the limit value 0.25 µg/L. This sum 
parameter is not supported by scientific data. Analytically, a measurement will hardly be 
detectable with the theoretical number of more than 2000 approved active substances.

IV. Changes to the Directive on Environmental Quality Standards (EQS Directive) 
 
1. Change of the system regarding the inclusion of river basin specific pollutants 
 
The existing system, according to which “river basin specific pollutants” have been relevant for 
classifying the ecological status, is to be changed to the effect that their compliance will be 
decisive for the chemical status in the future. So far, the EQS for river basin specific pollutants 
have been used to assess the ecological status of waters. In the future, they are to be resorted to 
for determining the chemical status of waters. German industry rejects these changes to the 
system for river basin specific pollutants. A detailed impact assessment is needed beforehand, 
not least with a view to the further tightening that comes with such a step for companies. 
 
With the extension of the scope of "chemical status" to "river basin specific pollutants" , the latter 
are to be subject to the rules for the chemical status in the future. Thus, they are directly 
included in the assessment of the water status in respect of the chemical status. Moreover, they 
will have to be examined in project-related assessments with regard to the compatibility of the 
project with the management objectives for the chemical status. 
 
This change is accompanied by a considerable shift in the system in the assessment of the water 
status and a tightening of requirements where the management objectives are concerned. 
 
Up until now, river basin specific pollutants have been used for orientation and support when 
assessing the ecological status. This means that exceeding a river basin specific pollutant is only 
significant for compliance with the management objectives if this worsens the status of a quality 
component of the ecological status or makes it impossible to achieve the status of that 
component. The decisive factor for this assessment is the projected impact on the biocenosis of 
the water body, which can or must be assessed in a water ecological appraisal by experts. 
 
If river basin specific pollutants are treated as substances of the chemical status, this means that 
any exceeding of the future EQS for just one of the river basin specific pollutants leads to a failure

to achieve the management objective for the chemical status. This will regularly cause violations 
of the prohibition of deterioration, while compliance with the requirement to achieve objectives 
is not safeguarded.  
 
Therefore, this is not merely a minor shift of the substances under debate from one WFD annex 
to another – this is a major change in methodology as well as a qualitative and quantitative 
expansion and tightening of the substance approach, where the chemical status is concerned. 
 
Existing classifications of water status – both ecological and chemical status – will no longer be 
directly comparable with future assessments, as the assessment methodology is changing. This 
is not only relevant for existing descriptions of water bodies but also directly relevant for the 
approval of projects with impacts on water bodies, as the data basis to be used is no longer 
comparable over the time series. 
 
Moreover, the expected requirements for river basin specific pollutants will become more 
stringent through further upgrading and a higher standing. For example, according to the 
legislative proposal, the Member States will also be obliged to provide measures for reducing 
and “phasing out” river basin specific pollutants. In addition, the Commission will be 
empowered to uniformly determine river basin specific pollutants, even if these should be 
relevant only in some sub-areas of the EU. This shifts the decision on the binding inclusion of 
river basin specific pollutants away from the Member States to the Commission. 
 
As far as can be seen, the planned changes to river basin specific pollutants are not justified in 
more detail in the legislative proposal and the effects are not examined more closely. 
 
Tightening the chemical status, as a result of the extension to river basin specific pollutants, also 
gives rise directly to the need for an unambiguous clarification of the scope of application for 
project-related exemptions under Article 4 WFD (§31 of the German Water Management Act / 
WHG) in the wording of the WFD. If the requirements for the chemical status are further 
tightened, it must be clear that the exemption fully applies to all management objectives 
including the chemical status, and to cases of failure in all constellations. 
 
2. Introduction of new substances to assess the chemical status of surface water bodies 
(SWB) 
 
Moreover, the list of substances decisive for the classification of the chemical status of SWB and 
the relevant EQS are to be amended. This list is currently in Annex I to the EQS Directive 
(2008/105/EC), which had been previously extended by Directive 2013/39/EU. This Annex I, and 
thus the list of substances relevant for classifying the chemical status of SWB, is to be extended 
by many new substances with relevant EQS. 
 
These amendments also bring a considerable tightening of the legal situation, requirements in 
analytics will increase, and the necessary documentation in permitting procedures will become 
more extensive. In turn, all this will delay the planning of important projects of public interest.

3. No state of the art for measuring methods 
 
The lack of sensitive analytical methods causes legal uncertainties, and monitoring becomes 
almost impossible due to bureaucracy. 
 
The tightening of EQS and the setting of threshold values for new substances come with 
increased requirements for the collection and evaluation of monitoring data on surface and 
groundwaters. For example, it must be ensured that the relevant measuring data for the new 
substances specified in the directives are gathered regularly. This takes suitable measuring 
techniques and equipment together with a well-established monitoring programme. 
 
Therefore, valid statements on whether threshold values can be complied with are not possible 
with measurement methods based on the state-of-the-art (e.g. individual substances such as 
bifenthrin and deltamethrin are below the measurement limits often applied; for "non-relevant 
metabolites in pesticides" there is so far a lack of sufficiently established and defined 
measurement methods). Here, there is a shortage of sensitive analytical methods. For a 
comprehensive data situation, these would have to be established by the competent public 
authorities and agencies within the measurement programmes for WFD implementation. 
Overall, this involves much cost and effort. 
 
Subsequently, the results of the monitoring programmes would need to be made publicly 
available as part of WFD reporting. Only in this way can it be ensured in the future that a project-
related examination of the relevant substance concentrations in ground  and surface waters can 
be carried out. Already today, there are not enough data for various water bodies, in respect of 
environmental quality standards. 
 
4. Tightening of EQS for nickel and mercury 
 
The still applicable EQS Directive prescribes the following AA and MAC values for 
 
 Pb 1,2 AA and 14 µg/l MAC, 
 Hg no AA and 0,07 as MAC and in addition 20 µg/kg as biota EQS, 
 Cd 0,08-0,25 and 0,45-1,5 µg/l, respectively, depending on water hardness, and 
 for Ni 4 (bioavailable share) and 34 µg/l.  
 
According to the Annex to the new EU Commission proposal, the following more stringent values 
are planned for SWB: 
 
 Hg no AA and MAC as previously, but biota value is halved to 10 µg/l (in the future, referred to 
methyl mercury). 
 for nickel, clear tightening with 2 (bioavailable) and 8,2 µg/l.

For German industry, the question arises on what scientific basis and on what new findings such 
tighter values for nickel and mercury rely. It is not clear why there is a need to tighten these 
parameters. 
 
The introduction of the new parameter methyl mercur seems to be a restriction compared to the 
existing parameter mercury in biota, since purely from a logical viewpoint a single, here organic 
Hg compound should have a lower concentration than the total mercury value. However, it could 
be that mercury in biota occurs only or predominantly in this organic bond form. Then, halving 
from 20 to 10 µg/l would be a clear tightening, which would correspond to the EU Commission’s 
policy of a phasing-out or the zero-pollution approach. This raises the question of the 
Commission's motives, especially regarding the impact on economic activities in the European 
Union. 
 
This tightening of the Hg parameter would not take into account the fact that almost all SWB in 
Europe have a poor chemical status precisely because of Hg pollution. This has always been a 
fatal signal in describing the chemical status to the public, as all rivers and lakes are red in the 
maps. 
 
With regard to the tightening for nickel, it should be pointed out that – according to studies by 
the German Federal Environment Agency (UBA) – a number of uncertainties and limitations arise 
when including bioavailability in the risk assessment of metals. The following quote is freely 
translated by the VCI for the purposes of this position paper: “Risk assessment for metals in 
general: The technical guidance for metal assessment for the European chemicals legislation 
REACH describes the necessary components of a risk assessment. The here pursued normalisation 
of the general PNEC into a value that takes into account the bioavailability is ambitious. In the risk 
assessment of metals under REACH the following instruments are lacking: inter alia, no sufficient 
number of standardised toxicity tests in the risk assessment for the compartments water and 
sediment (at least not in application … ” (source: Risikobewertung für Metalle unter REACH, 
(Umweltbundesamt). 
 
Also for nickel, with increasing hardness of water toxicity is reduced drastically. Moreover, the 
question comes up how to deal with geogenic background pollution. For example, nickel 
contents in the run-off of mine waters originate from “natural mountains/rocks”.

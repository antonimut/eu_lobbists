VCI  POSITIO N 
 
VCI Position on the  
Review of the Urban Wastewater Treatment 
Directive (UWWTD) – 
Focus on Extended Producer Responsibility 
(EPR) in the water legislation 
 
10 March 2023 
 
The Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive (UWWTD) introduces the issue of Extended Producer 
Responsibility (EPR) into the water policy. The chemical industry in Germany and the VCI have 
been intensely accompanying this issue for many years under the Trace Substance Strategy of 
the German federal administration. With the setting up of the Centre for Micropollutants (SZB) at 
the Federal Environment Agency (UBA), Germany has brought this successful cooperation in a 
steady form. 
 
Emphasis is on developing methods at source. First round tables on relevant trace substances 
were already implemented with an active involvement of the VCI and the industry. 
 
This stands in contrast to the EU Commission’s new approach to eliminate large numbers of sub-
stances, which are not defined more closely, for precautionary reasons and without clear-cut risk 
assessment. This is to be done in an “end-of-pipe solution” . We do not support this approach on 
several grounds which are explained in the following. 
 
Matters of financing 
The debate on a financing principle must not be about the question of a cost contribution from 
third parties under EPR in the water policy. 
 
The EU Commission should speak for considering, in addition to regulatory law, the whole range 
of instruments on producer responsibility such as detailed information, voluntary take-back of 
products and voluntary agreements. The ideas for a “Producer Responsibility Organisation” as 
outlined in Articles 9(4) and 10 are unclear as such and delegate too much responsibility to the 
Member States. It is foreseeable that the expected bureaucracy costs will be high and the system 
to be installed will be too costly. The matter of financing seems to be regulated insomuch as in-
dustry is planned to fully pay for quaternary treatment.

Burdens on manufacturers / the industry location Europe 
From the viewpoint of the German chemical industry, a one-sided financial burden on manufac-
turers from pre-selected sectors to concretise the polluter pays principle (PPP) and to finance 
quaternary treatment is inappropriate. There must be no unilateral strain on Germany and Eu-
rope as industry locations. 
 
When interpreting the PPP , all actors (such as manufacturers, trade, private and commercial con-
sumers like e.g. hospitals) should be taken into account. 
 
The issue triggered by the EU Commission of how to finance quaternary treatment in wastewater 
treatment plants is initially an obligation for the owners/operators of these plants. This is the 
case for primary, secondary and tertiary treatment – new, deviating rules would give rise to diffi-
cult borderline questions. 
 
Conclusion: 
 
When looking for a general financing system at EU level, an orientation to the “Swiss model” 
would be a pragmatic approach, while the EU concept would cause legal uncertainties and much 
bureaucracy. In Germany, the course for the future has been set with the Centre for Micropollu-
tants (SZB), and a form of active participation has been found which, from the VCI’s perspective, 
gives consideration to the interests of all actors in water management. 
 
 
Detailed examination of the UWWTD (proposal) 
In its legislative proposal of 26 October 2022, the EU Commission has made only few concrete 
specifications regarding the design of "extended producer responsibility" in Articles 8 to 10. The 
Member States are to be given much room which, however, leads to ambiguities, conflicting ob-
jectives and even possible supply shortages for generic medicines (see below): 
 
 Costs 
For large wastewater treatment plants (above 100 000 p.e.) an “end-of-pipe solution” of qua-
ternary treatment is demanded. Without clear-cut risk assessment, this causes disproportio-
nately high costs. The chemical industry acknowledges its responsibility for its substances. In 
addition to regulatory law, the whole range of instruments of producer responsibility (such 
as detailed information, voluntary take-back of products and voluntary agreements) should 
be considered. 
As a general financing system, the “Swiss model” of a consumer levy would be a pragmatic 
approach, while the EU concept creates legal uncertainties and much bureaucracy, which ci-
tizens would refinance ultimately through more expensive products.

 Lacking description of technology 
Annex I part D defines purification requirements of secondary, tertiary and quaternary treat-
ment. But it remains unclear how to achieve these requirements. From the viewpoint of the 
chemical-pharmaceutical industry, a technology description, as defined for chemical treat-
ment plants in the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED), is indispensable here. The chemical 
industry is apprehensive that full cost coverage for quaternary treatment, as demanded in 
Article 9, will bring uncontrollable costs and, due to the lack of technology description (see 
above) lead to unworkable solutions for quaternary treatment. In this context, the planned 
involvement of industry (UWWTD, Article 10(5)) in a “dialogue process” is not sufficient. 
 
 Trade barriers 
The free movement of goods in the “Schengen area” makes it impossible to trace goods flows 
inside the EU territory. It remains unclear how to establish EPR data without creating “non-tariff 
trade barriers” . 
 
 Supplies of medicinal products 
Analgesics such as ibuprofen and diclofenac are produced in India and other Asian countries and 
imported into the EU with the smallest margins on sales. If an importer were to stop importing 
such generics into the EU market for economic reasons, the EPR system and medical care for citi-
zens would collapse immediately. The EU Commission's proposal does not provide for any cor-
rective option for the EPR system here. 
 
 Energy (neutrality) 
Quaternary treatment will always be energy-intensive and thus puts into question the pursued 
energy neutrality of wastewater treatment plant operation, which is already energy-intensive to-
day. 
 
Firm requirements for installing quaternary treatment in the Member States (MS) 
Article 8(1) introduces “quaternary treatment” – additional treatment of wastewaters to elimi-
nate a broad range of “micro-pollutants” still to be defined. 
 
 By 31 December 2030 for 50% of wastewater treatment plants with at least 100 000 p.e.; 
 By 31 December 2035 for all wastewater treatment plants with at least 100 000 p.e.; 
 By31 December 2040 also for wastewater treatment plants between 10 000 and 100 000 p.e. 
in areas where the concentration or the accumulation of micro-pollutant represents a risk for 
human health or the environment. 
 
Unlike many other UWWTD requirements, the demands to quaternary treatment in the MS are 
very clearly formulated and leave little room to the Member States, in contrast to specifications 
of substances etc. The time limits proposed are not feasible in practice (developing a design for

quaternary treatment, technical implementation, changes to discharge permits under water law 
and so forth). Relevant infringement proceedings against the MS must be taken care of. 
 
Against the background of the strict requirements to quaternary treatment, the EU Commission’s 
ideas for restrictions regarding “non-prescription drugs” (marketing authorisation of medicinal 
products) and potential treatment of partial flows of hospital wastewaters (EQS D) etc are diffi-
cult to convey. Conceivable measures at the source must fit with “end-of-pipe” measures or need 
to be taken into account, respectively. 
 
Success control for quaternary treatment 
As regards success control (Article 8(5)) of quaternary treatment, the EU Commission takes orien-
tation from the Swiss model. Why does the Commission not do so in respect of financing? 
 
Financing a whole-of-society problem by “individual sectors” of the chemical industry? 
The current proposal provides for a one-sided financial burden on manufacturers of medicinal 
products for human use and cosmetics to implement the “polluter-pays principle” . The VCI finds 
this inappropriate and fully rejects this. As a matter of principle, all responsible parties causing 
an environmental pollution must bear the costs for its prevention or remediation under the PPP . 
The discharge of trace substances into waters depends on different causal chains, so that various 
polluters come under consideration. When making a concrete selection decision on whether to 
have recourse to a polluter or not, it must be determined first which direct contribution to the 
water pollution exists. If citizens cause water pollution with their lifestyles, they are responsible 
too – if industry causes discharges of substances with its production/wastewaters, industry is 
responsible. 
 
Full cost coverage according to Article 9(1) UWWTD vs. Article 9 Water Framework Directive (WFD) 
“cost-covering water services” 
Article 9(1) stipulates full “cost coverage” . Is this the installation and operating of quaternary tre-
atment, possibly including the planning and monitoring by public authorities? 
 
Firstly, there is the question of the amount of financial contribution, the borderline to municipal 
investments in the sewer network (primary, secondary and tertiary treatment). Secondly, there 
is the question of the borderline to the requirements of Article 9 WFD with “cost-covering water 
services” . Ultimately, are there competing or complementary legal rules? Should industry refi-
nance municipal water management – because irrespective of the “obligation to connect to and 
use municipal facilities” [“Anschluss- und Benutzungszwang”] the municipal water management 
is unable to fulfil its tasks arising under its duty to provide services for the public or to refinance 
itself through the citizens?

Treatment of polluting substances according to Annex I, part C point C 
The requirement for the treatment of substances in wastewater is formulated in a rather general 
way and should be given a focus on “relevant substances” . Otherwise, there is the risk of difficult 
weighing in the granting of relevant permits, as regards to the assessment of substance proper-
ties and the resulting substance quantities. 
 
Treatment of polluting substances according to Annex 1, part C point d 
Reference to the provisions of IED 2010/75/EU on the issuing of permits 
Already today, it is mandatory to comply with the here quoted requirements from the Industrial 
Emissions Directive for plants subject to the IED. Thus, this gives rise to double regulation which 
is unnecessary and would cause misunderstandings in enforcement. 
Furthermore, the IED is currently being revised. This means that any reference to the IED in the 
existing text will become meaningless or wrong in the near future.  
 
Reference to the IED should be deleted.

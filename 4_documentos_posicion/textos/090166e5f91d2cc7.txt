Integrated Water management surface and groundwater 
SUEZ’s comments and recommendations 
 
March 2023 
Interest Representative Identification Number: 27799842497-69 
Suez considers that the European Commission has proposed an ambitious directive on integrated 
water management surface and groundwater. Protecting water is  indeed critical for EU citizens and 
the environment. 
The proposal sets new standards of chemical substances to address chemical pollution in water, which 
will reduce impacts on the environment, human health, pollinators, and agriculture. This step is very 
important. SUEZ welcomes the enlargement of the list of chemical pollutants, which should lead to the 
better protection of water resources: 
- PFAS  
- Glyphosate  
- Pesticides 
- Bisphenol A: a plasticizer and component of plastic packaging  
- Some pharmaceuticals such as antibiotics, painkillers, and inflammatory drugs 
The new groundwater directive complements the  revision of the Urban wastewater treatment 
directive. For SUEZ, there is clear alignment between these two topics.  
 
SUEZ’s recommendations  
In general, Suez supports the goals of the directive on  integrated water management surface and 
groundwater. We would like to propose the following recommendations:  
Allowing recharge of aquifers 
While supporting the propositions in this revision, SUEZ would recommend that the strengthening of 
the standards set up in the revision to this Directive  does not prevent the possibility to recharge 
aquifers with reused water or water from rivers . Indeed, recharging aquifers is a necessary tool to 
combat hydric stress, an ever-increasing problem for some member states.  
SUEZ would like to present an example using PFAS levels, but which can also apply to other types of 
pollutants, linked to an area around a river in France with two different artificial aquifer recharges: 
• The level of PFAS in the French River is between 20 and 30 ng/l. 
• The level of PFAS of boreholes influenced by recharge is between 20 and 30 ng/l. 
• The level of PFAS of boreholes influenced by watersheds other than this French River is from 
40 to more than 100 ng/l. 
• The mixture of water from boreholes used to produce drinking water is between 30 to 80 ng/l 
(depending on the boreholes used and the sites).

Therefore, the replenishment of Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR) with natural river water would not 
be achievable according to the standards set up in the revision (less than 4.4 ng/l of PFAS). It is thus 
important not to impose rules that would prevent the virtuous practice of artificial groundwater 
recharge.  Moreover, today most laboratories are not able to measure at such a low level of ng/l for 
this example. Therefore, we would recommend that for the recharge of aquifers, the limit applicable 
to drinking water should be respected.  
 
Moreover, in certain cases where the watersheds bring pollutions (diffuse or localized pollution), in 
particular for very populated agglomerations, recharge with a few tens of nanograms per liter can even 
reduce the average concentration of pollutants and improve the overall quality of the water.  
 
 
Considering feasibility  
 
Concerning the levels defined in the texts, it is important to consider the technology available today 
and whether it is indeed feasible to reach these targets for certain substances.  
 
SUEZ would like to highlight that the means for reaching the targets indicated in the proposal without 
having negative effects on water cycles is not clear. Besides reduction at source and monitoring, it is 
not clear how member states would manage to attain these targets, especially for certain substances 
for which there is no solutio n today to treat them. However, we note promising research and 
innovation in this matter on different treatment technologies. For example, we already know that the 
use of activated carbon, a known technique in the production of drinking water, would retain  and 
reduce the PFAS content and then destroy them. 
 
Financing the new standards 
SUEZ continues to support the Pollu ter Payer principal. This is indeed in line with the European 
Commission proposal in the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive where an ex tended producer 
responsibility mechanism is proposed for the obligations of treatment of micropollutants. In addition, 
reduction at source is a vital step that should be more explicit in the texts proposed.

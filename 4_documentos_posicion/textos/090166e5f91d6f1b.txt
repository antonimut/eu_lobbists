Executive Summary  
One of the key expertise of SUEZ is to collect and treat urban wastewater with wastewater treatment 
plants of all types and sizes . This helps not only big cities, but also smaller ones to correctly treat 
wastewater for the population. For this, we have expertise in construction, financing, operation, and 
maintenance of wastewater treatment plants. Besides, we offer services as well in water production, 
waste recycling and recovery facilities. SUEZ has more than 10 000 designed and built plants around 
the world who serve more than 1 billion people. 
SUEZ sees an importance in this  directive and fully supports its revision and the proposition made 
by the European Commission.  In addition, in the current context regarding the energy crisis, 
Wastewater Treatment Plants have a clear objective to be energy efficient and, in most cases, produce 
renewal energy.   
Since 1991, when the directive was first implemented, technological progress has highlighted the 
emergence of new pollutants. The toxicity of pollutants such as microplastics, micropollutants and 
chemicals (Pollutants of Emerging Concern) has been confirmed, justifying the reinforcement of water 
treatment provided for by the revision of the directive.  
Technological solutions exist today that cover the requirements of this revision. It is vital that they 
are rolled out everywhere. Such change is more pressing considering new uses of wastewater (re-use 
for irrigation in agriculture, watering of public green spaces, recovery of sewage sludge and more ) 
which are likely to experience exponential growth in the coming years. 
The European Union proposes several funds to finance the transition in the energy sector, and for 
hydrogen, chips, and batteries. SUEZ proposes the creation of a Water Fund. Last summer was one of 
the hottest ever registered, with several droughts in European countries , but also the risk of floods. 
Water is and will become a precious resource that must be reusable and available. With an EU Water 
Fund the European Union could help to establish water projects which go in this direction. 
Finally, we strongly recommend that the ambition laid out in the proposal by the European 
Commission be maintained.

1. Definition 
To allow for a better implementation of the future Directive, some of the definitions included 
in the Proposal need to be further clarified or amended, such as: sludge, agglomeration, 
secondary and tertiary treatment, population equivalent, storm water overf low, separate 
sewer, and urban run-off, among others. Please see in the Annex under Article 2, the detail of 
the amendments that we support.  
In addition, some new definitions should be included, as these terms are used, but not 
defined, in the Proposal, such a s: discharge, urban runoff discharge, urban wastewater 
treatment plant, individual system, equivalent  treatment, energy audit, risk and risk 
assessment, and load. 
In the directive several articles mention gas emission (for example, article 21, 22, 11), but 
rarely mention the precise type of gas emission. For this, it would be helpful to include the 
exact names of gas emissions with their categorization of direct (methane, nitrous oxide, 
carbon dioxide) and indirect (electricity, district heaty) gas emissions.   
 
2. Stormwater Overflows and Digital Tools 
We welcome the provisions included as well as the template for development of urban 
management plans to ensure stormwater overflows  (Article 5)  and rainwater runoffs are 
managed.  
Urban water management in rainy weather justifies the introduction of “integrated water 
management plans” to manage pollution loads. According to the UWWTD impact study, 50% 
of the pollution comes from storm overflows a nd urban runoff. However, the “indicative” 
objective of reducing discharges to less than 1% of the annual load of collected urban 
wastewater seems unrealistic. 
Setting a more realistic  and binding target is essential for Member States to effectively 
commit to programs to reduce stormwater runoff . This objective may vary according to the 
context of the concerned agglomerations with levels depending on the zones. Thus, it could 
be set as an indication at 2.5% in areas with sensitive environments and areas with economic 
uses sensitive to health risks (swimming, shellfish farming, eutrophication), but should in any 
case never be greater than 5 %, regardless of the zone concerned. The implementation of 
these objectives can be achieved gradually.

Digital tools hav e been developed in recent years to help to announce, for example storm 
water overflows. SUEZ welcomes this initiative to use more digital technologies for storm 
water overflows and urban runoff (Article 5). These technologies exist already and are 
available. Moreover, they are very cost-effective.  
In addition, digital tools for wastewater treatment plants would also contribute to measuring 
the energy consumption to subsequently improve their energy efficiency and emissions. 
 
3. Energy neutrality and energy audits  
Firstly, we welcome the proposed energy neutrality requirements in Article 11. The inclusion 
of mandatory energy audits will support Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs) to measure 
their energy consumption. With a view to improving the energy consumption of the WWTPs, 
we consider that the mandatory energy audits should be accompanied by an action plan 
laying down a set of measures to be taken by the plants to reduce their energy consumption.  
While WWTPs are required to become energy neutral by 2040, improving their energy 
efficiency until this deadline will be critical to alleviate the energy bills of the municipality and 
for the WWTP to prepare for such neutrality. Many technologies (efficien t pumps, aeration 
systems, digital, etc.) to improve the energy neutrality of WWTPs exist with return on 
investments by reducing energy costs. 
Second, t he Proposal should consider a more holistic approach  on renewable energy 
production in WWTPs. We encourage considering renewable energy produced not only on-
site at the WWTPs, since the reality of the sector differs widely across Europe , to allow for 
economically and environmentally sound investment decisions.  
Therefore, we strongly encourage considering re newable energy used by WWTPs 
independently from its origin, i.e., the WWTP or other assets of the wastewater system or the 
urban water cycle (biogas, solar or wind energy, hydraulic energy (turbines)…); thermal energy 
extracted from wastewater streams insi de or outside the WWTP; heat and electricity 
recovered from sewage sludge outside WWTPs; renewable energy purchased externally or 
produced within a renewable energy community.  
Several business models for the utilisation of anaerobic digestion (AD) in WWTP  plants exist 
in Europe. Due to space constraints or economies of scale, urban wastewater treatment plants 
often organise into clusters with a single plant being equipped with an AD unit and centralising 
sludges of the neighbouring plants. Therefore, energy neutrality should take into account 
factors at and outside the wastewater treatment plant. 
Consequently, any way the sector contributes to the production of renewable energy  (for 
example biogas that is reinjected into the network) , even if used outside the wastewater 
facilities, should also be counted.

4. Circular Economy  
4.1 Water reuse  
The water sector is heavily affected by climate change through extreme weather events like 
droughts and floods. With droughts expected to become more and more frequent in the 
future, water reuse becomes a critical source of supply of water for agriculture, industry and 
other uses that do not require drinking water quality. Therefore, it is important to strengthen 
the water reuse requirements in Article 15 by requiring Member States to develop national 
plans tailored to their national water stress situation and utilizing climate chang e impact 
modelling.  
Technological solutions for water reuse are well -developed to provide for different qualities 
of water as required for reuse for different purposes and in line with health and environmental 
parameters. Timebound National Water Reuse Pl an should be deployed for municipal, 
industrial, agricultural, and environmental uses, taking account of climate change projections 
on the availability of water, to alleviate the pressures on the quantitative status of 
groundwater bodies as referred to in Directive 2000/60/EC, and surface water bodies in which 
treated urban wastewater is discharged.  
Furthermore, we consider that where treated wastewater is reused for agricultural irrigation, 
it shall comply with the requirements established under EU Water Reuse Regulation (EU) 
2020/741 but also to allow for derogation to Article 7, paragraph 1 to 4 to benefit from the 
agronomic value of the treated wastewater. Nitrogen and phosphorus from the urban 
wastewaters have an important agronomic value in irrigation , in application of the EU Water 
Reuse Regulation (EU) 2020/741 which highlights that the use of reclaimed water for irrigation 
purposes in agriculture or forestry as a way of restoring nutrients, such as nitrogen, 
phosphorus and potassium, to natural biogeochemical cycles. 
 
4.2 Sludge valorization  
Sludge is a valuable resource, and its valorization should be enhanced in the revised EU Urban 
Wastewater Treatment Directive. Whilst an EU Directive on Sludge exists, the Urban 
Wastewater Treatment Directive insists on maximizing the routes that would increase 
recycling and must acknowledge the least intensive and natural method for doing so which 
is sludge to land, which helps reduce the need for chemical fertilizers and enables carbon 
sequestration. 
 
4.3 Sludge and removal of chemical elements 
In the recycling process of water, it is important to remove chemical elements  that can be 
used as critical secondary materials in the market. For agriculture it would be phosphorous 
and nitrogen from sludge.  Possible European criteria  on sludge quality wo uld help to 
promote agronomic recovery of sludge.

5. Treatment  
Another major area of progress is the introduction of the principle of extended producer 
responsibility (Article 9) for medicinal products for human use and cosmetic products . On 
the one hand, it will enable the acceleration of the eco-design of these products and therefore 
reduce at source the presence of pollutants in wastewater. On the other hand, it represents a 
first step in the essential paradigm shift in terms of financing sanitation systems to introduce 
a balanced cost sharing: users and/or local communities will no longer have to bear all the 
cost of processing pollution for which they are not responsible . The proposed extended 
producer responsibility system will help public authorities to finance more advanced 
treatment solutions (Quaternary treatment, Article 8). Many advanced treatment solutions 
exist today on the market with varying energy intensities. The essential benefit of this 
treatment is to reduce micro pollutants which render a better quality in sludge and reused 
water.  
At SUEZ, we have been ex periencing for years the principle of extended producer 
responsibility in the waste sector. The model will need to be adapted for the Water Sector and 
we recommend that it be established under the supervision of Member States competent 
authorities with the support of producers for the purpose of fulfilling their obligations under 
Article 9.  
 
6. Sanitation  
The revised directive highlights the benefits of sanitation, via epidemiological monitoring of 
wastewater as a tool to protect public health (Article 17). The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted 
this expertise and accelerated the development of innovative solutions to monitor the 
spread of the virus thanks to sanitation networks, like the COVID -19 City Watch developed 
by SUEZ. Progress made in terms of analysis of chemical and biological markers in wastewater 
paves the way for new collaborations with managers of sanitation systems and health 
authorities. The obligation for member countries to implement monitoring systems for their 
wastewater will allow a consolidated vision of public health indicators (such as viruses, drug 
usage, consumption of medicinal products) and the possibility of implementing targeted 
prevention campaigns. The use of sanitation systems to promote public health is part of the 
“One Health” approach, which, with the adoption of a cross -functional approach to human, 
animal, and environmental health, should enable better anticipation of major global 
pandemics. 
We also fully support the right to sanitation (Article 19) included in the revised UWWTD and 
call for its adoption and implementation across all EU Member States.

7. EU Water Fund 
The rate of annual investments  is considered four times too low today in the world in terms 
of water and sanitation to achieve the ambitions set by the 2030 Agenda. Cooperation in this 
area is still too limited on the financial plan and makes insufficient use of mixed or innovative 
financing methods.  
Financing a transition in the sector is a critical piece of the revised UWWTD with the projected 
investments needed from public budgets, a slight increase in water tariffs and the proposed 
Extended Producer Responsibility Scheme.  
The European Union funds already some projects on water, mostly through the EU Cohesion 
Funds. However, this is not accessible for all Member States, as it is dependent on gross 
national income (GNI) per capita.  
SUEZ proposes the establishment of a  dedicated European Water Fund in the future to 
support such a transition in the same fashion as Europe has supported transitions in the 
energy, transport and many more sectors over the years.  Dedicated EU Funds have been 
established for many sectors: hydrogen, chips, batteries, EV charging in frastructure, 
renewable energy to name a few . It is important to understand the essential value of water 
for citizens and for any economic activity and support it through a strategic approach within 
funding.  
The purpose of the Water Fund would be to complement the already existing sources of 
funding that are available to the water sector.  In the case of the revision to the Urban 
Wastewater Treatment Directive, the European Commission has already identified sources of 
funding. Additional sources are also possible. Through the implementation of the Polluter Pay 
Principle, it can trigger further additional payment schemes for recycled and reused resources 
from wastewater by groups of stakeholders finding value in those resources.  
For example, farmers valorizing nutrient recovery from sludge as fertilizer, industries 
valorizing reclaimed wastewater, energy sector valorizing biogas generation at the urban 
wastewater treatment plant, etc.  
However, there will still b e a gap as financial pressure on consumers is limited, which is why 
the establishment of a Water Fund is necessary.

1. CONCLUSION  
SUEZ would like to clearly reiterate our support of the proposals made by the European 
Commission to the Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive. These proposals have taken into 
consideration the full potential of WWTP  and sanitation systems  and their corresp onding 
positive externalities. The proposal indeed takes into consideration  the urgency to be more 
ambitious to face climatic changes and notably water scarcity and stress as well as emerging 
health concerns.  
Increased public and private investment is imp ortant to ensure water security  and financing 
needs in the water sector. For instance, water and sanitation services in France suffer from a 
structural investment deficit. The French water sector estimates the need for additional 
investment of 15 billion euros over the next five years to anticipate the effects of climate 
change. 
We strongly recommend that the ambition laid out in the proposal by the European 
Commission be maintained.

Justification/Comments: 
(1.) ` urban wastewater’ : the definition must cover any possible mixture of domestic 
wastewater with non -domestic wastewater and/or urban runoff, since all 3 types of 
wastewater can be mixed. The Proposal was not considering this 3 rd option (as it is not 
possible to include and/o r in the legal provisions). The amended definition includes the 
possibility of mixing domestic water with non-domestic and /or with urban runoff. 
(3.) ` non-domestic wastewater’ : it’s important that definitions of domestic and non -
domestic wastewater are mutually exclusive. In this regard, non -domestic wastewater 
should include the wastewater produced in trade, institutions and industrial activities that 
has a different quality t o domestic wastewater, to avoid the burden of specific 
authorisations or permits for those activities whose wastewater is similar to domestic 
wastewater. 
(3a.) ‘discharge’: to allow for a more harmonised implementation of the Directive, the 
definition of ‘ discharge’ is considered necessary, given the extension of its use all along 
the text. 
(4.) `agglomeration’: the re -inclusion of “the population and/or economic activities” has 
the intention of better reflecting what an agglomeration can be considered, by better 
capturing the non-domestic part of it. 
Despite the fact that the different methodologies to define agglomerations have led to 
discrepancies among Member States, using the density of load (10 p.e./ha) is too simplistic 
and could lead, in some places,  to inappropriate implementation (e.g. in Sweden about 
0,2-0,5 million countryside family houses will immediately be defined as agglomerations 
since each of these countryside houses accommodate 10 people or even more during 
holidays peak seasons). Therefor e, we suggest not including this density of load in the 
Directive but revising the concept of agglomeration in the Report “Terms and Definitions

of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive 91/271/EEC (released in 2007) to include 
guidance on how to define the agglomerations, allowing for harmonisation across the EU.  
(5a) ` urban runoff discharge’ : it is important to include this definition to make the 
difference with storm water  overflows (from combined sewer systems) , but also to 
highlight that what can have an impact in receiving water bodies is the discharge of urban 
runoff. Urban runoff is a primary and general issue, which results in  2 secondary effects 
which are urban runoff discharges from separate systems, or storm water overflows from 
combined systems. The 3 concepts have to be explicitly defined to avoid confusion. 
(6) ‘ storm water overflow ’: since this is addressing the overflow of a combined sewer 
system, the wastewater discharged will be untreated, there is no need to specify it. In 
addition, th e discharge of urban wastewater from a combined collecting system is not 
necessarily into a receiving water body. The inclusion of the definition of discharge covers 
this. 
(7) ‘collecting system’: collecting systems cannot be reduced only to ‘conduits’ as they can 
also include other elements such as pumping stations, valves, gates, reservoirs, tanks, etc. 
Therefore we suggest including “accessory elements” in the definition. Furthermore, we

(8) ‘combined sewer system’: we propose to add the word “system” as the definition clearly 
aims at the whole system, not at an individual sewer or conduit. Besides, the propos ed 
amendment aims at better aligning the text in this definition with the amended definition

(9) ‘separate sewer system’: the proposed amendment aims at better aligning the text in 
this definition with the amended definition of collecting systems. Moreover, we propose to 
add the word “system” as the definition clearly aims at the whole system, not at an 
individual sewer or conduit. 
(9a) ‘urban wastewater treatment plant’: we suggest the inclusion of this definition as it is 
used all along the text, and also to establish a clear difference with the individual systems. 
It is important to underline the collective aspect of these facilities, enabling the reduction 
of costs for individuals and the society, the limitation of the impacts on the environment 
and the public health, and allowing for a higher expertise for management and monitoring 
and subsequent economies of scale. 
(9b) ‘individual systems’: we encourage the inclusion of this definition to allow for a clear 
and harmonised underst anding of what these individual systems are and what is their 
ultimate goal, i.e. ensuring the same level of environmental protection as secondary and 
tertiary treatment, but not the same level of treatment as secondary and tertiary treatment 
per se, as stated in Article 4 in the Commission’s Proposal.  
(10) `population-equivalent (p.e.)’: it is important in this definition to keep the notion of 
‘equivalence’, aiming to enable the addition of loads from wastewater from domestic and 
non-domestic sources. 
(10a) ‘load’: the definition of load is needed to clarify some requirements, such as those 
stated in Articles 5, 6, 7, 8 and 11, amongst others.

(11) ‘ secondary treatment ’: there is a need to establish the link between the type of 
treatment and the requirements to be met through such treatment, to better allow for 
checking compliance, and in this case such compliance would be with Table 1 of Annex I. 
(12) `tertiary treatment’: the definition in the Proposal considers the tertiary treatment as 
removing both nitrogen and phosphorus, which is contrary to the requirements set in Table 
2 of the Annex I, where Member States can remove Nitrogen and/or Phosphorus. Given 
‘and/or’ is not acceptable in a legal text, we suggest replacing “nitrogen and phosphorus’ 
by ‘nutrients’ to allow for covering Nitrogen and/or Phosphorus. In addition, there is a need 
to establish the link between the type of treatment and the requirements to be met through 
such treatment, to better allow for checking compliance, and in this case such compliance 
would be with Table 2 of Annex I. 
(13) ‘quaternary treatment ’: there is a need to establish the link between the type of 
treatment and the requirements to be  met through such treatment, to better allow for 
checking compliance, and in this case such compliance would be with Table 3 of Annex I. 
(13a) ` equivalent treatment ’: Article 6 introduces the possibility of an equivalent 
treatment, so it is important to de fine what that treatment is. The proposed amendment 
also refers to tertiary and quaternary treatment, because we are including this possibility 
in article 18 on risk assessment and management. The ultimate goal of any of the 
equivalent treatments used shou ld be to ensure the same level on environmental 
protection as secondary, tertiary or quaternary treatments, rather than the same level of 
treatment itself, since the overarching goal of the Directive is the protection of the 
environment.  
(14) ‘sludge’: the definition in the Commission’s Proposal covers all types of by -products, 
side-products, residuals, ... resulting from urban wastewater treatment, such as sand, grit, 
litter, etc. and for which recovery and/or recycling are different from ‘sludge’ as commonly 
known (for example in the Directive 86/278/EEC on the protection of the environment, and 
in particular of the soil, when sewage sludge is used in agriculture ). Sludge results from 
different stages of urban wastewater treatment and can have different c ompositions, 
aspects, or consistencies. The amendment aims to keep the definition of sludge as large 
as possible, to clearly distinguish ‘sludge’ from other by -products and to enable sludge, 
after relevant treatment, to be part of the circular economy. Spe cifying what sludge 
consists of and which materials, such as sand, grid, grease, and solid waste, should not be 
considered as part of the definition for sludge will furthermore increase the clarity of the 
text. 
(14a) ‘treated sludge’: sludge is a valuable resource stemming from the treatment of urban 
wastewater and presents many opportunities for reuse and recycling in line with the goals 
of Article 4 2008/98/EC. EurEau suggest distinguishing between sludge and treated sludge 
to enhance the clarity of the provisions of the legislative proposal and to better understand 
on which materials the requirements of Articles 20, 21(1) and 21(3)(b) should apply, and 
to avoid dewatering/thickening of raw sludge being considered as ‘treatment’. 
(16) ‘ micro-pollutant’: micro -pollutants are substances detected at the level of the 
microgram (0.000001 gram), not just under milligrams (0.001 gram).

(19b) ‘hazard’, (19c) `hazardous event’, (19d) `risk’, (19e) `risk assessment’: to allow for 
a better and harmonised implementation of Article 18 on risk assessment and management 
among Member States, the inclusion of the definitions of hazard, hazardous event, risk and 
risk assessment are needed. The definitions of hazard, hazardous event and risk are based 
on the ones included in the Directive 2020/2184, whereas the definition of risk assessment 
is the one in the Resilience of Critical Entities Directive ((EU) 2022/2557) recently 
approved. 
(20) ‘sanitation’: the wording “sanitatio n services” is used by the urban water sector to 
define the services providing collection and treatment of urban wastewater. In addition, 
article 19 only refers to sanitation facilities. In order to avoid misunderstandings when 
implementing article 19, we encourage deleting “services” from the definition.  
(24) ‘placing on the market’: `placing on the market’ is used in the Proposal linked to 
placing on the national market medicinal products for human use and cosmetics products 
(included in Annex III), whose quaternary treatment is to be covered by EPR schemes. 
More specifically, this term is related to the exoneration clause to the implementation of 
EPR schemes (Art. 9(2) which mentions that no EPR schemes need to be implemented 
when a producer places on th e market less than 2 tonnes of a product per year). If this 
exoneration is to be applied at the national market level it will lead to a lack of 
implementation of EPR schemes to cover the treatment of micro -pollutants from the 
abovementioned products in Mem ber States where the population is smaller (less people 
will mean less consumption of medicinal products for human use and cosmetics products 
and, therefore, less amount of these placed on the market of the Member State). This will 
generate inequalities for citizens in Europe regarding environmental and health protection 
based on the amount of population. To avoid this situation, the placing on the market 
should be on the EU market level (further details and explanation are provided in the 
justifications to amendments to Article 9)

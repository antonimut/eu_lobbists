Contents 
German insurers are grateful for the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s 
proposal for a directive harmonising certain aspects of insolvency law (hereinafter 
referred to as “proposed directive”). For the German insurance industry, the pro-
posed directive is relevant not only with regard to its role as major institutional in-
vestor but also with regard to its business activities as trade credit and surety in-
surer.  
With an investment volume of about EUR 1.9 trillion, the insurance industry is one 
of the largest institutional investors in Germany, making new investments of about 
EUR 300 billion each year. 
Trade credit insurance protects businesses against the failure of customers to pay 
for products or services supplied to them. Another key feature of trade credit insur-
ance is the ongoing assessment of the creditworthiness of the policyholder’s cus-
tomers as well as professional consulting and representation of trade creditors in 
insolvency proceedings, including participation in creditors’ committees. The vol-
ume of transactions covered by German credit insurers amounted to about 
EUR 510 billion in 2022.

Surety insurance is an insurance by which the insurer provides guarantees and 
sureties to compensate the beneficiary in the event that the policyholder fails to 
comply with contractual or legal obligations. If the policyholder becomes insolvent, 
the beneficiary may claim compensation from the insurer. Guarantees provided by 
surety insurance are particularly common in the construction and mechanical en-
gineering industry. They provide policyholders with liquidity and do not increase 
their credit exposure to banks. The total volume of guarantees and sureties pro-
vided by German surety insurers amounted to about EUR 78 billion in 2022.  
Executive summary
German insurers welcome the proposed directive insofar as it aims to strengthen 
the creditors’ position in insolvency proceedings across the European Union and 
to increase their chances to influence the proceedings, in particular through credi-
tors’ committees. The proposal is also fully supported insofar as it shall ensure that 
creditors are able to obtain the largest possible amount from the recovery of the 
assets belonging to the insolvency estate (increase in the amount to be distributed 
as a result of avoidance actions, asset tracing, directors’ duty to submit a request 
for the opening of insolvency proceedings).  
However, the proposed directive also includes provisions which do not take suffi-
cient account of the creditors’ interests. This applies to the provisions on pre-pack 
proceedings in their current form as well as to the envisaged introduction of simpli-
fied winding-up proceedings for microenterprises without the involvement of an in-
solvency practitioner. The proceedings for microenterprises, as currently laid out 
in the proposed directive, increase the risks of abuse with regard to actions reduc-
ing the insolvency estate and the transfer of assets.  
On Title II (avoidance actions) 
The proposed directive provides minimum harmonisation rules on avoidance ac-
tions. Their aim is to make sure that legal acts that are detrimental to the general 
body of creditors can be reversed. The proposal sets out three avoidance grounds, 
which are closely aligned to German legislation on avoidance actions:  
 Legal acts benefitting certain creditors/groups of creditors (“preferences”) 
 Legal acts against no or a manifestly inadequate consideration 
 Legal acts intentionally detrimental to creditors

Assessment:  
Introducing minimum standards for avoidance actions is appreciated. It allows for 
increased legal certainty when it comes to cross-border issues. The envisaged 
grounds and time limits for avoidance actions are in line with existing provisions in 
German law (Sections 129 et seq. Insolvency Code (Insolvenzordnung, InsO)). 
Pursuant to Article 6(3), by way of derogation from Article 6(1) and (2), “legal acts 
performed directly against fair consideration to the benefit of the insolvency estate” 
cannot be declared void. The requirements on legal transactions that cannot be 
declared void thus remain rather vague. Clear and precise provisions – for in-
stance, as stipulated in Section 142 InsO – would be desirable in this context to 
increase legal certainty.  
On Title III (tracing assets belonging to the insolvency estate) 
The proposed directive provides for rules on the tracing of assets belonging to the 
insolvency estate. The aim is to make sure that insolvency practitioners have ac-
cess to various registers which provide relevant information on assets that may 
belong to the insolvency estate (bank accounts, beneficial ownership registers, na-
tional asset registers). 
Assessment:  
Improving the access of insolvency practitioners to registers/public databases and 
other information on the assets is in the interest of the creditors and is supported 
provided that data protection and confidentiality requirements will be adequately 
fulfilled and consulting the registers will be conditional upon strict purpose limita-
tion. Where these requirements are met, it should be considered to extend the 
access to court records including criminal investigation records. They, too, fre-
quently provide information that allow for successful access to assets belonging to 
the insolvency estate.  
On Title IV (pre-pack proceedings) 
Member States shall be obliged to include in their national insolvency regimes so-
called pre-pack proceedings to allow for the sale of the debtor’s business or parts 
thereof already prior to the opening of insolvency proceedings.

Assessment:  
Introducing a procedure that allows for the efficient and rapid sale of the debtor’s 
business can be economically advantageous to the insolvency estate, and is wel-
comed in principle. However, it must be made sure that the creditors/creditors’ 
committees are given the opportunity to effectively influence the process despite 
the tight timeline. The draft does not yet take sufficient account of this issue.  
By way of derogation from what has been common practice so far, pre-pack pro-
ceedings would result in the fact that a transparent comparative evaluation of a 
business will be difficult to realise. A dual-track approach that is in line with the 
current practice in Germany (objective evaluation of the business by means of en-
gaging external experts) should at least be taken. It is therefore suggested to in-
clude a provision on carrying out such processes in Article 24 to take adequate 
account of the principle of best possible satisfaction of the creditors (Section 1 
InsO). 
It is being assumed that pre-pack proceedings will be integrated into the procedure 
for filing a request for opening insolvency proceedings that is common in the Ger-
man insolvency regime so that the necessary transparency and monitoring will be 
ensured through the already existing supervisory bodies (insolvency practi-
tioner/creditors’ committee). When opening the insolvency proceedings, it would 
thus also be ensured that the bodies familiar with the previous course of proceed-
ings can decide on the intended liquidation without much difficulty.  
Therefore, it is recommended to provide for participation of the creditors in the 
appointment of the monitor pursuant to Article 22 as well as within the scope of the 
sale process pursuant to Article 34 in accordance with the rules on the request for 
opening insolvency proceedings. Granting creditors the right “to be heard by the 
court” before the authorisation or the execution of the sale, which has been stipu-
lated under Article 34(1) of the proposed directive, is not sufficient.  
In addition, Article 34(4) provides for an extensive obligation to release security 
interests. Pursuant to Article 34(4)(a), Member States may depart from the require-
ment to obtain consent from holders of secured claims if the security interests re-
late to assets that are necessary for the continuation of the day-to-day operations 
and the creditors of secured claims fail to prove that the pre-pack offer does not 
satisfy the best-interest-of-creditors test. Such an extensive obligation to release 
security interests involving a reversal of the burden of proof is seen as problematic 
in terms of the suppliers’ rights with regard to the reservation of title as well as in 
terms of third-party securities, such as guarantees provided by surety insurers, and 
is therefore rejected by German insurers. It is necessary to make sure that the 
provisions on the release of security interests will not lead to secured creditors 
losing their priority rights to satisfaction without compensation. Existing securities 
shall be taken into account as a general rule.

Article 27(1), which states that the debtor’s contracts which are necessary for the 
continuation of the business shall be assigned to the acquirer even without the 
consent of the counterparty, also encroaches upon creditors’ rights. Pursuant to 
Article 27(2), exceptions shall only be allowed if the court decides that a termination 
of the contract is in the interest of the debtor’s business or if the acquirer does not 
meet certain technical or legal obligations to continue contracts with public author-
ities. Overall, this represents a significant, from our point of view too far-reaching 
interference with the contractual freedom which is protected under constitutional 
law in Germany through Article 2(1) of the Basic Law (Grundgesetz). Furthermore, 
such an interference is contrary to the legal consequence for surety insurance con-
tracts (termination of the contract upon the insolvency proceedings being opened) 
as stipulated under Sections 115, 116 InsO, resulting in an impairment of the real-
isation of securities within the scope of winding up the contract. 
On Title V (directors’ duty to request the opening of insolvency pro-
ceedings and civil liability) 
The draft provides for the directors’ duty to submit a request for the opening of 
insolvency proceedings where a legal entity has become insolvent. This shall be 
done no later than 3 months after the directors became aware or can reasonably 
be expected to have been aware that the legal entity is insolvent. In addition, Mem-
ber States shall ensure that the insolvent legal entity’s directors are liable for dam-
ages incurred by creditors as a result of their failure to comply with this obligation. 
Assessment:  
Minimum harmonisation in this area across the EU is welcomed. Given that the 
three month time limit, which is very long from a German perspective, is a maxi-
mum period, there is no need to make any amendments with regard to the German 
insolvency regime with its significantly shorter periods for submitting a request for 
opening insolvency proceedings, which should remain unaffected to protect credi-
tors’ interests. 
On Title VI (winding-up of insolvent microenterprises) 
The proposed directive aims at introducing simplified winding-up proceedings for 
insolvent microenterprises to reduce the costs of the winding-up process. This new 
type of proceedings, as a rule, does not provide for the appointment of an insol-
vency practitioner, the debtor remains in control of their assets and the day-to-day 
operation of the business (“debtor in possession”).  
0 6 C O M M E NT  
Assessment:  
According to the proposal, the simplified winding-up of microenterprises shall be 
allowed as an exception from the general rule. However, as a result of the refer-
ence made to the Commission Recommendation 2003/361 of 6 May 2003, the lim-
its set herein (enterprises which employ fewer than 10 persons and whose annual 
turnover/balance sheet total does not exceed EUR 2 million) apply to most of the 
insolvency proceedings in Germany. Hence, it will be the regular type of proceed-
ings rather than an exception. It is therefore suggested to establish significantly 
lower threshold values, for instance by cutting the numbers in half, meaning enter-
prises with fewer than 5 employees and an annual turnover/balance sheet total of 
up to EUR 1 million, or by referring to the limits (balance sheet total of up to 
EUR 350,000/turnover up to EUR 700,000) stipulated in Directive 2013/34/EU of 
26 June 2013 (Accounting Directive). 
Furthermore, there are fundamental concerns about introducing simplified pro-
ceedings in their current form that do not require the appointment of an insolvency 
practitioner.  
This applies in particular to the adequate consideration of creditors’ interests, even 
more so as the proceedings shall usually be conducted with the debtor in posses-
sion pursuant to Article 43, meaning that there will be no insolvency practitioner 
acting as an objective supervisory body. According to Article 39, an insolvency 
practitioner shall only be appointed if the debtor, a creditor or a group of creditors 
requests such an appointment and the costs of the appointment can be funded by 
the insolvency estate or by the party that requested the appointment. In proceed-
ings where the value of the assets of the insolvency estate is low, this will regularly 
result in no insolvency practitioner being appointed. The appointment of insolvency 
practitioners even in smaller proceedings, however, is common practice in Ger-
many and ensures that proceedings in general are conducted in a professional 
manner. In addition, it helps to build up the knowledge that is necessary to master 
large and complex proceedings. Given that most of the insolvency proceedings in 
Germany would have to be conducted in accordance with the provisions for wind-
ing up insolvent microenterprises without the involvement of an insolvency practi-
tioner (see above), important knowledge and winding-up capacities with regard to 
managing insolvencies would be lost. 
Most microenterprises will not be able to manage a proper winding-up of their busi-
nesses and an adequate realisation of the insolvency estate. Hence, they will have 
to seek professional advice, which raises the question whether a reduction in cost, 
which is the aim of introducing simplified proceedings, can actually be achieved.  
Proceedings without the involvement of an insolvency practitioner will probably not 
result in cost savings, but in shifting the costs from insolvency practitioners to the 
courts, which might indeed lead to an increase of the overall costs of insolvency

proceedings due to the significant increase in public administration capacities and 
resources of the insolvency courts.  
Without professional consulting and monitoring of the debtor, there is also a signif-
icant risk of abusive actions and transfers of assets to the detriment of the insol-
vency estate. In order to protect creditors, access to simplified proceedings should 
be made conditional upon the submission of complete and proper annual financial 
statements/accounting records. 
The proposed directive generally lacks clear provisions on the conditions under 
which creditors can influence the way the proceedings are conducted and how 
exactly they can exert influence on the proceedings. The satisfaction of secured 
creditors, for instance suppliers with a right to separate satisfaction due to reser-
vation of title, as a matter of priority, must also be ensured in simplified proceed-
ings. 
Not pursuing avoidance actions, which is possible pursuant to Article 47 of the 
proposed directive, which states that the pursuit of avoidance actions shall not be 
mandatory, but shall be left to the discretion of creditors, might result in the fact 
that any potential to increase the insolvency estate will only be insufficiently or not 
at all realised to the detriment of creditors.  
On Title VII (creditors’ committees) 
According to the proposed directive, Member States shall ensure that a creditors’ 
committee is established if the general meeting of creditors so agrees or if a cred-
itor requests to do so after the submission of a request for the opening of insol-
vency proceedings. In addition, minimum harmonisation shall be achieved with re-
gard to the following aspects: appointment, composition, working methods, func-
tion, and personal liability. 
Assessment:  
Creditors’ committees are an important tool to defend and pool creditors’ interests. 
The envisaged minimum standards are therefore reasonable with regard to estab-
lishing a proper legal basis for the work of the committees, which provides legal 
certainty across the EU. They are also for the most part in line with the existing 
provisions in Germany. 
The only concerns here relate to the envisaged creditors’ committees’ duty to pro-
vide information to the creditors, as stipulated under Article 64(1)(e). This provision 
is to some extent in contradiction to the confidentiality of information shared in the 
creditors’ committee, which is stipulated in Article 63(2)(d). Furthermore, the

introduction of a duty to provide information is not very practice-oriented and has 
indeed the potential to deter people from participating in a creditors’ committee.  
On Title VIII (factsheet with information on the national insolvency 
law) 
For the sake of greater transparency, Member States shall be obliged to make a 
key information factsheet on certain elements of national law on insolvency pro-
ceedings available on the European e-Justice Portal. The factsheet shall provide 
information on the following aspects, in particular: 
 conditions for the opening of insolvency proceedings 
 persons that can request the opening of insolvency proceedings 
 lodging and admission of claims 
 ranking of the claims 
 average length of proceedings 
Assessment:  
The introduction of a factsheet is appreciated. It might be a useful tool for creditors, 
which can provide important information that will help to understand the respective 
national insolvency law and its specific features.

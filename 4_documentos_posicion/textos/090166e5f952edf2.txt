Re:  Consultation for Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council 
amending Regulations (EU) No 648/2012, (EU) No 575/2013 and (EU) 2017/1131 as regards measures 
to mitigate excessive exposures to third-country central counterparties and improve the efficiency of 
Union clearing markets.  
 
 
Cboe Clear Europe N.V. (Cboe Clear Europe ) is pleased to have the opportunity to provide feedback 
on the Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council amending Regulations 
(EU) No 648/2012, (EU) No 575/2013 and (EU) 2017/1131 as regards measures to mitigate excessive 
exposures to third -country central counterparties and improve the efficiency of Union clearing 
markets  (EMIR 3.0) and welcomes the Commission’s endeavours and vision to improve the 
attractiveness of European CCPs. 
Cboe Clear Europe is registered in the Netherlands and has been authori sed since 2 April 2014 as a 
CCP under Article 14 EMIR . Cboe Clear Europe is authorised to  offer pan-European clearing of equity 
and equity-like financial instruments as well as index futures and index options.  Cboe Clear Europe is 
also recognised as a non-UK CCP in the United Kingdom and as a foreign central counterpart y in 
Switzerland.  
Cboe Clear Europe is a strong supporter of competitive markets, open and non-discriminatory access, 
and investor choice and is an enthusiastic supporter of enhancing  Europe’s clearing framework to 
ensure continental Europe remains competitive globally, progresses to a fully realised Capital Markets 
Union, and increases its attractiveness for global investment.  Cboe Clear Europe has  long 
championed and continues  to champion interoperability across the cash equities asset class. We 
therefore welcome and support the overall intent of the proposed amendments under EMIR 3.0 to 
make European clearing more attractive . We also take the opportunity to suggest some changes to 
the proposed amendments which we believe will support the policy objective s to achieve the goal of 
improving the efficiency of the European Union clearing markets and the attractiveness of European 
CCPs.  
We take the opportunity to highlight the crucial role interoperability and open access have played in 
bringing choice and  non-discriminatory access to clearing for market participants. Interoperability 
greatly contributes to the attractiveness of European markets – as such, actions that seek to 
purposefully disrupt clearing via interoperability should be prevented, since ultimately this leads to a 
reduction of investor choice , increased costs and hampers Europe’s efforts to compete globally. In 
addition, th e regulatory framework for open access should be protected and strengthened rather 
than diluted to ensure that investors can continue to have choice and access affordable clearing. 
Regulatory retracement of the open access framework should be stopped and r econsidered as it 
impinges on the practicality of interoperability arrangements. Furthermore, any exploitation of 
vertical silos (whereby trades are executed and cleared within the same financial market 
infrastructure group) to the detriment of open access and interoperability should be thwarted. Whilst

CCPs more attractive is to be achieved, it is also imperative that CCPs are able to extend their activities

Annex : Cboe Clear Europe Considerations in respect of EMIR 3.0 
 
 EMIR 3.0 Proposed Amendments  Cboe Clear Europe Considerations   
1. Active accounts  
Art. 7a is included to mandate that active accounts be 
established for financial and non-financial counterparties 
clearing certain categories of derivatives contracts. 
Cboe Clear Europe supports continued access between the EU and UK and promotes a consistent approach 
between the EU and UK when it comes to accessing the two markets for clients, clearing members and 
CCPs based in the EU and the UK. We also support the maintenance of a level playing field between the 
EU, the UK, Switzerland a nd jurisdictions deemed equivalent, ensuring the lowest possible amount of 
divergence amongst them in line with the nature of clearing markets. 
2. Authorisation of a CCP and extension of activities and 
services 
Art 14 and 15 are amended to clarify that authorised CCPs 
require authorisation to clear non-financial instruments. 
We respectfully suggest that further clarification could be made to eliminate the need for ESMA Q&A CCP 
Q6(a) and (d) and corresponding answers (pages 51-53 of the ESMA Q&A).  
 
In accordance with the response to ESMA Q&A CCP Q6(a), Article 15(1) could be amended to clarify that 
an extension of authorisation is required where the CCP intends to undertake additional activities or 
services which expose the CCP to new or increased risks.  
 
“A CCP wishing to extend its business to additional services or activities not covered by the existing 
authorisation shall submit a request for extension to the CCP’s competent authority. The offering of clearing 
services or activities which expose the CCP to new or increased risks, such as classes of financial instruments 
with a different risk profile or that have material differences from the CCP’s existing product set, for which 
the CCP has not already been authorised shall be considered to be an extension of that authorisation.”  
 
In accordance with the response to ESMA Q&A CCP Q6(d), the first subparagraph of Article 14(3) could be 
amended to clarify that activities for which authorisation shall be granted must present an objective link 
with the definition of “clearing” under Article 2(3) of EMIR.  
 
“The authorisation referred to in paragraph 1 shall be granted for activities presenting an objective linked 
to clearing, as defined in this Regulation under Article 2(3), and shall specify the services or activities which 
the CCP is authorised to provide or perform including the classes of financial instruments covered by such 
authorisation.”  
3. Authorisation of a CCP, extension of activities and services 
and procedure for granting and refusing authorisation

approval procedures more efficient.

respectfully note, however, that the proposed amendments do not alleviate, and may have the 
unintentional effect of prolonging the timelines even further.

assessment, reviews based on supervisory priorities, requests for approval of changes, regulatory

6. Transparency 
Art. 38 is amended to require clearing members and clients 
providing clearing services to inform their clients of margin 
models and provide a simulation tool.  
Cboe Clear Europe is supportive of the amendments to Art. 38(7) and (8) as they see m to largely mirror

7. Margin requirements  
Art 41 is amended to require the CCP to take into account any 
procyclicality effects when continuously revising the level of 
margins and consider the potential impact of intraday margin 
collections and payments on the liquidity position of their 
participants. 
We support the amendment to Article 41(1) requiring CCPs to continuously monitor margins, and the 
amendment to Article 41(2) which removes parameters from the requirements. 
 
We disagree, however, with the proposed language of Article 41(3): 
 
The second sentence introduces uncertainty  into and a potential conflict with a CCP’s regulatory 
obligations as set out under Article 41(1) which requires the CCP to ensure that exposures with all its 
clearing members are fully collateralised.

The final sentence introduces a ‘quasi’ obligation which in time risks being interpreted as a firm obligation 
which will not necessarily benefit the CC P or its clearing members given that implementation of a same -
day pass -thru mechanism for intra -day variation margin would dramatically increase the number of 
payments to be processed each day – thereby increasing the operational and settlement risks for both the 
CCP and its clearing members. As such, this requirement should not be established without further 
examination to ensure that such risks are mitigated. We note that CCPs’ intraday margin collection 
framework already considers the potential liquidity  impact on its clearing members, and that within the 
bounds of CCPs’ obligations to fulfil the requirements of Article 41(1), CCPs make every effort to not hold 
on to liquidity unnecessarily . Furthermore, for those CCPs that offset a clearing member’s total margin 
requirements with variation margin gains, this benefit would be lost and clearing members will likely see 
a potentially dramatic increase in their total margin requirement and the number of intra-day margin calls. 
We would support a requirement that CCPs ensure variation margin is calculated intra-day and ensure that 
this variation margin is reflected in the intra -day total margin requirement that must be met by clearing 
members. Separately, consideration should be given to whether this would align  within the cash equities 
sphere, with the moves being made towards T+1. 
 
Lastly, we note that a spirational language such as “strive to the best of its ability” raises interpretation 
issues and is, in our view, better placed in guidance and not in legislation.   
8. Liquidity risk controls 
Art 44 is amended to better reflect the entities whose default 
could materially affect a CCP’s liquidity position – require the 
CCP take into account the liquidity risk generated by the 
default of at least two entities, including clearing members 
and liquidity service providers. 
We respectfully disagree with the proposed amendments to the second paragraph of Article 44(1). In our 
view, the proposal is superfluous given the more granular requirements set out in Article 32(4) of 
Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) No  153/2013 (RTS 153). Furthermore, t he use of the term 
“entities” in the proposed language is very broad and introduces ambiguity and uncertainty, for instance 
as to whether FMIs , including interoperable CCPs,  and central banks should be considered as entities. If 
they would be, we could be stepping beyond “extreme but plausible” scenario territory.  
 
Additionally, we do not see how this proposed language furthers the goal of increasing the attractiveness 
of EU CCPs. The proposed language could have the opposite effect by undermining interoperability 
arrangements, reducing competition in the market and undermining efforts to level the playing field 
globally.

10. Review of models, stress testing and back testing 
Art 49 is amended to make the relevant approval procedures 
more efficient. 
We remain concerned that the proposed amendments for those changes considered to be significant 
changes may not alleviate, but rather may have the unintentional effect of prolonging overall timelines 
even further. Shortening the time in which competent authorities must perform their allocated tasks risks 
more of the process being made informal to allow the appearance of the set timelines being met.  
 
Nonetheless, we welcome efforts to streamline the relevant procedures for CCPs to obtain authorisation 
pursuant to Article 49(1a), in particular, the removal of “parameters” from consideration as a significant 
change. We also welcome the introduction of a non-objection procedure for those changes which are not 
significant pursuant to paragraph 1g. We observe, however, that the proposed amendments do not appear 
to recognise that there are categories of changes that would be deemed as neither a significant change 
nor a non-significant change. We therefore support the inclusion of language clarifying that changes not 
qualifying as significant under paragraph 1g of Article 49 and changes not qualifying as non- significant 
under paragraph 1a of Article 49 shall not be subject to any procedures under Article 49. We also do not 
consider the outright rejection of an application where it is concluded that the application does not contain 
the required documents to be particularly helpful to aid a quicker procedure. 
Finally, the catch-all provision in proposed Article 49(1g)(g), which defines as a significant change  “any 
other change to the models that could have a material effect on the overall risk of the CCP” creates 
uncertainty and should in our view be deleted.  
 
We welcome the introduction of regulatory technical standards to specify the list of required documents, 
noting that CCPs are subject to ongoing and comprehensive supervisory scrutiny (e.g., annual article 21 
assessment, reviews based on supervisory priorities, regulatory reporting, ESMA stress testing, requests 
for approval of changes, independent validations), and currently any member of the CCP college has the 
right and opportunity to request information as part of the oversight processes. As such we respectfully 
suggest an amendment to the proposed paragraph 5 as follows:

the list of required documents that shall accompany an application for validation pursuant to paragraph

11. Interoperability arrangements 
Article 54 amended to require ESMA and college opinions 
prior to approving an interoperability arrangement. 
Interoperability provides choice to the markets - it is therefore essential that such arrangements are 
nurtured and encouraged. Although interoperability was established prior to EMIR, the language of Title V 
EMIR has embedded requirements which are subject to ongoing oversight and at least annual review as 
part of the Article 21 assessment process. As such, we believe the proposal should not result in existing 
interoperability arrangements being subjected to further approval processes. In order to address this 
concern, we would support the inclusion of language in Article 54(1) or in the recitals  with such 
clarification.  
 
“Authorised and recognised CCPs that at the time of the entry into force of this Regulation already have 
interoperability arrangements in place shall not be subject to this Article.”

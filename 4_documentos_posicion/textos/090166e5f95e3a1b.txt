 
 
The biogenic carbon cycle 
It is important to acknowledge that natural and technical removals differ. Most notably, natural removal solutions do not have the same 
permanence as technical carbon capture and storage. As such, the forest is not a static sink, it fluctuates over time. Overall, by ensuring 
that forest harvesting does not exceed growth, the forest absorbs as much CO2 as it emits. This is why biogenic emissions are accounted 
for as CO 2-neutral. The natural carbon cycle consists of two key parts: photosynthesis, where the plant absorbs CO 2 and produces 
biomass with the help of sunlight and water; and oxidation, where the plant transforms the biomass into energy and, in turn, releases 
CO2. When the tree dies, or when the wood product can no longer be used, recycled, or reused, the carbon in the wood fibers is emitted 
back to the atmosphere. 
Carbon removals are welcome and necessary 
We need to urgently address climate change and live within 
the planetary boundaries. Stora Enso, therefore, supports 
the EU ’s plans to develop a voluntary Union certification 
framework for carbon removals. It is important to incentivise 
the uptake of carbon storage and ensure that when doing 
so, removals are quantified in an accurate and robust 
manner. 
 
There are numerous ways to tackle climate change. The 
most important one is to avoid and reduce emissions in the 
first place. Second to that, carbon removals are also 
needed. These can be achieved in many ways such as 
permanently capturing and storing carbon through technical 
removal processes including Biogenic-Carbon Capture and 
Storage (BECCS), as well as by ensuring carbon is 
sequestered via natural removals, such as by forest growth 
or through other natural removal methods. In addition, 
storing carbon in products is key in tackling climate change 
as emissions are delayed as long as they remain within that 
product – potentially hundreds of years. 
 
Carbon removals and Stora Enso 
Stora Enso is a renewable materials company that employs 
over 18 000 people in the EU. We deliver products that store 
carbon and provide alternatives to fossil -based products. 
While we are one of the biggest private forest owners in the 
world, we also sour ce wood from other, smaller -scale 
private forest owners. Stora Enso’s whole business, 
therefore, contributes greatly towards carbon removals by 
not only ensuring that we never harvest more than we can 
grow and thus enhance the sequestration in the forest, but 
also by constantly developing our management methods to 
create more resilient trees. This ensures forests can grow 
better to sequester and store more carbon. Once trees are 
harvested, our products continue to store carbon for their 
lifetime, thus also contributing to carbon removals.  
 
Finally, Stora Enso is also involved in developing technical 
BECCS removals from our emissions in the mills. Stora 
Enso is part of a consortium, which has proven that BECCS 
is technically feasible. Now we simply need to make sure it 
becomes financially viable. We investigated the possibility 
and cost of implementing BECCS in our Skutskär (Sweden) 
kraft pulp mill in 2020. The study concluded that it is 
possible to install BECCS with a capacity to remove 1 
million tonnes CO2 annually (all biogenic).  
 
 
 
 
With this expertise and experience outlined above, we 
recommend that the certification framework:  
1. Differentiate between fossil and biogenic removals to 
ensure biogenic carbon removals are not used as a 
cover for continued high levels of emissions. 
2. Differentiate between various carbon farming 
methods. 
3. Clarify that re -certification is possible at end of 
monitoring period. 
4. Ensure the additionality requirements are fit for 
purpose. 
5. Ensure that promotion of renewable materials over 
fossil materials. 
 
Differentiate between fossil and biogenic emissions 
It is important that increased carbon removals are not used 
as an excuse for continued high levels of emissions. If fossil 
emissions continue to increase but are accounted for in the 
same framework as carbon removals, there is a risk that the 
overall accounting of net emissions will wrongfully portray 
an improving picture . This could give the impression that 
emissions are being tackled, when in reality they continue 
to increase, but with less visibility due to carbon removals 
increasing at the same time. It is therefore crucial that the 
accounting of biogenic emissions, an d any removals from 
biogenic sources, are clearly differentiated and accounted 
for separately from fossil emissions reductions. As such, 
any biogenic removals must be accounted for in the Land 
Use and Land Use Change Framework (LULUCF) that 
accounts for bi ogenic emissions. Fossil emissions 
reductions, such as CCS, should be accounted for in the EU 
Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS). This distinction needs to 
be clarified in the proposal to ensure that any potential lack 
of fossil emissions reduction is not obsc ured by combining 
reporting and accounting of biogenic and fossil emissions. 
 
Clarifying that all biogenic removals, such as BECCS and 
other storage, should be accounted for in the LULUCF is 
logical, as biogenic emissions come from renewables, 
grown on land covered in scope of the LULUCF. Using the 
same accounting system and regulation for all biogenic 
storage products, emissions, and possible carbon removals 
from this sector will avoid double -counting in other 
regulations and systems. It will also ensur e that a new 
system does not need to be created once the EU ETS 
comes to an end in 2058, when the EU ETS cap will reach 
zero. Biogenic emissions and renewable products are 
expected to continue in the EU beyond 2058. We, therefore, 
propose that the text sho uld clearly state that biogenic 
removals are included in LULUCF (whilst fossil carbon 
capture storage (CCS) should remain in the EU ETS).

Not one-size-fits-all for carbon farming 
Stora Enso welcomes that natural removal solutions are 
included in the framework as a way of creating more value 
for forests and nature. Payments for ecosystem services by 
forests are clearly needed. To this end, we recommend that 
carbon farming, for which the draft legislation provides a 
single definition, is divided into two more granular segments 
before methodologies are developed. It is important that 
carbon farming in agriculture and forestry are treated as two 
separate concepts because they are substantively different. 
Carbon farming in agriculture could e.g., include p ractices 
such as year -round crop cover, preserving permanent 
grasslands, or converting cropland to fallow or permanent 
pasture, as examples. Rotation cycles for agriculture are 
relatively short (for example with multiple harvests possible 
each year). With carbon farming in forests, however, (with 
much longer rotations), there should be  a focus on long -
term carbon sequestration, which can be fostered via 
enhanced growth of forests. 
 
In addition, the proposal states that the Commission will be 
empowered to a dopt delegated acts to further define e.g., 
the establishment of minimum sustainability requirements 
(Article 7), technical certification methodologies (Article 8), 
and identification of potential eligible carbon farming 
activities in forest ecosystems. Gi ven that forests vary 
across the EU due to differences in geography, soil 
conditions, climate, and history of land use for each 
landscape, it is essential that member states be closely 
involved when developing these more detailed provisions, 
in order to ta ke account of national, regional, and local 
specificities that are relevant for forest ecosystems. This is 
more likely to ensure a robust framework, which delivers on 
the objectives of the Carbon Removal Certification. We, 
therefore, recommend the use of i mplementing acts for 
carbon farming in forest ecosystems. 
 
Greater clarity on re -certification at the end of a 
monitoring period 
There are many factors to consider when identifying 
suitable monitoring periods for carbon removal activities. 
These could range from the risk of reversals due to climate 
change events or long value chains where one operator has 
limited capacity to guarantee the removal. However, the 
activity itself could realistically last much longer than the 
monitoring period. There are referen ces in the proposal to 
carbon being considered released to the atmosphere at the 
end of the monitoring period, although it follows from 
reading the whole text that re-certification for a consecutive 
monitoring period is possible if continued carbon storage is 
proven via uninterrupted monitoring. We recommend 
making the possibility of re -certification more explicit. This 
will provide a clearer basis for secondary legislation to 
ensure that the certification methodologies appropriately 
recognise the multi-decade carbon storage provided by, for 
example, wooden buildings. 
 
Additionality 
A project which generates CO 2 removal credits for the 
carbon sequestered must demonstrate that “it takes place  
due to the incentive effect of the certification” i.e., that the 
CO2 would not have been removed/stored without the 
money from the certificate. Whereas Stora Enso agrees that 
any certification needs to be robust, and that its purpose is 
to encourage additional measures that go beyond today’s 
practices, we believe that the additionality requirements 
must also be workable. If the current wording in the text is  
interpreted in the strictest sense, most carbon removals  
said to be  needed today would not be eligible, as such 
activities already take place (to a greater or lesser extent). 
These include emerging technologies such as BECCS, 
direct air capture, and more established activities such as 
wooden buildings to turn cities into carbon sinks, as well as 
carbon farming . Recognising that many factors , including 
the certification incentive, may partly motivate investments 
in car bon removal activities, would better reflect the 
apparent intention of t he proposal to “positively recognise 
the action of first movers who have already engaged in 
carbon removal activities.” 
 
Promote renewables over fossils 
If we are ever to achieve a green transition, we need to 
move away from a fossil-based linear economy to a circular 
bioeconomy. This means that it is not enough to move to a 
circular economy, we also need a circular economy based 
on renewable raw materials instead of fossils. To achieve 
this goal, the proposal should be amended to include this 
consideration in the sustainability objectives as well as 
regarding the certification methodology. The current 
geopolitical context, with the aftershocks of the COVID 
pandemic and the Russian invasion of Ukraine, has shown 
the need for us to be more resilient to counter act disrupted 
supply chains. Biobased materials have a double 
advantage: they allow not only for the development of a fully 
European supply chain, but also offer an opportunity to 
address resource scarcity and reduce emissions.

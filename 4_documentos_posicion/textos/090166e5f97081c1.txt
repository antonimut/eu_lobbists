EU Commission legislative proposal on EU rules for the 
certification of carbon removals  
 
EDA position  
EDA supports the initiative aimed at establishing common EU standards for carbon removals.  The dairy 
sector acknowledges the potential benefits, as well as the accompanied risks, of a financial scheme that 
rewards farmers for the removal of carbon from the atmosphere. This kind of business model could be a 
beneficial instrument to speed up the carbon removals in the agricultural sector. 
Carbon sinks such as pasturelands  and forests are, amongst others, crucial in the transition towards a 
carbon neutral continent by 2050. Carbon sequestration  and carbon storage  of pastureland has the 
potential to partially offset dairy livestock emissions. Dairy producers can help reduce the greenhouse 
gas impact of their operations through efficient farm management which promotes carbon absorption in 
soils and biomass, of which the most conventional example is permanent grassland. Moreover, major 
dairy producers in Europe have successfully worked on establishing a methodology to quantify carbon 
sequestration on dairy.1 
EDA supports the development of a certification of carbon removals that supports voluntary market 
solutions in which the sector of origin of the carbon credits (such as the agri -food sector) is clearly 
recognised, to acknowledge the efforts of food operators in contributing to the climate objectives. 
 
We ask the EU institutions to consider the following key points when proposing EU rules: 
• Existing national and private initiatives should be taken into account when developing the EU 
framework, combined with the experience already gained in the private market for carbon credits.    
Today a private market already exists for carbon farming and there is already an opportunity for 
farmers to enter the currently unregulated private market for carbon credits. The Commission 
should assure a smooth transition of existing initiatives into the future EU rules and avoid that 
uncertainties about future rules result in stagnation of starting up new initiatives. This underlines 
the need for quick guidance and close dialogue with private actors to identify advantages and 
disadvantages. Regarding Article 5, it is important that existing and continuous practices can be 
rewarded and that there is a national acknowledgment of the EU framework for certification.  
• A high soil carbon content and deep rooting vegetation, such as the one seen in permanent grassland, 
is not only relevant to increase carbon storage in soil and biomass, but also enhances biodiversity 
value of agricultural land and climate resilience against dry and wet weather. Therefore, it is not only 
important to reward increasing soil carbon in the so il, but also reward maintenance of high soil

Definition of carbon farming / Farmers' activities: 
o Carbon farming practices should include efficient farming practices and animal sectors that have 
a positive climate impact and ensure high standards of animal welfare. It will help to create a real 
business opportunity for  every farmer that wants to contribute to achieving the EU's climate 
goals. 
 
o Both carbon removals and GHG reductions should be part of the scope. 
 
o It is important that carbon farming reduces the overall net emissions on farm, without resulting 
in increased emissions in other part of the value chain or farming operations. 
 
o For GHG emissions, voluntary carbon credits currently exist, such as for manure digestion (CDM: 
Methane recovery in animal manure management systems --- Version 21.0 (unfccc.int) and GHG 
Emission Reductions from Manure Management Systems and Municipal Solid Waste – Gold 
Standard for the Global Goals ) and feed additives ( VM0041 Methodology for the Reduction of 
Enteric Methane Emissions from Ruminants through the Use of Feed Ingredients, v2.0 - Verra). 
Another example is the French initiative Label Bas Carbon (LBC). These carbon credits need to be 
aligned, centralized , registered and verified in this EU proposal.  Otherwise, it will lead to a 
confusing situation where there will be two voluntary markets with trade in carbon credits , of 
which one is EU recognized and controlled and the other is not. 
o In order to achieve the intended effect, the Commission should take a holistic view of the farm. 
We would be in favour of having a scheme that looks at GHG emissions and carbon removals from 
farming as a whole, because a carbon removal can also have positive or negative side effects on 
other sources of GHG on farms and on the soil carbon content of unregistered soils. W e would 
like to avoid burden shifting from one parcel to another within one farm. One of the risks is that 
farmers will certify only a few plots of the ir land and put all their fertilizer on these plots to get 
the credits, but on their other plots of land carbon stock will decrease. Even without this proposal 
we see large fertilization induced differences in carbon balances of different farm plots due to 
crop rotations and plot distance to farm (logistics). An example of a side effects is the following: 
converting arable land to grassland to increase soil carbon stock , whereas this  may result in a 
different diet of the cow and subsequent different enteric methane emission s and manure

Method for measuring carbon farming / certification: 
 
o Sampling depth has a large effect on the carbon removal potential of soils , especially regarding 
permanent grasslands with deep roots . Current scientific evidence on the de pth to which 
grasslands are able to sequester i s contradictory. We strongly suggest to the carbon removals 
Expert Group to execute a desk study and initiate additional scientific practical research to 
determine carbon removal at different soil depths for grasslands and to make sure that on one 
hand as much carbon removal can be certified as possible, but on the other hand sampling is done 
consistently. We recommend to take into account the FAO LEAP recommendation on this 
(CA2934EN.pdf (fao.org) recommendation 5: “Soil organic carbon stocks should be reported for 
the 0–30 cm layer to comply with IPCC recommendations, and appropriate error and uncertainty 
should be reported. Soils less than 30 cm deep should be sampled as deep as possible and stocks 
extrapolated to 30 cm. Soils more than 30 cm deep should be sampled as deep as possible, and 
the SOC stock in the 0–30 cm layer shall be reported separately. Sampling to depths greater than 
30 cm or subsampling the 0 -30 layer may be warranted, however the impact of increased costs 
and potential increase in uncertainty need to be considered”.  
 
o We also recommend to shape carbon removal standards in such a way that they can evolve in 
line with new scientific insights on carbon removal potential in the future.

1. carbon can be easily released from the soil if the farmer changes his land management;

carbon credits for the voluntary carbon market , it is important to prove additionality  (i.e., that 
the carbon removal action would not take place without the financial contribution provided 
through such a system). This is to avoid greenwashing. The challenge of proving additionality in a 
policy context with high climate ambitions such as the EU is important to consider, not to erode 
opportunities to tap into the voluntary carbon market.

double counting regarding the carbon footprint of food products. That is also important to 
consider when carbon accounting is used in other policy or methodological areas, for instance in 
the context of the future use of the Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) method. 
 
Coherence with existing/future EU policy frameworks: 
Coherence and alignment with other existing and future policy frameworks, such as the upcoming 
legislative framework on substantiating green claims, should be ensured.  
Regarding green claims and the use of the Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) methodology, our main 
concerns are  that the formula in Article 4 of the carbon removals proposal  requires to include ‘GHG 
increase’, which are also in scope of the PEF methodology.

Conclusions 
The European dairy sector has  been working for years  on enhancing its carbon sinks, and it has also 
significantly reduced its GHG emissions in the past decades. The carbon footprint per produced unit of 
milk in Europe is already among the lowest in the world and dairy farm crop and pastureland offers an 
excellent opportunity to further reduce our climate impact and achieving the EU climate goals. It should 
be recognized that many dairy farms have already sequestered large volumes of carbon in soils, through 
the carbon sequestration of permanent grassland, the use of grass in crop rotations  and the use of 
manure as fertilizer. It is imperative that these good management practices are recognized also in 
certification systems promoting removals. Maintaining high carbon content in soils on farms with 
beneficial crop rotations and management practices for soil carbon sequestration is equally important to 
increasing it in soils that have been eroded through practices reducing the soil carbon content, for 
instance through grain crop rotations with little or no addition of C sources from crop residues and/or 
manure. 
EDA supports the QU.A.L.ITY criteria set in the proposal.  Regarding the sustainabil’ ITY’ criterium we want 
to emphasize that reducing climate impact is only part of our European dairy strategy: the goal of the 
dairy sector is to improve the overall environmental performance of dairy operations. GHG emissions is, 
in fact, only one indicator for environmenta l, economic and social sustainability, and a more holistic 
approach is needed to ensure co -benefits are obtained on other environmental , economic and social 
components such as eutrophication (N and P), land and water use, animal welfare, farmers livelihood, fair 
trade, human rights and biodiversity. The European Dairy PEFCR, approved by EU Commission and 
Members States in 2018 as the reference methodology for the dairy sector, represents the right tool for 
assessing the environmental performance of our products across a broad range of environmental 
indicators and we support  to incorporate carbon removals in this method  to maintain the holistic 
overview of the environmental performance of dairy products.   
The sector is fully committed to contribute to the achievement of the EU climate and environmental goals, 
without compromising on food safety, security, quality, variety  and nutritional value . Moreover, the 
economic viability of farming and processing activities should not be negatively impacted. 
We look forward to our continuous contribution in the EU  institutions work of building a coherent and 
consistent framework of actions to alleviate evitable legal burden, protect the functioning of our 
European Single Market and build a better societal foundation with a scientific sound base.

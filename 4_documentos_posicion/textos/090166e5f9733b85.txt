FEEDBACK ON: FIGHTING HUMAN TRAFFICKING – REVIEW OF EU RULES 
RED CROSS EU OFFICE FEEDBACK : proposal for amending directive 2011/36/EU : 
preventing and combatting trafficking in human beings and protecting its victims  
BACKGROUND  
The Red Cross EU Office (RCEU) represents the 27 National Red Cross Societies in the European Union, 
the Norwegian Red Cross, the Icelandic Red Cross and the International Federation of Red Cross and 
Red Crescent Societies. 
National Red Cross Societies in Europe assist all migrant people in need, without discrimination and 
irrespective of their legal status both throughout their journeys and once they are settled. Several of our 
members across the European Union (EU) provide protection services, psychosocial support, integration 
and legal assistance to identified victims of trafficking in human beings (THB), as well as training for 
professionals working with migrants and asylum seekers, on identifying and referring trafficking cases.   
In the area of anti-trafficking, the RCEU works in close cooperation with the Action for Trafficked persons 
Network (ATN), a thematic network of European Red Cross Societies and other Red Cross and Red 
Crescent actors, aiming to contribute to the reduction of human trafficking and to the improvement of 
protection mechanisms for victims of trafficking. The ATN carries out several activities for anti-trafficking 
practitioners, including exchange of practices, peer learning experiences, joint transnational projects and 
cooperation with other relevant stakeholders.   
OVERVIEW  
The RCEU has engaged in the review process of the EU Directive by contributing to the open 
Consultation held in the first quarter of 2022 and in the research that led to the Impact Assessment 
Report, through two interviews, building on the expertise of National Red Cross Societies in Europe. When 
analysing the different policy options taken into account by the review process, the RCEU advised against 
a revision of Directive 2011/36/EU, underlyning how in our view, the text of the Directive is not outdated, 
and appears flexible enough to include new forms of trafficking and exploitation.  Such opinion was also 
justified by the fear that an unnecessary reform could undermine or delay the implementation of the 
Directive, imposing the creation of new legal instrument and administrative bodies, that could hamper the 
functioning of existing procedures. Conversely, the RCEU contribution emphasised the need for stronger 
non-legislative guidance to EU Member States for enhancing the implementation of the directive. 
Taking stock of the Commission’s decision of proposing an amendment to the directive, the RCEU 
expresses mixed views on the proposed changes. On the one hand, some initiatives, such as the 
mandatory establishment of National Referral Mechanisms, are positive, as they can lead to stronger 
coordination across the EU in the provision of victim support (Art. 11.4). Similarly, improving data 
collection through yearly reporting per each Member State can allow a better understanding of how the 
trafficking phenomenon evolves in Europe (Article 19a). On the other hand, most of the measures 
included in the proposal focus on reinforcing criminal justice response to the trafficking crime. This lack of 
balance represents therefore a missed opportunity to strengthen those elements of the Directive linked to 
prevention and victims’ support. Areas that could have been improved concern, for example, the principle 
of non-punishment of victims (art. 8); the assistance and support to victims of THB (art. 11) – especially 
with regards to granting unconditional residence permits to trafficking survivors; the provisions on 
compensation to victims (art. 17).

In addition, the proposal fails to address the correlation between THB and migration management. Firstly, 
it would have been suitable to ensure a clear link between anti-trafficking framework and asylum 
procedures, enabling timely systematic support to asylum seekers and refugees who have experienced 
trafficking or exploitation. Secondly, the proposal does not recognise the marginalisation of 
undocumented residents in EU Member states as a factor of vulnerability to traffickers. As Red Cross and 
other organisations have advised for long time, addressing the social exclusion of undocumented 
migrants by supporting their access to services, can help reducing their vulnerability to THB.  
In line with the fundamental Red Cross principle of Neutrality, National Red Cross Societies do not engage 
in prosecution and cooperation in judicial processes related to investigating crime. For this reason, the 
RCEU views on the provisions related to criminal justice response to trafficking are only based on the 
impact of these to trafficked persons, in line with a victim-centred approach, that we believe should be at 
the core of all legislation on combatting THB. 
PROPOSED AMENDMENTS TO DIRECTIVE 2011/36/EU  
INCLUSION OF FORCED MARRIAGE AND  ILLEGAL ADOPTION AMONG THE TYPES 
OF EXPLOITATION IN THE DEFINITION OF THE DIRECTIVE  (ARTICLE 2.3)  
Including to the list of trafficking offences ‘forced marriage and illegal adoption” reflect the importance of a 
concerning phenomenon expressed in the fourth report on the progress made in the fight against 
trafficking in human beings (2022). The RCEU welcomes this amendment, which will hopefully help guide 
Member States to focus on these issues, and to support and protect victims. Nevertheless, these two 
acts fall into the definition of trafficking only if exploitative means (threat, force, coercion or abuse of 
power) are used, and if such abusive behaviour is motivated by the purpose of exploitation. Some 
clarification1 on how to identify the victim and the presence of trafficking, especially in the case of illegal 
adoption, should be added.  
REFER ENCE  TO THE ONLINE DIMENSION OF TRAFFICKING ( new article 2a) 
The online dimension of trafficking is not excluded from the scope of the 2011/36 directive: the 
amendment suggested would only ensure due attention from national legislators to online recruitment and 
exploitation. Attention to online trafficking has increased especially in times of the Covid-19 pandemic, 
where movement restrictions led people – including children – to spend more time online, exposing them 
to heightened risks of trafficking. While such amendment is certainly welcome, the directive should stress 
that measures addressing online recruitment and exploitation should include adequate prevention and 
information campaigns. In this sense, wording in article 18.2 should also be strengthened.  
CLEAR ‘OBLIGATION’ OF ESTABLISHING NATIONAL REFERRAL MECHANISMS  IN 
ALL MEMBER STATES ( AMENDMENT TO ARTICLE 11.4)   
The establishment of National Referral Mechanisms (NRMs) in all EU Member States can be a positive 
step if it leads to the harmonisation of victims’ referral to protection and assistance services in cases of 
cross-border trafficking. The explanatory text of the proposal explains also that this amendment is the first 
step to the establishment of a European Referral Mechanism. Some more clarity should be given to how 
such instrument can improve the support of victims. In addition, those Member States who so far have 
not established NRMs have put in place other alternative mechanisms at regional level . We advise that

Lastly, the Commission refers to improving cross-border cooperation as one of the purposes for the

ensure to have the capacity to address them. 
CRIMINALISATION OF PEOPLE  WHO KNOWINGLY USE SERVICES PROVIDED BY 
TRAFFICKING VICTIMS  (DELETION OF ART ICLE 18.4 AND NEW ARTICLE 18A).  
This provision is related to criminal justice, and in this sense, covers an area where National Red Cross 
Societies and IFRC do not engage actively. Nevertheless, there are humanitarian concerns related to the 
impact that a demand-focused approach to exploitation represents.  Based on the experiences of some 
National Red Cross Societies2, promoting exclusively a demand-focused approach may risk making 
undocumented and people with vulnerabilities even more isolated and susceptible to harm. For this 
reason, it is essential to ensure that a ‘do no harm’ approach is taken in consideration within the process 
of amending the directive.    
So far, the “knowing use of services” perspective has been predominantly applied to sexual exploitation, 
although it could cover as well domestic servitude and other forms of labour exploitation. Exclusively 
linking this approach to one type of exploitation risks to conflate human trafficking and prostitution, and 
therefore overshadow awareness and action towards other forms of exploitation. In some of the countries 
that have included this provision in their national legislation – like the Netherlands – the “knowing use”, 
referring to the buyer’s knowledge that the person is a victim of trafficking, has proved very hard to 
demonstrate. On the other hand, a generalised criminalisation of users risks exacerbating abusive 
behaviours towards trafficking victims. Widespread information campaigns on human trafficking, including 
to potential customers, can represent an alternative prevention action that could lead to improving 
general awareness of all forms of trafficking and make the referrals of victims more likely (as customers 
would not be discouraged to turn to the authorities out of fear for prosecution). 
ANNUAL DATA -COLLECTION  AND STATISTICS ON THB (NEW ARTICLE 19A)  
The improved list of indicators for data collection on THB is a very welcome provision, to monitor the 
progress made in contrasting THB and ensure adequate prevention, detection, and protection. The RCEU 
supports this amendment to the Directive. The involvement of Civil Society Organisations and National 
rapporteurs on THB in this provision should be better clarified.

The principle of non-punishment of trafficking victims, expressed in article 8, ensures that survivors of 
trafficking are not persecuted or punished for illegal actions committed while being trafficked. In practice, 
the implementation of this article is inadequate: only a few Member States have introduced specific 
provisions to exempt trafficking survivors from prosecution, which in certain cases is only limited to less 
serious crimes. A recent report of the Special Rapporteur on trafficking in persons, demonstrates how the 
implementation of the non-punishment principle has serious gaps also in EU Member States. Article 8 
could be amended by including a clear obligation to make the non-punishment principle unconditional 
and extend it to all criminal charges, to ensure that victims’ proceedings can be promptly terminated as 
soon as trafficking grounds are found. This would provide clarity and adequate guidance for law 
enforcement officials, prosecutors and judges. 
➢ Improving victims’ access to compensation 
Article 17, defining the compensation to victims, is another provision that so far has not been adequately 
implemented, as indicated by a number of GRETA recommendations. Compensation has an important 
function in the recovery process of trafficking survivors. On the one hand it guarantees economic 
empowerment and reduces therefore the risk of being re-trafficked; on the other, it can have a significant 
function in the victim’s psychological healing. Amendments to this article should ensure that more 
guidance is given on the specific mechanisms to ensure compensation, such as the establishment of a 
national budget to fulfil this goal, foresee adequate information provision to victims on how to access 
compensation, include clear instructions on how to quantify and assess different damages (physical or 
mental harm, loss of earnings, lost opportunities, moral damages, reimbursement of legal fees, etc). 
A feasibility study could be conducted to identify how to improve access to compensation. 
➢ Granting unconditional residence permits to all trafficking survivors   
To provide for durable recovery of trafficking survivors, it is essential to grant them unconditional access 
to residence permits, irrespective of their choice to co-operate with criminal justice. This provision should 
require an amendment of directive 2004/81/EC, which subordinates the access to residence permit to 
their cooperation with the authorities.   
➢ Strengthening prevention measures 
The anti-trafficking directive draws on a framework that includes a range of different policy areas. A 
number of other policy areas should be addressed to strengthen the prevention of THB. More in detail, 
the directive should include proposals  to review other linked legislative areas to address the root causes 
of trafficking. Some examples could cover: 
• Address the factors making people vulnerable to trafficking, such as migration status, socio-
economic conditions, discrimination, in order to mitigate the circumstances that makes some 
target groups more likely to be trafficked; 
• Recognise the intrinsic vulnerability to trafficking of asylum seekers, including due to the 
delays in accessing the labour market, and promote an interpretation of the Reception 
Conditions Directive ensuring timely access to employment. 
The Red Cross EU Office is committed to ensure constructive dialogue on these proposals and will 
engage with National Red Cross Societies and EU co-legislators to ensure a victim-centred approach.

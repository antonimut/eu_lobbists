La Strada International  
European NGO Platform against Trafficking in Human Beings 
 
  
Feedback on the revision of Directive 2011/36/EU on preventing and combating 
trafficking in human beings and protecting its victims 
 
21 March 2023 
 
La Strada International - European NGO Platform against Trafficking in Human Beings - comprises 30 AT NGOs 
in 24 European countries. Our members assist a large part of the detected and assisted trafficking persons in 
Europe each year. Through the direct assistance and prevention work, we monitor the situation on the 
ground. Next to monitoring the implementation of the EU anti-trafficking Directive, since it has been adopted 
back in 2011, we have been closely following its revision process. The evaluation has revealed the remaining 
bottlenecks and the lack of implementation, next to ways for improvement.  
 
La Strada International generally supports the amendments proposed by the Commission, with the exception 
of the binding criminalisation of the knowingly use. While the Directive is an important instrument to combat 
human trafficking and provide support to trafficked persons, we regret that the revision was not used more  
to strengthen trafficked persons rights. The measures proposed focus mainly on reinforcing the criminal jus-
tice response to human trafficking, while we believe the revision should have instead focussed more on im-
proving rights protection for all victims. In particular migrants including asylum seekers, refugees and undoc-
umented persons, who are very vulnerable for trafficking and other exploitative practices, need to have their 
rights protected in order to successfully prevent and combat human trafficking.   
 
As it is stated in the 2021 European Parliament Resolution on trafficking, asylum seekers are particularly 
vulnerable to trafficking,1 due to the need to flee their countries in unsafe conditions, their social and cultural 
isolation,  the lack of access to basic resources and livelihood opportunities, and often the lack of a regular 
status.2 The revised directive should make clear reference to these marginalised groups, to the relation be-
tween human trafficking and conflict and post conflict situations and ensure more linkages between the  anti-
trafficking framework and asylum procedures.  
EU laws and policies must generally address the structural root causes for human trafficking - in particular 
the lack of regular migration opportunities, restrictive permits and border and immigration control practices 
that place people in situations of vulnerability, as well as the continuum of labour exploitation, from a labour 
rights perspective.  
 
To strengthen the rights of victims and related vulnerable groups in the revised directive, we propose revi-
sions of the current articles on non-punishment (article 8); Assistance and support for victims including chil-
dren (art 11, 13 and 14) including their access to residence; victim compensation (art.17) and prevention (art. 
18).

Feedback on the proposed amendments   
 
La Strada International understands the reasons for the amendments to the definition (article 2 of the di-
rective) to include forced marriage and illegal adoption among the types of exploitation, to ensure that EU 
Member States take these forms of exploitation more in account. We however underline that these possible 
forms fall within the definition of trafficking, only when one of the acts, one of the means and the purpose 
of exploitation can be established.  In order to make an illegal adoption fulfil the exploitative purpose element 
of trafficking, the situation would have to be exploitative – or the intent of exploitation should be proven, 
next to the other elements of the trafficking definition. If an adoption process seeks to place a child in a 
situation of exploitation (e.g. for forced criminality, marriage, sexual exploitation or recruitment for the mil-
itary services) then this could likely be recognized as form of trafficking-related exploitation within most legal 
frameworks. As for Forced marriage – when it does not result in any sexual, domestic or labour exploitation 
it would most likely not be considered a case of human trafficking.  
To promote more clarity, there is a need for more guidance for EU MS on the definition and its application, 
including on the interpretation of these forms to avoid different approaches towards the application of the 
definition in the different EU Member States.  
In relation to the definition, La Strada International would welcome to include a reference to labour exploi-
tation or particular exploitative working conditions in article 2, as referenced to already in the EU Employers 
Sanctions Directive3. This is needed, as currently it is very challenging to get severe forms of labour exploita-
tion, including forced labour, recognised as human trafficking. Fortunately, there are some positive develop-
ments in various EU countries to better define severe forms of labour exploitation in national criminal law.  
Reference to labour exploitation in the Directive could encourage more EU Member States to take legislative 
steps and to establish stand alone offences for other severe forms of labour exploitation, including forced 
labour.  
 
La Strada International supports the proposed explicit reference to human trafficking offences committed 
or facilitated through information and communication technologies. Still very little is known on the use of 
technologies for the different forms of human trafficking, also as details on this are often not registered by 
those providing support to trafficked persons. We believe more reliable data needs to be collected, also to 
prevent all kind of claims made that are currently not based on much evidence.  
 
Extending mandates of law enforcement to address online recruitment and online exploitation of persons, 
should be carefully monitored for its impact on data and privacy protection of persons. Measures addressing  
online recruitment, advertisement and exploitation of victims should not lead to violations of rights, or in-
creased for trafficking victims who are undocumented. The same goes for the proposal for an EU wide annual 
data collection on human trafficking. Also here it should be ensured that harm preventive measures are em-
bedded and the privacy of trafficked persons and related vulnerable groups is fully protected.

In order to ensure that national data is fully reliable and comparable, Member States should take the neces-
sary measures to establish national rapporteurs or fully independent equivalent mechanisms and allocate 
adequate resources. Their tasks should include conducting assessments of trends in trafficking in human be-
ings, measuring results of anti-trafficking actions, including gathering statistics in close cooperation with rel-
evant civil society organisations active in this field, and reporting. We further believe that EU Member States 
should take the necessary measures to carry out an assessment on the human rights impact of anti-trafficking 
measures on a regular basis.  
 
In relation to the formalizing and establishment of NRMs, and a possible European Referral Mechanism, we 
believe that civil society actors should be involved in both their development and implementation. Formalis-
ing existing established efficient systems should leave room for different models based on national context.  
A European Referral Mechanism should primarily focus on the adequate and safe referral of victims con-
ducted from a human rights-based approach, including needs and risk assessments. It should not be 
(mis)used as a vehicle for victim return, including an increase of Dublin returns of victims of trafficking. Con-
cerningly we see often how trafficked persons with a Dublin claim are returned to the first EU country of 
entry without being offered the necessary assistance and support.  
 
We welcome the Mandatory sanctions for legal persons, believing that this measure together with the pro-
posed EU ban on forced labour products and the due diligence proposal can hold companies and employers 
more to account. All these proposals we would like to see assessed for its impact on all potential affected 
workers, while we also call for more reference to workers’ rights and engagement of workers in implemen-
tation of proposed measures, as well ensuring that workers have access to remedies in case of exploitation 
or other severe human rights violations, or as a result of the measures, due to which for example they have 
their lost their jobs and income.   
 
To address exploitation, labour rights should be promoted, as well as safe labour migration and sustainable 
corporate governance. It is essential those in situations of exploitation are able to come forward, report 
abuses and file complaints without fear of retaliation or facing other harmful consequences, for example due 
to their migration status.  
 
The proposed amendment to ensure a binding criminalisation of ‘knowingly use  ’of services of trafficked 
persons, we do not support, while realizing that the current national practice in the EU is also not desirable. 
Currently two-thirds of the EU Member States have already introduced this provision in national legislation 
and mostly apply this criminalisation only to users of sexual services. This seems more an attempt to tackle 
prostitution instead of human trafficking.  
 
We oppose the binding criminalisation of knowingly use for several reasons. Firstly, according to research 
conducted by La Strada International, there is currently no proven impact of this criminalisation on combat-
ing human trafficking. There is  only very limited prosecutorial activity and few convictions across the EU also 
because of the practical difficulty to proof the knowingly use. This has also been acknowledged by the Com-
mission.   
 
Secondly for the effective use of this offence, it is needed to firstly successfully prosecute human trafficking, 
which is not simple. As we know investigations and prosecutions on trafficking lag seriously behind. We are

concerned that criminalisation of consumers – who are likely not intending to exploit those that provide their 
services and not responsible for their exploitation  - will take further away attention and resources to ensure 
further progress on prosecuting the real perpetrators of human trafficking. This support the current situation 
of impunity of those responsible for the crime.  We are worried that literary every European citizen, could 
risk criminalisation. Victims of trafficking have been identified in many different labour sectors in Europe, 
both regulated and non-regulated sectors. We would therefore propose to include the requirement for the 
intention to exploit or profiting related to this provision.    
 
Further and this is for us very important, we see harmful side effects of this proposed criminalisation both 
on victims and precarious (sex) workers and other workers. Those selling sexual services are compelled to 
move their activities to more isolated and unsafe places, where they suffer abuses and violation, which often 
goes unreported. Criminalisation results in less access to health, social and legal assistance and significantly 
lower chances to identify individuals who have been trafficked.   
 
Also we see that criminalisation of sex work or irregular work is currently often used for immigration enforce-
ment and used to detain and deport workers without any assessment on their victimhood; and as such there 
are logically low identification figures on trafficking. Criminalising the knowingly use will not give trafficked 
persons more rights, not only is it unclear whether they as witnesses will actually receive access to protection 
and support, also we hear they can be requested to testify and contribute to court procedures against users 
of their services, which can lead to revictimisation.  
 
Instead we see much more added value in other prevention measures, including the structural provision of 
information including by the labour inspectorate and police and the establishment of effective safe reporting 
and complaints mechanisms to ensure that those facing exploitation and abuse can safely report crime. Such 
mechanisms  - including a clear firewall between labour inspection and immigration enforcement activities 
-  are currently absent in most EU Member States.  
 
Proposed additional amendments  
 
In addition to the amendments proposed by the Commission, we would like to propose additional amend-
ments to the Directive in order to strengthen victims’ rights. As for Article 8 related to Non-punishment of 
the victim, this principle is an essential guarantee that trafficked persons are not detained, prosecuted or 
punished for crimes they have committed in the context of trafficking. The revision of the Directive offers 
the opportunity to introduce into the EU legislation more stringent obligations for states to ensure the effec-
tive implementation of the principle of non-punishment, including the obligation to adopt specific penal pro-
visions and prosecutorial guidelines applying to trafficking cases. 
 
We believe there must be a legal obligation to apply the non-punishment provision as early as possible, and 
thus to discontinue any proceedings and any measures implying restrictions of victims’ rights including (but 
not limited to) detention, as soon as relevant grounds have been found. It is important to legally ensure that 
the non-punishment principle applies to criminal, civil, administrative and immigration offences, regardless 
of the gravity or seriousness of the offences committed. Such an approach has been endorsed by the Human 
Rights Council Special Procedures.

The application of the non-punishment provision must be totally unconditional, and it should never be made 
dependent on the victim’s ability or will to cooperate with authorities in criminal investi-gation, prosecution 
and trial. In other words, it should not be used to obtain information in ex-change of immunity. 
 
As for article 11 - Assistance and support for victims of trafficking in human beings, for nearly 30 years we 
have been calling for unconditional support to victims of trafficking. According to its unconditional nature 
(Article 3 paragraph 3 of the current Directive), assistance and support must be provided on the basis of an 
individual assessment conducted according to a human rights-based approach, on a case-by-case basis. Such 
an assessment must take into consideration the victim’s personal situation, which includes a range of circum-
stances, depending on whether it is the victims safety, state of health, family situation or some other factors 
which have to be taken into account”, identify the measures and timing needed for the victim’s full recovery 
and include a risk assessment, as the safety of the victim must always be a primary consideration.  
 
Trafficking should not only be addressed via the criminal justice framework and victims should not be de-
pendent on successful criminal procedures to get access to justice, protection and support. There should be 
an additional pathway, based on a victim’s personal situation, that provides for assistance and support, not 
necessarily requiring their cooperation in criminal proceedings.  Member States should be obliged to estab-
lish systems to ensure effective identification, referral, assistance and support to victims, in cooperation with 
relevant support organisations, including civil society organisations. 
 
It is not to taken for granted that State authorities alone must be in charge of identification. According to 
some national good practices, civil society organisations have an essential role in the first assessment of 
victims’ personal situations and their preliminary or formal identification. Bodies in charge of identification 
and referral could consist of multidisciplinary teams established at the local level, in which relevant support 
organisations including civil society anti-trafficking organisations and trade unions should be represented, in 
addition to institutional actors such as police authorities and labour inspection services.4  
 
The full recovery of victims requires a durable solution in terms of their rights, safety and dignity, and thus 
the establishment of long term measures including access to formal education, training, independent accom-
modation and decent employment as well as residence. A regular residence status is essential to enable 
trafficked persons to claim and exercise their rights.  
 
We believe a new provision on residence permit should be introduced, providing access to residence on 
personal grounds, to ensure that third-country nationals enjoy the same rights set forth for any trafficked 
persons by the directive. Furthermore, trafficked persons must always be allowed to claim international pro-
tection or similar forms of protection provided for by national legislation, including during identification and 
referral procedures.

Additional provisions are needed to ensure identification of victims of trafficking during international protec-
tion procedures, and in more general terms among asylum seekers, even though a formal  asylum procedure 
has not been initiated. In this light, some national experiences have shown excellent results in terms of iden-
tification and referral by authorities in charge of such procedures, as in the case of the cooperation between 
the Italian National Commission for International Protection and UNHCR.5 
 
Furthermore, trafficked persons must always be allowed to claim international protection or similar forms of 
protection provided for by national legislation,6 including during identification and referral procedures. In the 
determination of international protection claims of a trafficked person or a person at risk of being trafficked, 
no requirement should be made of submitting a formal complaint to the authorities about their trafficking 
or their will to cooperate with law enforcement. Such criteria are not included in the refugee definition and 
cannot be considered prerequisites for enjoying asylum or other forms of international protection.7 
 
In some EU Member States assistance and support on grounds of trafficking and on grounds of international 
protection are alternative pathways. It is thus necessary to clarify that trafficked persons who are applicants 
for or beneficiaries of international protection, when grounds exist for both, must be entitled to assistance 
and support measures laid down under anti-trafficking and asylum regimes. Based on a cooperative approach 
between bodies in charge of victim identification procedures and international protection procedures, and 
based on regular cooperation between such competent bodies and support organisations including civil so-
ciety organisations, the best tailored and durable solution for the person concerned must be identified and 
implemented on an informed and consensual basis.  
 
In the light of a better protection of trafficked persons’ rights, they must have the possibility to appeal a 
negative decision regarding, the issuing and renewal of the residence permit, and a withdrawal of a residence 
permit. In order to enable victims to exercise their right to appeal, the above-mentioned decisions must be 
motivated and notified to the person concerned. Moreover, the applicant should be provided with adequate 
information on the appeal procedure and on available legal assistance. Such information includes the proce-
dure for challenging the decision in accordance with national law and information on relevant support or-
ganisations including civil society organisations.  
 
The EU should further create  regular migration pathways and decent work permit schemes across income 
levels, skills and labour sectors to prevent exploitation.

As for article 17 on Compensation to victims, we would like to see more binding measures to enhance access 
to compensation. One of the obstacles to effective compensation is the lack of confiscated assets, for perpe-
trators usually hide their patrimonies. The Commission’s proposal aimed at incorporating into the Trafficking 
Directive the new rules on seizure and confiscation provided for by the Proposed Directive on asset recovery 
and confiscation9 is welcome, as it is expected to ensure early freezing and confiscation of the proceeds of 
crime, and thus compensation to victims. In addition, recovered assets and administrative fines, including 
those imposed on companies, should be used to pay compensation to trafficking victims.  
 
The Dutch law providing for the immediate payment by the State of compensation awarded to a victim as a 
result of a conviction pronounced in criminal or civil proceedings should be a source of inspiration for a similar 
provision to be introduced in the Directive. Subsequently the State can act to obtain the reimbursement of 
the amount advanced to the victim from the convicted physical or legal persons. 
 
All damages suffered by a trafficked person including moral damages, medical expenses and expenses af-
forded for legal assistance and for victim participation in criminal procedures should be covered, which is 
currently often not the case.  We would also like to ensure that legal assistance is provided to victims not 
only to access compensation procedures but also for the execution of compensation orders. In fact it happens 
that awarded compensation orders are not enforced as a consequence of the lack of  a victim’s financial 
resources to afford legal assistance for an execution procedure.  
 
Lastly, as for article 18 on prevention, the current article does not sufficiently address many aspects of pre-
vention. In particular, targeted efforts are needed in the field of labour exploitation, concerning inter alia 
recruitment, due diligence obligations and businesses’ accountability, labour inspections, and public procure-
ment policy. 
 
Prevention includes measures to address factors that make people vulnerable to trafficking,10 including those 
originating in source, transit and destination countries, also related (but not limited) to migration. Among 
them, it is important to mention vulnerability factors linked with systemic issues such as global inequality, 
systemic discrimination, exclusion and poverty. Moreover, particular attention should be paid to causes and 
consequences of conflict11 and humanitarian crisis including displacement and natural disasters. A specific

Conclusion  
 
La Strada International believes the EU Anti-Trafficking Directive is an important instrument to prevent and 
combat trafficking and provide access to justice for its victims. La Strada International generally supports the 
amendments proposed by the Commission, with the exception of the binding criminalisation of the 
knowingly use. We also regret that the revision was not used more  to strengthen trafficked persons rights. 
We see in particular a need to strengthen the provisions on non-punishment (article 8); Assistance and 
support for victims including children (art 11, 13 and 14) including their access to residence; victim 
compensation (art.17) and prevention (art. 18).

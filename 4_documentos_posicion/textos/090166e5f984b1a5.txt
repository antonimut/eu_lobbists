  
Conservation International (CI) welcome s the initiative of the European Commission to propose EU 
rules on certifying carbon removals. As the most recent IPCC report has reiterated, removing CO2 is a 
requirement in addition to immediate emissions reductions in order for climate targets to be reached. 
In this context the development of scientifically robust certification mechanisms is essential to ensure 
support reaches the most impactful forms of CO2 removal. 
 
 
Feedback scope   
 
CI would like to take the opportunity to offer specific feedback on the following definitions and 
article from the current proposal: 
 
From Definitions 
G) Permanent carbon storage’ means a carbon removal activity that, under normal circumstances 
and using appropriate management practices, stores atmospheric or biogenic carbon for several 
centuries, including bioenergy with carbon capture and storage and direct air carbon capture and 
storage;  
(h) ‘carbon farming’ means a carbon removal activity related to land management that results in the 
increase of carbon storage in living biomass, dead organic matter and soils by enhancing carbon 
capture and/or reducing the release of carbon to the atmosphere; 
 
Articles 
[13] 
Atmospheric and biogenic carbon that is captured and stored through a carbon removal activity risks 
being released back into the atmosphere (e.g. reversal) due to natural or anthropogenic causes. 
Therefore, operators should take all relevant preventive measures to mitigate those risks and duly 
monitor that carbon continues to be stored over the monitoring period laid down for the relevant 
carbon removal activity. The validity of the certified carbon removals should depend on the expected 
duration of the storage and the different risks of reversal associated with the given carbon removal 
activity. Activities that store carbon in geological formations provide enough certainties on the very 
long-term duration of several centuries for the stored carbon and can be considered as providing 
permanent storage of carbon. Carbon farming or carbon storage in products are more exposed to the 
risk of voluntary or involuntary release of carbon into the atmosphere. To account for this risk, the 
validity of the certified carbon removals generated by carbon farming and carbon storage in products 
should be subject to an expiry date matching with the end of the relevant monitoring period. 
Thereafter, the carbon should be assumed to be released into the atmosphere, unless the economic 
operator proves the maintenance of the carbon storage through uninterrupted monitoring activities. 
  
Feedback   
 
The approach as currently described above is problematic, inconsistent with IPCC science, 
inconsistent with the accounting precedents recommended by the IPCC for national GHG inventories, 
Ref. Ares(2023)2091272 - 22/03/2023
and although out of scope for the purposes of this initiative it should be highlighted that it is at odds 
with rules for international transfers of mitigation outcomes under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. 
 
- The track record of geologic storage is short and too insufficient to support the assumption 
that storage is permanent.  
- In contrast, there exists abundant data on the duration of storage and the rates of reversal 
for Natural Climate Solutions (NCS) activities, defined above as ‘carbon farming’, going back 
several decades, with transparent and readily observable data available through 
methodologically consistent, vetted global data sources. For example, Global Forest Watch 
provides one such source of data, among many others.  
- The empirical data on NCS durability does not support the assumption that NCS storage is 
100% reversed once monitoring ceases. Two supporting lines of evidence are captured in 
Harris et al. 2021 and Xu et al. 2021. Harris et al. documents that only about 0.1% of forest 
carbon has been emitted annually, on average, since 2000. This means that about 98% of 
the carbon that was in forests in 2000 is still in those forests today. In addition, those gross 
emissions have been more than compensated by gross removals, meaning that net carbon 
stocks have actually increased since 2000, in all biomes. This is a far cry from the assumption 
that forest carbon is always lost whenever it is not monitored. Xu et al. also documents the 
quantity and source of variability in biomass carbon stocks over time (using different 
methods than Harris et al, with convergent results). See Table 2 of that paper and Figure 5, 
which indicates no net trend of loss among the various sources of variability. This suggests 
that the assumption of total loss is vastly overstating the risks for NCS activities – especially 
when conducted at a landscape or national level. 
- The IPCC guidance that countries (including European Union member states) use in their 
own national GHG inventory reporting has numerous instances that contradict the 
assumption of complete loss of carbon stored through NCS activities. The text as it stands 
would be at odds with accepted reporting approaches and would create perverse incentives 
within national GHG accounting. To cite just two examples: first, the guidance has long 
recognized the designation of “unmanaged land,” which is assumed to have neither 
emissions nor removals. Under the proposed text, a plot of land that was restored and then 
left “unmanaged” would be assumed to have 100% of its carbon emitted. The assumption of 
complete loss would remove the incentive to keep such land in protected status (e.g., a 
natural reserve or indigenous territory) after the explicit monitoring of carbon stocks had 
ceased. This would be at odds with the deference that has been shown by previous 
guidance. Second, the guidance provides a means to account for the delayed emissions from 
wood products harvested from forests, by estimating emissions over time through equations 
for exponential “decay” of the carbon stock in harvested wood products. Thus, by assuming 
the 100% loss of stored carbon in (unmonitored) ecosystems, as proposed, the Commission 
would create a perverse incentive to harvest the wood in those ecosystems so that their 
emissions can be accounted in a delayed way, rather than immediately at the end of 
monitoring. This outcome is exactly the opposite to what would be best for the climate.  
- The provisions for transactions under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement use a different 
standard than the one proposed by the Commission. They require these transactions to be 
supported “by minimizing the risk of non-permanence of mitigation across several NDC 
periods and when reversals of emission removals occur, ensuring that these are addressed 
in full.” (In particular, see the Annex to Decision 2/CMA.3). Monitoring could be an aspect to 
“ensure” that reversals are addressed, but on its own, it is not sufficient to meet this burden 
of proof. And these risks could be addressed through other means as well, such as 
conservative crediting and buffer systems. This gives more flexibility to Parties to meet these 
criteria. More to the point, under the Article 6 rules, the absence of monitoring does not 
equate to 100% risk of reversal – there’s an entirely different approach here, one that NCS 
activities can meet. And again, one implication is that the mere assumption that geologic 
storage is permanent would probably not be enough to meet these criteria.  
  
Conclusion   
 
These are not inconsequential points – this would have hugely important and material implications 
for people on the ground and their ability to monetize the climate bene fits of their resource 
management activities. It is enormously important for the accounting of risks to be based on 
empirical evidence and/or robust projections, not assumptions and shortcuts that create an 
artificial bias against NCS.  NCS cannot do its necessary job  as described by the IPCC  if it faces an 
inequitable double -standard based on assumptions unsuppor ted by real evidence. In fact, such 
assumptions could trigger the exact opposite of their intended effect .

 
Feedback on the European Commission’s proposal for a Regulation establishing a Union 
certification framework for carbon removals (CRCF) 
Carbon Gap welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for a carbon removal certification 
framework as a first and significant step toward robust certification, and ultimately, for the necessary 
upscaling of carbon removals (CDR) to meet EU and global climate change mitigation targets. However 
as currently proposed, the framework needs to be strengthened to ensure it delivers its goals and 
does not cause unintended negative consequences on the carbon removal sector in Europe and 
beyond. 
We la id out the case for effective carbon removal certification  in our Guide to Certifying Carbon 
Removal, placing particular focus on the design of the CRCF. Below, please find our seven central 
recommendations for making the currently proposed CRCF as robust and beneficial as possible. 
1) Follow scientific consensus on the definition of carbon removals by excluding emission 
reductions from the definition. 
In its latest report, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) defined  carbon 
removals as “anthropogenic activities removing carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere and 
durably storing it in geological, terrestrial, or ocean reservoirs, or in products .”1 Emission 
reductions from biogenic carbon pools , as included in the pr oposal’s definition of carbon 
removals, do not meet the IPCC’s definition, and consequently should not be included in this 
proposal’s definition.  
 
While both emission reductions and carbon removals will need to strongly contribute to 
meeting global and EU -wide climate goals in line with the Paris Agreement, they provide 
different climate benefits , and should therefore be measured, reported, and rewarded  
separately from carbon removals, especially when considering the risk of emission reduction 
deterrence. Adopting a definition for carbon removals in line with scientific consensus will 
avoid confusion and allow the CRCF to take on a globally leading role in robust public carbon 
removal certification.  
 
2) Establish ground rules  for the use of certificates to prevent greenwashing and emission 
reduction deterrence. 
The CRCF requires provisions determining permitted uses of carbon removal certificates and 
certified units, to prevent mitigation deterrence, greenwashing and the erosion of public trust. 
The current proposal lacks guardrails as to which claims can be made based on the 
characteristics of generated certificates and the CDR methods used to generate them . 
Following the proposal’s current lack of use restrictions , removal certificates could be used 
for emission compensation claims , as well as for a range of other  purposes (results-based 
finance, contribution claims, demonstration of alignment with a policy or requirement, etc.).  
 
When removals are used for compensation, specific rules must apply and will need to be 
spelled out in the CRCF itself. This cannot be left open to interpretation or deferred to other

legislation to regulate, because the rigour (and therefore the cost) of certifying a removal must 
be substantially higher for compensation claims than for other uses. In short: 
- Only unavoidable, expensive-to-abate emissions are permissible to compensate for with 
removals. Other emissions should be reduced or avoided, not offset. 
- Fossil fuel emissions can only be compensated for with highly -durable, permanent 
removals where the long -term storage of carbon matches the lo ng-term impacts of 
emissions. 
- Emissions from the land sector could be balanced by removals into the land sector. The 
EU already separates the LULUCF pillar from industrial sector emissions. Rather than risk 
crossing that divide by trying to sell land-based removal into an undifferentiated voluntary 
market, other options to incentivise the removals that fall under the “carbon farming” 
label should be developed . Many such options exist already, from re -gearing and 
increasing Common Agricultural Policy incentives, to dedicated reforestation schemes, to 
other direct public and private incentives in which contributions are made to fund 
ecosystem restoration, but no emissions are compensated for.  
 
3) Clearly distinguish between storage types. 
The current proposal separates the diverse group of CDR methods into three categories, based 
on their storage type: 1) carbon farming, 2) carbon storage in products, and 3) permanent 
carbon storage. However, these three carbon stora ge categories do not clearly differentiate 
CDR methods based on their carbon storage dura bility nor separate biological from 
geochemical storage media. Separation of these storage media is essential as the need and 
difficulty of MRV vary significantly between CDR methods based on their stora ge media, 
especially as the reversal risk of biologically stored carbon is (generally) significantly higher 
than the reversal risk of geochemically stored carbon.  
 
The current categorization furthermore is unclear regarding which CDR method falls under 
which CDR category. While the proposal’s associated impact assessment sorts a range of CDR 
methods into the three categories, some CDR methods  could fall into two or all three CDR 
categories, dependent on their exact application (see e.g. biochar). Furthermore, questions 
remain as to wh ich CDR categories emerging  CDR methods may fall under once ready for 
wider application and certification. The certification framework and its category definitions 
should consequently remain flexible to reflect changes in available CDR methods and the 
latest scientific consensus on carbon storage durability.  
 
4) Put in place a definition o f reversals an d assign s tewards for remediation of potential 
reversals. 
As absolute permanence of carbon removals can unfortunately not be guaranteed and storage 
durability varies significantly between CDR methods, it is essential to include : i) a definition 
on what constitutes a reversal, ii) which remediation actions need to be taken should reversals 
occur, and iii) who is responsible for taking appropriate remediation actions at each specific 
point in a removal activity’s and certificate’s lifetime. This is necessary to ensure that climate 
benefit, once generated and reported  (and potentially claimed for offset or contribution 
purposes), can be relied upon to persist, and to be immediately re-established should reversal 
of carbon removal occur. 
 
5) Define clear additionality criteria. 
In order to ensure that carbon removals activities certified under the framework are 
additional, strict criteria defining additionality need to be put in place and enforced. This is 
necessary to ensure real climate benefit towards the net -zero greenhouse gas emission goal 
goal originates from each certified unit, and to avoid greenwashing. 
 
 
6) Refine geographical scope provisions. 
While the current scope of the proposed certification framework is limited to carbon removal 
activities taking place within the European Union, it is essential to consider parts of the supply 
chain, which may be most efficiently place d outside of the European Union. An example of 
this could be  the creation and operation of sufficient geological CO 2 storage capacity in 
locations with more abundant storage capacity and better explored storage conditions, such 
as Iceland or Norway. Another open question remains in regards to the potential sourcing of 
biomass from outside the EU for CDR purposes, and its consequential consideration under the 
certification framework. 
 
7) Introduce safeguards ensuring a social and just transition. 
Carbon removal activities, like most economic activities, require a range of resources, 
including land, energy, and water , and are expected to affect local communities , e.g. due to 
land use and economic activity changes . Local communities may therefore be concern ed in 
regards to potential adverse social impacts or risks associated with the conduct of carbon 
removal activities, such as CO2 leakage from storage or transport infrastructure. Social and 
environmental safeguards need to be established, ensuring that these concerns are taken into 
consideration and  thoroughly addressed, and that potential negative impacts for local 
communities are minimized while social and environmental benefits are maximized.

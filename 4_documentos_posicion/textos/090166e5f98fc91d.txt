 
 
Repsol recommendations on the EU Commission’s proposal for a 
Regulation on an EU certification for carbon removals 
Repsol welcomes the Commission’s initiative to develop a regulatory framework for the certification of carbon 
removals to deploy a harmonized EU certification system to ensure the role of carbon removal solutions, both nature-
based and technology -based, ne cessary to contribute to achieve the EU’s climate targets. Repsol would like to 
emphasize that GHG emission reductions must be prioritized to reach climate neutrality and that Carbon Dioxide 
Removal (CDR) is a supplementary measure. 
Reaching climate neutra lity by 2050 will require all readily available, scientifically proven, net -zero compatible 
technologies to be developed and deployed at scale. Thorough and scientific carbon accounting accompanied by full 
life-cycle analysis (LCA) needs to be at the basis of any regulatory framework that will support the removal of carbon 
dioxide (CDR). 
As the deployment of industrial solutions will require time, infrastructure and massive investment, it is necessary that 
the Regulation on the certification of carbon removal provides legal and financial certainty to the industry. This means, 
defining a clear framework from the establishment of the certificates to their implementation, while developing 
minimum standard requirements to provide a necessary basis for continuous innovation.   
The Regulation should ensure consistency and complementarity with other EU policies like ETS or LULUCF. And, while 
the Regulation’s focus is on carbon removals, is necessary to be aligned with future CCUS policies. 
It’s also important to develop a framework to deploy carbon capture and use and/or storage (CCUS), whose transport 
and storage infrastructure, and the scaled -up capture technologies, would enable and enhance the deployment of 
certain technology-based carbon removal solutions (e.g. BECCS, DACS). This infrastructure will be necessary to store 
both fossil and biogenic or atmospheric carbon, enabling both emission reductions and carbon removal respectively. 
This framework should outline the synergies between carbon removals and  CCUS activities. In addition, any 
harmonized EU certification system to ensure the role of carbon removals should be coherent with CCUS certification 
systems.

Prioritize the avoidance of emissions and circularity of carbon 
In parallel to the deployment of industrial solutions for carbon removal, we believe that the priorities of both the 
industrial sector and the EU should be: 
Avoid emissions in the first place, notably by reducing the carbon intensity of production processes and value chains, 
while removals will need to focus on hard -to-abate emissions. Capturing sources of concentrated CO 2 at industrial 
point sources, which is less costly and energy-consuming then Direct Air Capture, should be prioritized.  Avoided GHG 
emissions through Carbon Capture and Usage need to be appropriately recognized by the Emissions Trading System 
(ETS) Monitoring and Reporting Regulation (MRR). 
Increase the circularity of the carbon already circulating in the economy  will contribute to reduce the EU’s 
dependence on fossil carbon. To this end, capturing CO 2 from industrial processes and using it as circular feedstock 
for chemicals and plastic needs to be recognized appropriately in the EU ETS framework by using robust GHG 
accounting based on the origin of the carbon and the release into the atmosphere as a basis.   
  
Criteria for a robust EU certification system: the QU.A.L.ITY criteria  
 
The proposed definition of CDR (Carbon Dioxide Removal) includes the concept of reductions as well. We recommend 
a clear and exhaustive definition to provide clarity to ongoing CCS projects and policy discussions, enable large-scale 
deployment and avoid costly, ineffective, and sometimes even counterproductive measures. It is crucial to have clear 
definitions of removals before integrating CDR into the EU’s existing climate architecture.  The definitions in the 
proposal do not clarify whether some solutions such as biochar and the biogenic part of waste -to-energy with CCS 
would be eligible.  
 
Certification of CDRs must be based on high -quality robust and transparent systems for monitoring, accounting and 
reporting, where CDRs are verified through a full life -cycle analysis of the climate impact. Double counting must be 
avoided. 
  
 QUantification: In order to deliver unambiguous benefits for the climate, carbon should only be considered 
as “removed”, if it causes a net reduction in atmospheric CO2 concentration. Clarity should be given on 
whether the EU standard or the national standards should prevail for baselines and references, where one is 
more rigorous than another 
 
The carbon is either captured directly from the atmosphere (through Direct Air Capture) or it comes from a 
biogenic source. Tailored certification methodologies should clarify the rules applying to capture and storage 
of biogenic carbon. CCS applied to bioenergy is not the only potential form of carbon removal. Other sources 
of biogenic CO2 capture with durable storage, in line  with the IPCC definition of carbon dioxide removal, 
should be considered as removal as well as bio -based materials with a demonstrated long -term storage 
capacity. For example, hydrogen and ammonia production approximately represent a quarter of the EU 
chemical industry’s direct emissions. Considering capture and storage of CO2 process emissions when 
biomethane is used for steam reforming would give reassurance that investment into carbon capture

technologies and storage infrastructure can get a return on investment, even if the share of natural gas in the 
energy mix goes down in the longer-term.  
 
 Additionality:  
Further guidance is needed regarding how the projects are supposed to meet national statutory requirements. 
We consider, in relation to section 1. b) from Article 5, that there must be certification for carbon absorption 
produced as a result of incentives provided by existing certification schemes, such as the one created by the 
Spanish Office for Climate Change that was founded in 2014, which allows for projects created since 2012. 
We also consider that said criteria should not exclude projects developed in reforested areas. 
 
We understand the principle of additionally for removals into natural sinks. However, such concept is not 
applicable as such to technological carbon removals through technological solutions. As carbon removals in 
industry are negligible today, the relevant baseline is effectively close to zero.  
 
 Long-term storage: We believe it is important to clarify the contribution of circularity to the carbon removal 
objective. The contribution of the chemical sector and its products to balance remaining emissions is clearly 
recognized in the Commission’s long-term strategy, which indicates that the chemical sector would be carbon 
negative by 2050.  
 
If atmospheric or biogenic carbon is captured into products, ensuring their circularity will help keeping this 
carbon into “carbon chemical pool” instead of re-emitting it. As recycling loops may not be perfect and result 
into losses (i.e. partial release of carbon into the atmosphere), certification systems should be able to account 
for these losses. This could be addressed with the Commiss ion establishing threshold standards per 
methodology, as is for example already done with carbon stored in products in buildings. The methodology 
and threshold would have to be specific to the product but in the end should reflect the principles of long -
term storage, end use and application and risk mitigants.  
 
We find that the notion of “carbon storage in products” is not adequate in order to describe a carbon removal 
activity. Carbon storage in products equals the very notion of organic chemistr y. As the proposed regulation 
is only about atmospheric or biogenic carbon, we believe that “carbon removal products” would be more 
appropriate.  
 
There is a need to define duration of storage for a method to be considered as long lasting. Durations other  
than ‘permanent’ should be detailed in order to account for the varied durability of the different activities 
covered under this framework.  
 
 SustainabilITY:  Repsol agrees with the application of the “do no significant harm” principle within 
certification systems.  
We consider that the delegated and implementation acts reflected in the proposal must guarantee that the 
principle of sustainability, protection and restoration of biodiversity is applied to different types of 
plantations, and that it must also take into account a potential for later exploitation. In this regard, there are 
well recognised studies that correlate forestry exploitation as a factor of forest resilience, reducing their 
vulnerability to extreme climate events and forest fires.

Regarding the plantation of productive species, we consider that efforts related to the maintenance and 
exploitation of these species not only increases forest resilience, but it also provides incentives for the 
development of reforestation and carbo n capture projects. Therefore, we consider that efforts should be 
made to plant autochthonous species and naturalized species. 
Regarding the interoperability of registers 
We consider it crucial that the interoperability of the different certification schem es and registers is guaranteed. In 
this sense, we support that the registers that exist in EU Countries find within the implementation acts considered in 
Article 12, technical criteria to guarantee the interoperability of the registers. We believe it is of vital importance that 
these requirement groups seek to reduce the proliferation of parallel registers. 
About certification and verification 
Finally, we are in agreement with the notion expressed in Article 11, whereby public and private certification schemes 
must be subject to external audit. In this sense, we believe that the efforts of duly accredited private professional 
entities for the certification and verification of projects should be valued, since they can contribute to decongest public 
efforts on the matter. We find it positive that the texts argue for both public and private systems to be subject to 
these requirements.

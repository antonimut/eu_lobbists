IFOAM Feedback to the public consultation on the certification 
framework of carbon removals 
 
 
While climate mitigation has to clearly prioritize absolute emissions reductions, the trend of declin ing carbon 
stocks in EU soils has to be reversed and carbon sequestration should be incentiviz ed. The organic movement 
believes that a certification framework for carbon removals should contribute to transition farming systems 
towards sustainability, prevent greenwashing and a further intensification of the agricultural sector.  The 
adoption of beneficial management practices for biodiversity protection and carbon sequestration in the 
agricultural sector should be encouraged and farmers should be remunerated for their efforts.  
  
1. Carbon farming has to positively contribute to biodiversity enhancement and ecosystem protection   
The climate and biodiversity crisis are highly interlinked, and it is essential to consider climate and biodiversity 
as part of the same complex problem.1 Looking at the issue from a single perspective and solely focus on carbon 
sequestration does not take other environmental impacts of the land use s ector such as nitrate leaching into 
groundwater, air pollution, soil health or biodiversity loss into account. It is crucial to ensure that carbon farming 
contributes to enhancing biodiversity and to the protection of ecosystems. The legislation needs to b e explicit 
on how carbon farming has to contribute to environmental objectives besides carbon sequestration and needs 
to go beyond a vague “do no harm” principle . In the communication by the European Commission on 
‘Sustainable Carbon Cycles’ it is also men tioned that carbon farming has to be done ‘[…] in respect of ecological 
principles favourable to biodiversity and the natural capital overall’ . 2 The preservation of biodiversity and 
resilient ecosystems is essential and a prerequisite for carbon storage, the adaptation to consequences of climate 
change such as extreme weather events and the provision of clean water, air and healthy soils .  
  
2. Protection of frontrunners and not penalizing first movers 
The efforts of frontrunners, such as organic farmers, who are already contributing to enhanced carbon stocks 
have to be recognized. Soil health is a cornerstone of organic agriculture, and organic farmers have already in 
the past applied management practices that are enhancing soil fertility and are increasing soil organic carbon 
stocks.3 This reduces the potential of organic farmers to store additional carbon in their soils until saturation 
levels are reached. It needs to be ensured that those past efforts are recognized and that those who are already 
contributing to enhanced carbon sequestration are not penalized. A carbon farming scheme should not favour 
those who have failed to take action in the past. The actual soil carbon sequestration potential is very site-specific 
and depends beside previous management practices also on climate and soil conditions.  The increasing impact 
of climate change might make it already challenging to maintain carbon stocks, let alone increase them. 
Beneficial management practices, however, need to continue in order to maintain soil organic carbon stocks and 
protect them in the long run, while no additionality can be demonstrated.  Ongoing support is needed to ensure 
the continuation of good practices. Any perverse incentives have to be prevented in this respect.  
 
3. Management-based approach is better suited for land-based removals 
The fluctuation and  high uncertainty of natural sinks  regarding the quantification of carbon stocks and 
sequestration, the long -term, non-permanent and reversible nature of soil carbon storage leads to many risks 
when favouring a result-based approach. A management/activity-based approach could differentiate beneficial

4. Ensure a systemic transition towards organic farming and agroecology 
In order to  move the farming system towards sustainability the change of single practices is not enough. 
Therefore, a systemic transition  towards organic farming and agroecology  needs to be incentivized that 
contributes to all of the objectives of the European Green Deal and the Farm to Fork – strategy. This is especially 
important given the fact that  land-based removals, such as  sequestering soil organic carbon , agroforestry or 
removals in biomass, can be easily reversed by a change of management practices. Stopping beneficial practices 
or applying practic es that counteract sequestration, immediately leads to a release of carbon back to the 
atmosphere. The climate benefit can therefore be lost very easily. To reduce this risk and ensure a continuation 
of beneficial practices, organic and agroecological farm ing have to incentivized of which sustainable soil 
management is an integral part. 
 
5. Lack of social safeguards  
Besides adequate environmental safeguards, the proposal also lacks any social safeguards. It is, however, crucial 
to ensure that any adverse social outco mes as a consequence from carbon farming activities are prevented. A 
clear risk that could arise from selling carbon removal certificates is the commodification of land and 
consequently an increase of land prices. Higher land prices severely and immediate impact the possibility of 
farmers to access land accessibility.  Land accessibility is already an issue for many farmers, being especially 
difficult for young and new farmers. The legislation should include clear safeguards to prevent such impacts on 
land prices resulting in land grabbing and reduced land accessibility. 
  
6. No delay in emissions reductions  
The absolute reduction of GHG emissions always has to  be the clear priority of climate policy. Carbon removals 
can only be additional to reduction efforts and should not disincentivize them. Carbon stocks in soils are currently 
declining in the EU and it is therefore important to reverse this trend and incre ase those carbon sinks. Science 
shows very clearly that land cannot compensate for delayed emissions reductions in other sectors. 4 It is of 
outmost importance that carbon removals do not delay any efforts of reducing emissions.   
 
7. GHG reductions are different from removals 
While the reduction of GHG emissions has to be the main priority of climate mitigation, they are inherently 
different to removals. They are both needed to ensure that the agricultural sector is  contributing to the EU  
climate objectives, but it is important that emissions reductions and removals are not mixed up . It is crucial to 
avoid an approach that only looks towards GHG intensity  and neglects other negative impacts of agriculture on 
the environment. Focusing solely on  the amount of carbon being stored and on  GHG emissions could lead to 
incentives counteracting biodiversity , animal welfare objectives and the aim to reduce the  use and risk of 
synthetic pesticides. The organic movement believes that a ll negative impacts of agriculture have to be 
addressed together.

Many crucial aspects are not tackled and are left open in the proposal. Among those aspects is the  use of 
certificates which is not mentioned in the legislative proposal. However, the potential use of the certificate has 
an immediate impact on the methodology and defines it. Different uses of carbon removal certificates might 
require different approaches. These aspects should already be defined within the legislation. It is also important 
to ensure that any use of carbon removal certificates is excluded that leads to greenwashing. Furthermore , the 
proposal does not define who will be liable in the case of a release of carbon and what appropriate liability 
mechanisms are. Especially important to further discuss are those cases which are determined by environmental 
impacts that are beyond the farmer’s control. 
 
9. Carbon markets are not an adequate financing tool 
The organic movement doubts that voluntary carbon markets are the right tool to provide reliable and adequate 
funding to farmers for their provision of public goods.  The uncertainty of the  carbon price, the  exact 
sequestration rate and the associated monitoring, reporting and verification costs pose uncertainties and a risk 
to the profitability for farmers. Carbon certificates from agriculture should not be an easy way for other sectors 
to reduce their emissions reductions efforts by buying certificates from  climate action in agriculture . 
Furthermore, if certificates are sold to other sectors, the removals do no longer count towards the climate effort 
of the agricultural sector. The focus should lie on how new and existing public funds, like the Common 
Agricultural Policy (CAP), can be used to best incentivise favourable management practices. IFOAM Organics 
Europe stands for the principle that public money has to deliver public goods .

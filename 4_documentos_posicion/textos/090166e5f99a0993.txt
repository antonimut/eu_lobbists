 
 
GDPR: procedural rules on cross-border enforcement; DOT Europe position 
With the General Data Protection (Regulation 2016/679, GDPR) approaching five years since its formal 
entry into force, DOT Europe  welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to support the robust 
enforcement of the GDPR and believes that the time is right to consider the operation of this landmark 
regulation to see what worked well, what did not and what could work better  as well as of course 
identifying why . Although aspects of the GDPR are still being clarified via EU jurisprudence and 
Supervisory Authority (SA) guidance and enforcement, one issue that has consi stently suffered in its 
application is the one-stop-shop (OSS) mechanism, including dealing with cross-border cases. 
 
The OSS is one of the main achievements of the GDPR. It is worth recalling that the mechanism was 
introduced to enhance consistency in app lication, legal certainty and reduce the administrative 
burden for controllers and processors, while also resulting in significant added value for individuals in 
the EU, who could now benefit from central enforcement of the right to data protection . The 
implementation of the GDPR has, in practice, led to a less than ideal operation of the OSS mechanism. 
We believe the OSS has not achieved its full capacity, where resistance from  certain SAs  has 
contributed to complex procedures that do not deliver the legal  certainty and mutual cooperation 
intended by the GDPR.  
 
Against this backdrop, DOT Europe welcomes this targeted consultation but is troubled by the ongoing 
focus on enforcement as the primary measure of success of the GDPR. The legislators’ goal in adopting 
the GDPR was broad – to promote a high and harmonized level of the right to data protection across 
Member States; clarify  legitimate uses of data; introduce more effective transparency and control for 
data subjects;  and strengthen legal certainty for compliant organisations. Narrowly focusing SAs’ 
efforts on enforcement, in particular enforcement by means of administrative fines,  risks detracting 
from other important regulatory objectives central to the EU data protection framework: upholding 
the accountability principle ; enabling amicable resolution ; and promoting compliance by issuing 
targeted and actionable guidance  and enforcement actions not restricted to administrative fines . 
These are important tasks of SAs and the E DPB, and this consultation should take care to position 
enforcement action as one of the mechanisms through which the GDPR can achieve its policy goals  
rather than overshadow these other crucial regulatory activities. Failure to strike this balance sets a 
concerning precedent for EU regulation more broadly, and this could chill investment and growth in 
the EU’s digital economy , while creating a process that does not efficiently and effectively meet EU 
policy goals. 
 
Our contribution will touch on the following aspects of the OSS mechanism: 
• Its significance for data subjects and organisations in the EU; 
• Taking unnecessary burdens off SAs; 
• Procedural deadlines; 
• Promoting confidentiality; 
• Streamlining how parties are heard during the process; and 
• The role of the EDPB and other related issues. 
 
OSS mechanism: its significance for data subjects and organisation in the EU 
The OSS mechanism is essential to the GDPR’s goal of providing a high common level of data 
protection for all data subjects in the EU. The centralised enforcement led by the LSA ensures that 
regulatory decisions are implemented across Member States, reducing fragmentation a nd uneven

outcomes for organisations and businesses in the EU. The OSS mechanism, paired with the consistency 
mechanism, also provides clarity to data subjects as to the competent regulator and by ensuring a 
consistent and efficient application of data protection rules across the EU.  
 
The OSS mechanism is also particularly important for companies with pan-EU business operations. It 
provides operational efficiency, management of cost and legal certainty via one ‘lead’ SA being their 
single point of contact. Similarly, smaller companies established in one Member State and providing 
services across t he EU benefit from the OSS provision as it significantly reduces the complexity of 
compliance for nascent businesses.  Without this mechanism, they would be obliged to engage with 
multiple SAs, which would not be  commercially viable. A well-functioning OSS mechanism has the 
potential to reduce  red tape and confusion on the competency issue and to avoid conflicting 
approaches by SAs. 
 
DOT Europe observes that most SAs support and respect the OSS principle described in the GDPR and 
consider that a single establishment (acting as a controller  / processor  or an establishment of a 
controller / processor outside of the Union) triggers the OSS mechanism on the basis of article 56(1) 
GDPR. Certain SAs, however, seem to deviate from this mechanism and add further requirements for 
such establishments that go beyond what is included in the GDPR and relevant case law. DOT Europe 
recommends that the European Commission clarify that organisations that have a single 
establishment in the Union benefit from the OSS mechanism on the basis of Article 56(1) GDPR, 
without needing to meet further or heightened criteria which are not provided in the GDPR. 
 
Another relevant issue that merits clarification is the GDPR provisions on local cases, which can 
undermine the lead SA concept. Despite the existence of the OSS mechanism under Art icle 56(1) 
GDPR, DOT Europe members have observed cases where other SAs approach organisations arguing 
that a case is considered “local” and, as such, they are empowered under Article 56(2) of the GDPR to 
investigate. This derogation from the OSS mechanism is often difficult for organisations to reconcile. 
DOT Europe thus believes that more clarity is needed on the aspects of the relevant Article, which are 
underlined below: “By derogation from paragraph 1, each supervisory authority shall be competent to 
handle a complaint lodged with it or a possible infringement of this Regulation, if the subject matter 
relates only to an establishment  in its Member State or substantially affects data subjects only in its 
Member State.” 
 
In addition, DOT Europe continues to maintain that the SA of an organisation’s main establishment 
should be the only competent authority for decision -making, including for managing complaints and 
deciding on appropriate sanctions. This SA should coordinate joint actions ; be the main competent 
authority for decision-making; and have a leading role in any cooperation procedures with other SAs 
in relation to an organisation whose main establishment is within its competence. This is preferable 
to a model of co -competence, which would weaken the OSS mechanism and introduce more 
uncertainty, inefficiency, and costs in the procedure.  The European Commission initiative should 
preserve and strengthen the OSS mechanism and not afford more powers to the wider SA community 
to review and comment on the factual findings, draft determinations and ultimate sanctions during 
the course of lead SA investigations. 
 
Although the European Commission has noted that it wants to focus on administrative measures, 
such as introducing procedural deadlines, clarifying the position of complainants in the procedural 
steps and streamlining how parties are hear d during the process, DOT Europe would invite policy 
 
 
makers to first reaffirm and strengthen the OSS mechanism which reinforces the European single 
market. 
 
Taking unnecessary burdens off SAs  
The consultation notes a lack of uniformity between SAs as to amicable resolution. DOT Europe invites 
the European Commission to recommend that all SAs adopt this process as a means of strengthening 
the accountability principle and ensuring that less compl ex data subject complaints are resolved 
quickly. This is in line with proposals made by the EDBP, who supports the authorisation of Amicable 
Settlements for non-contentious cases. This would ensure that SAs’ resources are focused on the most 
egregious breaches. 
 
As a first step, we believe that all SAs should require data subjects to exhaust company complaint 
mechanisms first, before considering a matter for further investigation. This will ensure that non-GDPR 
matters are identified and redirected to the o rganisation concerned, and that SAs lean on the 
accountability principle to encourage rapid resolution of less complex matters and ongoing 
improvement of compliance. 
 
Once company complaint mechanisms have been exhausted, organisations should be given the 
opportunity to resolve all complaints amicably, including cross-border cases. However, any framework 
to strengthen the amicable resolution process should ensure that settlements in cross-border cases 
are not burdened by too far -reaching coordination obliga tions. Otherwise, this could unreasonably 
deprive the lead SA of its prerogatives and could have the effect of turning settlements, which are 
intended to be quick and simple solutions in the interests of both data subjects and data controllers / 
processors, into longer and more complex processes. It could also deter the amicable settlement of 
disputes, an outcome which would clearly be contrary to the objectives of the GDPR. 
 
Relatedly, it should be made clear that enforcement action is a measure of last resort. SAs should be 
required to consider a full range of measures available in the GDPR, such as formal warnings or 
correction notices. An outcome -focused approach should exhaust other measures before using 
administrative sanctions. For cases that create serious and measurable real harm for individuals and 
where sanctions are appropriate, the EDPB should develop and publish a clear, predictable, consistent 
and proportionate  model to calculate fines giving clear consideration for mitigation factors and  
independent of statutory maximums.  
 
Moreover, when company complaint mechanisms and amicable resolution procedures have been 
exhausted DOT Europe agrees with the importance of  setting thresholds for admissibility of 
complaints of all kinds – domestic as well as cross -border. For those complaints deemed to require 
further action, it is important that SAs adopt the same, high thresholds for admissibility and that they 
are applied consistently to provide the intended legal certainty.  Thresholds should also apply to own 
volition investigations.  Application of these thresholds should be actively monitored and assessed to 
ensure fairness and consistency.  
 
However, it is common for non-GDPR complaints and legitimate but less complex in-scope complaints 
to be held up in long queues in the originating SA, causing unnecessary delays in responding to the 
consumer. Cross-border complaints can remain ongoing for long periods of time, som etimes due to 
inaction by the complainant or the originating SA. In line with the desire to clarify when a complaint 
should be dismissed or terminated, DOT Europe invites the Commission to recommend that cross -
 
 
border complaints should be automatically clos ed if the complainant or the originating SA fails to 
respond to the controller/processor’s response within a defined period.  This issue, together with 
admissibility criteria is highlighted by the EDPB as a point of divergence between MS, which should be 
addressed.  
 
Procedural deadlines 
We understand that the European Commission is considering whether procedural deadlines are 
necessary to tackle undue delays and dispa rities in the finalisation of cases. Such deadlines are 
considered as a way to alleviate concerns that the process is moving too slowly. 
 
We would ask the European Commission to consider that the length of time required to complete 
each procedural step dep ends on the complexity of the subject matter of the inquiry in question.  
Deadlines, while potentially useful, should thus not be arbitrary and applicable across the board, but 
strike the right balance between being prescriptive and allowing for the complexities of each case to 
be reflected. This applies to various stages of the process, such as commencement of an investigation, 
issuing of a draft decision, and preparation of a revised draft decision. 
 
We would also ask the European Commission to be mindful  that providing for arbitrary and fixed 
deadlines for procedural steps which apply to all inquiries with out regard for the complexity of the 
subject matter, would likely operate to the detriment of the party under investigation and undermine 
the fair, properly reasoned, efficient, and consistent application of the GDPR. It is relevant to note that 
the exercise by SAs of their powers must be “ subject to appropriate safeguards, including…  due 
process set out in Union and Member State law in accordance with the Charter” (Article 58(4) GDPR). 
We trust that the European Commission will take into account parties’ right to due process when 
considering whether procedural deadlines are necessary. 
 
Promoting confidentiality 
DOT Europe does not object to having complainants being appropriately involved in the procedure, 
but we believe that certain safeguards must be put in place. This is where harmonisation can be most 
beneficial, in laying down rules on what constitutes the minimum scope of the file (e.g., required types 
of documents to be included) . Any proposed harmonisation  of such processes, however,  must be 
compatible with Member States’ national laws. 
 
On the question of access to the procedure, we would like to highlight the importance of 
confidentiality to ensure due process is achieved at the decision-making stage of the OSS mechanism. 
We have seen cases where confidential information on ongoing investigations was intentionally made 
available to the public and media outlets. While we respect the right to information and transparency, 
the disclosure of confidential documents in the context of investigations can compromise the integrity 
of the process by placing undue pressure on the decision-makers and exposing commercially sensitive 
information. As the Commission reviews the rules that can enhance GDPR enforcement, DOT Europe 
would welcome further considerations on confidentiality rules in the context of the OSS mechanism 
and appropriate sanctions for wilful breaches . As the European Commi ssion reviews the rules that 
can enhance GDPR enforcement, DOT Europe would welcome further considerations on 
confidentiality rules in the context of the OSS mechanism. 
 
Streamlining how parties are heard during the process 
 
 
DOT Europe believes that the right to be heard needs further adjustments to ensure that it is available 
consistently throughout the process, even where cross-border cases are concerned. 
 
We maintain  that the right to be heard covers both the factual and legal elements raised in the 
investigation; and that parties should be able to make written and / or oral submissions, as 
appropriate. The right to be heard on cross -border cases should be extended to  any Article 65 
proceedings and a right to be heard before the EDPB and not be limited only to a right before the SAs. 
Any harmonisation of rules on the right to be heard that do not extend to the EDPB would undermine 
the right. This is especially pertinent in cross-border investigations where the EDPB has the power to 
resolve disputes between the SAs, where it would be necessary for the parties concerned to be able 
to make representations. 
 
Current procedural rules only allow parties under investigation to  make submissions to the lead SA 
before its draft decision is finalised. However, it is not uncommon to have the EDPB adopt an entirely 
new position without any representations by the parties concerned. In the experience of some DOT 
Europe members, there h ave been cases where the EDPB issued binding decisions based on an 
inaccurate or incomplete understanding of the facts  - issues that could have been addressed if the 
right to be heard was applicable.  In practice, this would mean that a party would have to be given 
access to the provisional views of the EDPB, so they can address any outstanding points before a 
decision is adopted. An obligation should be set for hearings to take place before the draft decision is 
prepared and shared with concerned DPAs. 
 
Furthermore, scope, modalities, and timing of hearings should be further clarified. A level of flexibility 
should be maintained, for example regarding the format of hearings . Summaries of hearings should 
be provided to all parties. Parties should be given the  right to make representations to supervisory 
authorities before formal action is taken. In addition to this right, any proposed rules must ensure that 
organisations in an investigation have access to all the evidence to be considered in their case. 
 
DOT Europe believes that the right to be heard in front of the EDPB is very important, considering 
the substantial administrative fines and other effects its decisions can have on a party under 
investigation. Allowing for oral and / or written submissions would ensure that decisions are based 
on accurate and complete facts and that any concerns of the EDPB and the parties can at least be 
addressed. 
The role of the EDPB and other issues 
Following from the emphasis placed on the lead SA, it is important to al so consider the role of the 
EDPB in the process.  DOT Europe has maintained that the EDPB needs to be an exceptional 
mechanism and not function as a general appeal body for legitimate decisions taken by the lead SA 
consistent with their duties under GDPR.  This weakens the OSS mechanism and delays enforcement 
activity. The OSS  mechanism was designed with cooperation and  mutual assistance in mind, and 
concerned SAs already actively work together with the Lead SA towards OSS decisions . If the use of 
this dispute mechanism (Article 65) is not limited to exceptional cases only, we believe that the le ad 
SA principle will be u ndermined and cooperation between SAs would become ineffective. There are 
also practical consider ations; the EDPB does not have the resources to efficiently consider multiple 
referrals under the Article 65 process. If not reserved for genuine circumstances  of disagreement 
between SAs, decision-making would slow down to the detriment of all parties concerned , reducing 
 
 
trust in the OSS. This has already been recognised in the European Commission’s own call for evidence 
for an impact assessment.  
 
Related to  this point, the urgency procedure should also not be used as an alternative to the SA 
leading on an issue. Cases that justify the urgency procedure should be extremely limited to serious 
cases in need of immediate remedial measures where the competent SA is proven unable to take such 
measures in time. Moreover, to preserve the OSS  mechanism and the adequate assessment of the 
matter, the measures should be provi sional and the competence to adopt final measures should 
always stay with the competent SA. 
 
Furthermore, it is important to remember that the EDPB is not competent to investigate or conduct 
any fact-finding exercise in the course of its functions. Fact-finding falls solely within the competence 
of the lead SA. Some of our members have experienced cases where they consider the EDPB to have 
strayed into exercising fact -finding powers, which is not permitted under the GDPR . Relatedly, the 
EDPB has in certain cases exercised regulatory overreach by directing lead SAs on how they should 
use their corrective powers and conduct their investigations. In such situations, there is no direct 
recourse for appealing or challenging the EDPB’s directions, resulting in a legal void and limitation of 
affected parties’ rights. We would welcome alignment of the scope of the EDPB’s role and 
accountability with the GDPR . The justifications for such an alignment are practical as well as legal. 
Regarding fact-finding functions: (i) neither the EDPB secretariat nor the SAs acting collectively in the 
forum of the EDPB have the resources to, in addition to discharging their domestic responsibilities, 
also undertake the extensive complex work that would be required to conduct their own independent 
investigations; (ii) a second fact -finding exercise would be duplicati ng efforts;  and (iii) would add 
unnecessary delays to the decision making process.  
 
DOT Europe recommends that the European Commission looks into the circumstances which trigger 
the responsibility of the EDPB . The process would benefit from a clearer definition  and a more 
targeted approach that limits EDPB involvement in more targeted cases  and does not undermine 
the lead SAs independence and competence. Furthermore, the urgency procedure and any evidence 
of abuse should also be examined as part of this initiative; as should be cases where the EDPB may 
have acted beyond its remit by conducting investigations.

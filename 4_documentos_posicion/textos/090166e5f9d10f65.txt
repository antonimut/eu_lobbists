 
  
 
EMMA-ENPA written contribution to the Commission’s consultation on the proposed 
revision of the legislation on the classification, labelling and packaging of chemical 
substances and mixtures (Regulation (EC) no 1272/2008 of the European Parliament 
and of the Council) 
 
 
1. Introductory remarks  
 
The free and independent press is financed to a considerable extent by advertising. Without 
advertising revenue, many newspapers and magazines would not be financially viable . For the 
printed press , titles are financed about half by subscriptions and retail sales and half by 
advertisements. In some segments, especially the specialised press, the share of financing 
through advertising is significantly higher. In particular, d igital offerings of newspapers and 
magazines are financed by more than 90 % through advertising. 
Against this background, the existing compromise between obligatory product information and 
free media financing through advertising found at EU level must be maintained. This compromise 
ensures on the one hand that all European consumers across all distribution channels are informed 
about energy efficiency, hazards or other characteristics of the products, also through prominent 
graphic labels. The same applies to product brochures, etc. This likewise ensures that all citizens 
are fully informed before making a purchase. Advertising in the media, on the other hand, remains 
free of obligatory information, or at least free of prominent information in graphic form  such as 
labels, pictograms, etc. that interfere with  the advertising message. At most, purely textual 
references to efficiency class, dangers, etc. appear reasonable. 
Within the framework of the revision of Regulation (EC) No. 1272/2008 on classification, labelling 
and packaging of substances and mixtures (CLP Regulation), the mandatory information in media 
advertising (Art. 48) is to be further extended, inter alia via the mandatory inclusion of graphic 
pictograms in media advertising.  Mandatory information in media advertising  risk endangering 
the free financing of the media through advertising and should therefore be rejected, especially at 
at time where the diversity of the press in its various forms is facing historic challenges. 
In keeping with aforementioned comments, EMMA-ENPA will focus its feedback on the proposed 
changes to Article 48 relating to advertising in the existing regulation.   
 
2. No further advertising restrictions within the framework of the revision of the CLP 
Regulation 
 
According to Article 48(1) of the CLP Regulation, it is necessar y to indicate the corresponding 
hazard classes or hazard categories when advertising hazardous substances. When advertising 
hazardous mixtures, according to para. 2, an indication of the hazard properties has so far only 
been required in the context of distance selling. This measure is aimed at ensuring that consumers 
buying online can access the same level of information as consumers buying the product in brick-

and-mortar stores, where the labels present on the products are clearly visible and can be 
assessed in person.  
However, since no graphic design of the indication of the hazard classes or hazard categories 
concerned is required by law, the current text still ensure a balance between consumer information 
and freedom for the press to gain revenues from advertising. We regret that this compromise is 
now to be deviated from within the framework of the revision of the CLP Regulation. 
According to the proposal of the EU Commission, the obligatory information for every 
advertisement for a hazardous substance (Art. 48 para. 1) or a hazardous mixture (Art. 48 para. 
2) shall be significantly extended. In future, the graphically highlighted hazard pictogram, the 
corresponding signal word, the corresponding hazard class and the hazard statements are to be 
included in all media advertising.  
In our view, such an extension of the compulsory information is disproportionate and does  not 
appear sensible. As the compromise outlined above shows, mandatory particulars at the point of 
sale, in distance selling, in manuf acturers' and distributors' materials can achieve the legitimate 
aim of informing consumers before they decide to buy  a certain product. In contrast, mandatory 
disclosures in media advertising  appear unnecessary, interfere with media freedoms and  could 
even prove counterproductive. 
Before envisaging disproportionate advertising restrictions, it should first be ensured that existing 
information deficits of consumers are eliminated when making purchasing decisions. The new Art. 
48a inserted in the EU Commission's proposal provides, for example, that the applicable labelling 
information must also be provided in the case of distance selling offers.  
However, especially in view of the financing possibilities of the free press, further compulsory 
information in me dia advertising should be avoided. Without advertising revenues, many 
newspapers and magazines would not be able to sustain their financing. For advertisers, placing 
ads generally becomes unattractive if ads or product campaigns are overloaded with mandatory 
information. Prominent graphics, labels, pictograms, etc. are particularly intrusive. It is above all 
the printed and digital press that, with editorial freedom, provides most of the information to draw 
attention to and educate about sustainability, pol lution-free environment, etc. Therefore, any 
sensible consumer policy must refrain from further restricting advertising in the media. 
Moreover, media advertising does not have the function of informing about all positive and negative 
aspects of a product. Advertising aims at bringing attention to the advertised product or offer, 
which usually does not lead to an immediate decision, but directs the interest to the advertised 
offer. 
Furthermore, the final report of the Forum's pilot project on the CLP Regulation, which focused on 
the control of internet sales by the European Chemicals Agency, has shown that the existing 
information obligations, inter alia in the area of advertising regulations, are hardly complied with. 
Before imposing further information obligations in media advertising to the detriment of press and 
media freedom, the focus should be on enforcing the existing regulations and closing information 
gaps in purchasing decisions accross all distribution channels. 
For this reason, we reject the disproportionate extension of information requirements in advertising 
in Art. 48, Recital 29 and 30 and would support the provision of complete information to 
consumers across all distribution channels, product brochures, etc.

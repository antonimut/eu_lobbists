The German Bar Association (Deutscher Anwaltverein – DAV) is the professional 
body comprising more than 61.000 German lawyers and lawyer-notaries in 253 local 
bar associations in Germany and abroad. Being politically independent the DAV 
represents and promotes the professional and economic interests of the German 
legal profession on German, European and international level.  
 
The DAV welcomes the objective to allow EU public limited liability companies to 
create multiple-voting shares in order to promote access to the capital market, but at 
the same time to provide for certain restrictions to protect the rights of the other 
shareholders and to prevent undesirable developments. However, it does not seem 
convincing to the DAV to limit this - as currently stated in the Draft Directive - to such 
companies seeking admission to an SME growth market for the first time, while for 
other public limited companies it shall remain prohibited to issue multiple-voting 
shares. The objective of the draft Directive to open up a level playing field for EU 
companies in terms of access to the capital market through the possibility of creating 
multiple-voting shares and the high standard of protection that is already achieved 
and guaranteed by the provisions of capital market law, company law and group law 
and which is to be expanded by further protective measures of the Draft Directive, 
make it appear appropriate to extend the scope of application of the Draft Directive to 
all companies in the legal form of a public limited company, SE (if not already 
covered) and KGaA (“Kommanditgesellschaft auf Aktien” – German partnership 
limited by shares). At the same time, however, the Draft Directive itself should specify 
the quantitative restrictions on the creation of multiple-voting shares provided for in 
Art. 5(1)(b) by certain thresholds.  
 
A. General Introduction 
 
The maxim "one share - one vote" has a long and eventful history. However, for 
decades the development has clearly been pointing in the direction of a strict 
implementation of the principle without exception. This development may now have 
come to an end and may even be reversing itself - both at national and European 
level, as the present proposal for a Directive demonstrates. 
 
 
 
Seite 5 von 21 
 
A one-share-one-vote rule can already be found in the first modern codification of 
company law in Germany - the relevant provisions of the ADHGB of 1861 - in Art. 
190 ADHGB. However, at that time it was possible to deviate from the (dispositive) 
legal principle and to provide for multiple-voting shares in the statutes. The 1884 
amendment to the law of shares and subsequent reforms did not lead to substantial 
changes. As a result, in the Weimar Republic in the years after 1919, shares with 
multiple-voting rights were widespread in about one-third of public limited liability 
companies. This is explained by a certain concern - which still exists to some extent - 
about an actual or supposed "sell-out" of the German industry. It was feared that 
foreign companies could acquire shares in German companies on a large scale in an 
unstable economic environment. In order to prevent this and to protect the voting 
status quo, multiple-voting rights were used on a large scale.  
 
However, the instrument of multiple-voting rights was also increasingly used by public 
administrations to shield themselves from criticism, control and intervention. The side 
effects of these efforts became so evident in the Great Depression from 1929 
onwards that multiple voting rights fell into disrepute not only in Germany but also in 
other countries. However, it must be born in mind that at that time there were no 
highly developed capital markets with differentiated protective instruments with 
regard to capital market law. Flexible barriers to voting rights (voting prohibition, 
fiduciary duty, etc.) and protective mechanisms of the corporate law in order to 
mitigate majority rule were not well developed at that time. Moreover, the self-
regulatory forces of the capital markets (expectations of institutional investors, 
recommendations of voting advisors, requirements for inclusion in a stock exchange 
index, etc.) were still significantly less developed than today. Consequently, there 
was a concrete need for other regulatory instruments. This is why Section 12 para (2) 
sentence 1 of the German Stock Corporation Act (AktG) declared multiple voting 
rights inadmissible in 1937. However, exceptions remained admissible upon  
approval by the authorities if the exception was necessary for the welfare of the 
company or overall economic interests. Furthermore, existing multiple-voting shares 
remained untouched. 
 
The complete abolition of the multiple voting share was already called for in the 
context of the 1965 reform of the German Stock Corporation Act. In the end,

however, only the provision providing for the exception was made more stringent to 
the extent that in section 12, para 2, sentence 2 AktG, "the well-being of the 
company" was no longer sufficient for a ministerial or official waiver (of the 
prohibition), as it had been in the 1937 AktG, but only "the safeguarding of overriding 
macroeconomic interests". Accordingly, the desire of family societies, for example, 
for "protection against alienation" was no longer sufficient in itself to obtain a 
dispensation. 
 
The German Control and Transparency in Business Act (KonTraG) of 1998 was a 
preliminary terminus of the development in German law. Henceforth any exemption 
by the ministry or another authority was excluded (section 12 (2) AktG). In addition, 
section 5 (1) of the Introductory Act to the German Stock Corporation Act (EGAktG) 
stipulated that multiple voting rights expire on 1 June 2003, unless the general 
meeting has previously declared their continuation by a majority of at least three 
quarters of the capital stock represented at the time of the resolution. The 
explanatory memorandum to the KonTraG summarizes the arguments put forward 
then and now against the admissibility of multiple voting rights as follows: They 
“contradict the idea that in the public limited company the voting power should in 
principle be based on the number of shares held with voting rights. The granting of 
influence without corresponding share ownership does not correspond to the 
expectations of the capital market and weakens owner control ... The repression of 
differentiations in voting right resulting only from the articles of association serves the 
further standardisation of the share as an investment security", (BT-Drs. 13/9712, p. 
12). 
 
Although these concerns still carry weight, they have only limited persuasive power 
today. This applies above all to the argument emphasized in the explanatory 
memorandum to the KonTraG that the multiple voting right contradicts the 
expectations of the capital market. The fact that such expectations are difficult to 
determine and are subject to change over time is illustrated precisely by the present 
proposal for a Directive. It highlights how the capital markets' view of multiple voting 
rights has changed, at least in certain constellations. Thus, the Draft Directive on 
multiple voting rights is part of the EU's package of measures on stock exchange 
listing, which is intended to make the public capital markets more attractive for EU 
 
 
Seite 7 von 21 
 
companies and to make it easier for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to 
raise equity capital on the capital market. In addition, in view of the advanced 
internationalization of the capital market, experience in other legal systems plays an 
important role. In this respect in particular, however, there have been important 
changes in recent years and decades that make the assumption of fixed expectations 
of the capital market appear questionable. Thus, with varying restrictions, around 
50% of EU member states now permit multiple-voting shares, and in the UK dual-
class shares can now also be admitted to the top segment of the stock exchange 
(premium listing), albeit only under very restrictive conditions. Last but not least, in 
the USA, which has the most important capital market in the world, listed companies 
can be provided with multiple-voting shares in a legally secure manner. In view of 
these findings, there is evidence to suggest that the introduction of multiple voting 
rights should be viewed with a fundamentally more open mind. Their acceptance 
should be left largely to the self-regulatory forces of the capital markets and not be 
dictated by a legislative assessment that excludes multiple voting rights. Provided 
there is sufficient transparency, investors can then decide for themselves whether 
they want to participate in companies with multiple voting rights or not. In addition, 
there is a justified expectation that institutional investors and voting advisors will in 
any case bring the "expectations of the capital markets" to the attention of company 
management and ensure that structures that contradict these expectations do not 
prevail.  
 
This does not mean, however, that multiple-voting rights should be introduced without 
any accompanying protective instruments. The negative experiences of the past 
already argue against this. The efficiency risks with regard to the owner control, 
which are also mentioned in the explanatory memorandum to the KonTraG, must still 
be taken seriously. If the synchronization of control and economic risk is lost, there is 
a risk, that shareholders privileged through multiple-voting rights will not act with due 
diligence, as wrong decisions will not be made at their expense or, if at all, in an 
inadequate proportion. The excesses observed in Germany in the 1920s - the 
insufficient sense of responsibility already noted by contemporaries among some 
shareholder groups who hardly bore any personal capital risk as a result of multiple 
voting rights - are certainly not a model for today. Then again, a complete 
liberalization of multiple-voting shares is not up for debate at present. The state of 
 
 
Seite 8 von 21 
 
minority protection achieved today and the present proposal for a Directive with its 
extensive catalogue of supplementary protective measures differs seriously from the 
legal framework of the 1920s. With this in mind, the DAV shares the view that a 
complete ban on multiple voting shares is no longer appropriate. An appropriate 
liberalization is rather indicated in order to facilitate access to the capital market for 
companies in the European Union, to create a level playing field within the EU and to 
counteract a worse position of EU companies compared to companies from third 
countries. 
 
B. Comments on the provisions of the Draft Directive  
 
Art. 1 and 3 Draft Directive 
Scope; adoption or continuation of national provisions on multiple voting 
rights 
 
Art. 1 defines the subject matter and scope of application of the Draft Directive. It 
aims at creating uniform structures within the EU with multiple-voting shares in 
companies that are applying for admission of their shares to trading on an SME 
growth market in one or more Member States and whose shares are not already 
admitted to trading on any trading venue. This is intended to achieve a minimum 
harmonization at the European level for such companies. At the same time, Art. 3 
provides that Member States may introduce, adopt or maintain national provisions 
allowing companies to establish structures with multiple-voting rights shares even in 
cases not covered by the Directive. By doing so, the Draft Directive aims to take into 
account the different legal cultures and company laws of the Member States in the 
area of multiple-voting rights and to ensure a sufficiently flexible implementation 
process. In member states such as Germany, where multiple-voting rights have not 
been permitted so far, it is upon the legislator to create the possibility of multiple- vote 
rights in the cases covered by the Directive. Beyond that the legislator is free to 
introduce multiple-voting rights as an option under company law or to continue to 
prohibit them. 
 
The DAV has serious concerns about the approach of the Draft Directive to allow 
multiple-voting shares only for companies seeking a listing on an SME growth 
 
 
Seite 9 von 21 
 
market. The Draft Directive hereby leaves room for national regulations that exclude 
the creation of multiple-voting rights if a company seeks listing in a higher-value 
market segment. It would be possible to upheld national regulations excluding 
companies from multiple-voting rights structures which are already listed on an SME 
growth market or which are listed in another stock exchange segment. This 
regulatory approach of the Draft Directive leads - to the detriment of all capital market 
participants - to an unequal treatment of companies seeking a listing in a higher-
quality market. It further leads to unequal treatment of companies whose shares are 
already admitted to an SME growth market or a regulated market. Finally, public 
limited companies that do not seek a listing for their shares at all would be treated 
unequally as well. From the point of view of the DAV, there is a need for a 
substantiated justification for these restrictions. The draft directive does not contain a 
solid justification for the appropriateness and necessity of these restrictions. 
 
This applies first of all to the restriction that only the listing on an SME growth market 
is to be privileged, but not the listing in higher-quality stock exchange segments. In 
Germany, this would currently only concern the “scale” shares (a segment of the 
german Stock exchange for SMEs) as part of the Open Market of the Frankfurt Stock 
Exchange. Why should it be possible for national legislators to impose the SME 
Growth Market segment on companies seeking the creation of multiple-voting shares 
for the purpose of listing and to close higher-value segments to them? The DAV does 
not see a convincing reason for this; instead, this element of the Draft Directive stops 
half-way on the path to the goal of creating a level playing field for European 
companies. 
 
For the same reason, the DAV is not convinced by the Draft Directive only providing 
for the admission of multiple-voting shares for companies whose shares have not yet 
been admitted to trading. Even if the shares have already been admitted to trading, 
there may be a reasonable interest in granting multiple voting rights to existing 
shares or in issuing new multiple voting shares by way of a capital increase. 
Particularly in the case of international mergers, a lack of flexibility in the creation of 
multiple-voting shares can be a noticeable competitive disadvantage vis-à-vis other 
EU legal forms and vis-à-vis the legal forms of third countries.

Given the numerous changes in the history of multiple voting rights and given the bad 
experiences made by several member states with this instrument (the prohibition), it 
is crucial to decide if it is justified that member states may upheld the prohibitions of 
multiple-voting shares. There are good reasons to consider it as necessary and 
appropriate - as the Draft Directive does - to admit multiple-voting shares in the 
future.  
The risks of multiple-voting shares are sufficiently countered by the progress of legal 
development, by appropriate additional instruments for protection and by 
transparency for investors. But in this case, one has to bear in mind the objective of 
the Draft Directive to create a level playing field for European companies. The DAV 
consequently deems it necessary not to limit the mandatory admission of multiple-
voting shares to companies seeking an stock exchange launch on an SME growth 
market, but to allow the establishment of multiple-voting shares also with regard to 
companies seeking the first-time admission of shares in another stock exchange 
segment or whose shares have already been admitted. 
 
As a consequence, one has to decide on how to treat companies whose shares are 
not listed on a stock exchange and that don’t seek to be listed neither. Although such 
companies are outside the objective of the Draft Directive, the admission of multiple- 
vote rights for listed companies requires that the creation of multiple-voting shares is 
admitted also and a fortiori for non-listed companies. 
 
All in all, the DAV advocates that the scope of application of the Draft Directive 
should not be limited in the manner envisaged, but should be extended to all public 
limited companies (and KGaA (German partnership limited by shares), see in this 
respect the following comment on Art. 2). Should the Draft Directive adhere to its 
narrow scope of application, an appeal should already be made to the German 
legislator to repeal the ban on multiple-voting shares (while at the same time creating 
appropriate protective provisions; cf. the comment below with regard to Art. 5). This 
would mean a complete change of position of the German legislator compared to the 
way which has been pursued for decades and which was reconfirmed with the 
adoption of the KonTraG (‘German Control and Transparency in Business Act’). On 
the basis of appropriate safeguarding measures made possible by Art. 5, this seems

systematically preferable to a solution limited to companies in a certain market 
segment that are willing to be admitted. 
 
Art. 2 Draft Directive 
Definitions 
 
According to the definition of "company" contained in Art. 2 lit. a), which refers to 
Annex I of Directive 2017/1132, only stock corporations fall within the scope of 
application of the Draft Directive, but not partnerships limited by shares. Even though 
the SE is not explicitly listed in Annex I of Directive 2017/1132, it is likely to be 
covered by Art. 10 SE Regulation. A differentiation between public limited-liability 
companies, SEs and partnerships limited by shares would in any case not be 
objectively convincing. If it can be argued for the partnership limited by shares that 
there is no need for multiple voting rights in this legal form, since the influence of the 
founding shareholders intended by multiple voting rights can be ensured through the 
influence executed over the general partner, this argument does not apply to the SE 
from the outset. Additionally in the case of the limited shareholders, whose legal 
position is also governed by stock corporation law, a transfer of the multiple voting 
right appears systematically and dogmatically more convincing than a restriction of 
the multiple voting right to the stock corporation. If the Draft Directive does not make 
improvements in this respect, the German legislator should in any case make use of 
its right under Art. 3 and extend the possibility of introducing multiple voting rights to 
the partnership limited by shares and the SE. 
 
Art. 4 of the Draft Directive 
Admission of multiple voting shares 
 
These provisions are a consequence of the limited scope of the Draft Directive. If the 
Directive is extended to all public limited companies and KGaA (German partnership 
limited by shares) irrespective of the question whether and in which segment their 
shares are or are to be admitted to stock exchange trading, Art. 4 is superfluous.

Art. 5 Draft Directive 
Ensuring adequate protection of shareholders 
 
1. Para. 1 
 
Pursuant to Art. 5 para. 1 of the Draft Directive, Member States shall be obliged to 
provide for various protective measures in order to ensure equal treatment of 
shareholders, adequate protection of shareholders who do not hold multiple-voting 
rights and the protection of the company. 
 
a) Letter a) (resolution requirements) 
 
According to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a subpara. 1, both the introduction of multiple-
voting shares and the modification of existing multiple-voting shares in a 
manner that affects the voting rights shall require a resolution of the general 
assembly adopted by qualified majority in accordance with the applicable 
national law. If several classes of shares exist, according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a 
subpara. 2, such decisions (resolutions) shall be subject to a separate vote for 
each class of shareholders whose rights are affected. 
 
According to the Draft Directive, the first introduction of multiple-voting shares, 
whether by way of conversion of existing ordinary shares into multiple-voting 
shares or by way of a capital increase upon granting multiple-voting shares, 
requires a resolution of the general assembly adopted by a qualified majority. 
The same shall apply to any change in the structure of multiple-voting rights 
that affect voting rights, which would presumably already include the issuing of 
new ordinary shares diluting the voting weight of the multiple-voting shares. 
This is appropriate regarding both, the shareholder as well as the protection of 
the company, and would be legally required in any case under the German 
Stock Corporation Act as a resolution amending the articles of association 
according to section 179 (2) sentence 1 AktG or section 182 (1) sentence 1 
AktG. However, according to the Draft Directive, it is not entirely clear whether 
it would be compatible with Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a subpara. 1 to provide for a lower 
capital majority in the articles of association pursuant to section 179 para. 2 
 
 
Seite 13 von 21 
 
sentence 2 or section 182 para. 1 sentence 2 AktG; in the opinion of the DAV, 
a dispositive amount of the capital majority in the Articles of Association would 
also be in conformity with the Directive since the Draft Directive explicitly 
refers to the applicable national law. 
 
The DAV believes that the following question has to be discussed: Is a 
resolution of the general assembly (adopted with a majority to amend the 
Articles of Association) sufficient, when only some of the ordinary shares are 
to be subsequently converted into shares with multiple-voting rights or when 
the subscription warrants are to be excluded at the moment of issuing of new 
shares with multiple-voting rights, both leading to an unequal treatment of the 
shareholders. Under German law, it is questionable whether, in addition to the 
resolution amending the Articles of Association (statutes), the consent of all 
disadvantaged ordinary shareholders is required or whether it is sufficient to 
require a special material justification for the legality of the shareholders' 
resolution. The DAV is skeptical with regard to this specific question which 
could become all the more problematic the greater the ratio of voting rights 
permitted by national law and the greater the proportion of the share capital 
represented by the multiple voting shares permitted by national law. As a 
mediating solution it might be worth considering to provide for a special 
resolution (decision) of the disadvantaged ordinary shareholders in addition to 
the decision by the general assembly. It is uncertain whether the Draft 
Directive in its current version would permit such protective mechanisms of 
national law. These provisions should not be contained in the Draft Directive, 
but left to the national legislator. However, the Draft Directive should clarify 
that such requirements would have to be conform to the Directive. 
 
The Draft Directive leaves open the question of whether it would also be 
possible to provide for the issuing of multiple-voting shares when creating 
authorized capital. This question should likely be answered in the negative. 
Presumably, it would already have to be denied based on the Draft Directive, 
since it likely assumes that the general assembly would have the sole right to 
decide on the creation of multiple-voting shares. However, it would be 
desirable to clarify this as well.

Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a of the Draft Directive deals with the protection of minority 
shareholders and the company if multiple-voting shares are subsequently 
introduced in an existing public limited company. However, it would also be 
conceivable, that the articles of association of a public limited company 
provide for ordinary and multiple voting shares from the outset. In case of an 
entirely new formation, no resolution of the general assembly is required, but 
in this case all founders have to agree by adopting the articles of association. 
The Directive could clarify this. The requirements of letter a) are to be met if a 
public limited company with multiple voting shares is established by way of a 
change of legal form or a merger to form a new company. 
 
In Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a subpara. 2 of the Draft Directive, it should also be made 
clear that only in the case of amendments to the detriment of a category of 
shares, a special resolution of the disadvantaged category is required. For 
example 
 
"For the purposes ..........whose rights are adversely  
affected." 
 
A special resolution of the disadvantaged category would correspond to the 
legal requirement that exists anyway for German stock corporations under 
section 179 (3) AktG. For the special resolution, a dispositive amount of the 
capital majority in the articles of association should be in conformity with the 
Directive as well. 
 
b) Letter b (limitation of voting weight) 
 
Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b wants to oblige the member states to limit the voting weight 
of multiple-voting shares by law. In doing so, they are to have two options: 
Either, according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b item i, they are to set a maximum 
weighted voting quota and a requirement for the maximum percentage of the 
company's issued share capital that can be represented by the total amount of 
multiple voting shares. Or, according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b item ii, the Member 
 
 
Seite 15 von 21 
 
States shall restrict the exercise of the extended voting rights attached to 
multiple-voting shares in regard to resolutions which must be adopted at the 
shareholders' general assembly by qualified majority. The regulatory mandate 
in Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b is to be understood in principle as an alternative. 
However, according to Art. 5 para. 2 sentence 1, which allows for further 
protective measures by the member states, the national legislator may also 
prescribe both restrictions cumulatively and/or add further ones. 
 
The regulatory concept of the Draft Directive, not to permit multiple voting 
rights without limit, but to restrict them appropriately, is to be welcomed. 
Existing shareholders have an interest in multiple voting shares in order to 
maintain their decision-making power. They offer new shareholders the 
opportunity to gain decisive influence. However, the issuance of multiple-
voting shares can lead to significant shifts in the voting weight, and the greater 
these shifts become, the more weight is given to the disadvantages and risks 
associated with allowing multiple-voting shares. In order to limit them, it stands 
to reason that the additional power a multiple-voting share brings may not be 
determined arbitrarily. 
 
In particular, a limitation of the maximum voting weight and the maximum 
capital share of multiple voting shares according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b item i 
seems appropriate. The German Stock Corporation Act already contains a 
capital-related restriction in section 139 para. 2 AktG for preference shares. 
However, it remains unspecified in the Draft Directive, which values should be 
set for a maximum voting weight and a maximum capital share. Thus, it is 
ultimately up to the member states to decide how strong or weak the 
restriction should be, and the Draft Directive does not contain any limitation of 
this range. The DAV does not consider this as suitable. Whether a national 
legal system allows, for example, a voting right ratio of 1:5 or of 1:500 makes 
an enormous difference, all the more so if, for example, a ratio of 1:5 goes 
hand in hand with a restriction of multiple voting shares to 5% of the share 
capital, whereas a ratio of 1:500 may be created for 50% of the share capital. 
If the aim of the Draft Directive is to create a level playing field for European 
 
 
Seite 16 von 21 
 
companies, it cannot allow such a wide range for the national legislator. The 
directive itself must instead provide for appropriate upper limits. 
 
A restriction of multiple voting rights to certain matters requiring a resolution 
can also be an appropriate alternative to a volume-based restriction of the 
voting weight. However, it will have to be discussed whether it is appropriate, 
as stipulated in point (ii), to extend this limitation to all decisions to be taken by 
qualified majority. Firstly, the question of whether national law prescribes a 
qualified majority is not a suitable criterion for distinguishing between essential 
and non-essential decisions in all cases; the example of the amendment of the 
articles of association shows that a majority can be required to amend the 
articles of association for completely insignificant measures as well as for 
measures with far-reaching structural changes. Secondly, it is probably also 
going too far to exclude all essential decisions from the right to multiple voting 
rights, because there is likely an interest in multiple voting shares precisely in 
order to have a higher voting weight in essential decisions. The DAV therefore 
fears that alternative no. ii in its current version will remain without practical 
relevance. On the other hand, it seems appropriate to allow the national 
legislator to impose certain restrictions on multiple voting rights within the 
framework of Article 5 para 2 (see also below, No. 2 b). 
 
2. Draft Directive Para. 2 
 
Pursuant to Art. 5 para. 2, it shall be left to the Member States to implement 
protective mechanisms exceeding the scope of para. 1 in order to ensure an 
adequate protection of other shareholders and of the company's interest. The 
list in para. 2 is not exhaustive ("in particular"), so that other protective 
mechanisms than those listed here could also be provided for. 
 
a) Sunset Clauses 
 
In line with existing regulations in EU member states, para 2 is primarily 
dealing with the limitation of the period of validity of the multiple voting rights 
(sunset clauses). This approach is recognizably shaped by the idea that the 
 
 
Seite 17 von 21 
 
introduction of multiple voting rights is only (temporarily) justified in order to 
facilitate financing via the capital market for start-ups or SMEs, which could 
lead to a minority shareholding of the founding shareholders. In an initial 
phase of listing, they should still be able to retain control over "their" company. 
 
"Sunset clauses” are in principle suitable means of limiting the risks 
associated with multiple voting rights. The opening of such sunset clauses by 
the Directive is therefore appropriate. However, a mandatory legal requirement 
at the national level should be considered carefully, as it could weaken the 
advantages expected from the introduction of the multiple-voting share, 
especially if one wants to extend it to all public limited companies from the 
very narrow SME growth market. It should rather be left to the (founding) 
shareholders to provide for appropriate restrictions in the articles of 
association. Protective mechanisms are already sufficiently implemented 
through the regulations in Art. 5 para. 1 with the amendments proposed here 
and the established regulations on transactions with related parties, on group 
law as well as through the fiduciary duty (and, if necessary, a substantive 
resolution control). The capital market also represents a corrective, on which 
the shares of companies with multiple voting rights lacking market standard 
regulations can only be traded at a valuation deduction. In addition, stock 
exchanges or index operators would be free within the scope of their discretion 
to impose requirements on the time limit of multiple voting rights for inclusion 
in certain segments or indices. 
 
aa) In particular, a mandatory time limit after the expiry of a legally 
determined period of validity (time-based sunset clause - lit. b) could be 
disadvantageous. The idea of such a provision is to relativize over time 
the specific “impulses” of the founders and thus the “agency” advantages 
of companies with multiple-voting rights. However, there is hardly a "right" 
period of validity for multiple-voting rights. Only without a time-based 
expiry clause could multiple-voting rights could be a permanent 
alternative to, for example, a KGaA structure. 
 
 
 
Seite 18 von 21 
 
If the national legislator makes use of time-based sunset clauses, he should 
rather set a generous threshold of ten years and also provide for an option to 
extend the period by a resolution of the general assembly with a capital 
majority in accordance with section 179 para 2 AktG. Finally, with regard to 
stock corporations listed on a regulated market, the legal consequences of 
takeovers would also have to be considered: an automatic expiry of multiple 
voting rights can be associated with significant shifts in voting rights and a 
"passive acquisition of control" by a shareholder with regular voting rights. In 
this respect, it would probably have to be regulated that a passive acquisition 
of control does not give rise to an obligation to make a mandatory offer 
(section 35 WpÜG) or, in any case, that an exemption can be considered 
(section 37 WpÜG). 
 
bb) It is quite reasonable to link the expiry of the multiple voting rights to the 
transfer of the shares to third parties, the death or incapacity of the holder 
or other reasons for a withdrawal (transfer-based sunset clauses - lit. a). 
This is because, as a rule, the original justification for the additional voting 
power would no longer apply. However, on the one hand, possible 
circumventions, such as holding the multiple voting shares in an 
intermediate holding company, would then also have to be covered and, 
on the other hand, certain exceptions would also have to be considered 
(e.g. in the case of a transfer within a certain family tribe). 
 
At any rate, in the case of transfers by legal transaction, there is effective and 
also more flexible protection with the (mandatory) transfer restriction of 
registered shares than that of the strict falling away of the multiple voting 
rights. According to section 68 para 2 of the German Stock Corporation Act 
(AktG), the consent to the transfer can also be decided by the general 
assembly in accordance with the articles of association. The resolution would 
then have to be passed without the validity of the multiple voting rights in order 
to come into effect. A statutory rule on the expiry of the multiple voting right in 
the case of a transfer should therefore be combined with the possibility to 
determine otherwise in the articles of association. For public limited companies 
with a listing on the regulated market, a free disposal of the multiple voting

shares would also be conceivable due to the legal consequences of 
takeovers. Their acquisition may be accompanied by a change of control and 
the obligation of the acquirer to make a mandatory offer (section 35 WpÜG). 
The remaining shareholders would be sufficiently protected by this exit 
possibility. 
 
cc) The introduction of a mandatory event-based sunset clause (lit. c) upon 
the occurrence of an event determinable by the articles of association will 
finally depend above all on whether the multiple-voting shares will only be 
permissible for companies in an SME growth market. If so, the termination 
of the multiple voting rights would be consistent in the event of a change 
of stock exchange segment (uplisting to the regulated market) or delisting. 
Furthermore, forfeiture in the event of other events, such as the 
resignation of the holder of the multiple voting shares from the 
management board or supervisory board, should in principle only be an 
option under the articles of association. 
 
b) Material restrictions 
 
The purpose of lit. d is to limit the subject matter of multiple voting shares with 
respect to certain resolutions. The disproportionate voting power of multiple 
voting shares should not be able to be used to prevent resolutions of general 
assemblies aimed at preventing, reducing or eliminating adverse effects of 
business activities on human rights or the environment. 
 
This provision should be deleted from the Draft Directive. The political concern 
pursued in this way has nothing directly to do with the structuring of multiple 
voting rights under company law. There is no evidence that they are 
associated with a higher "ESG risk" than other companies under a controlling 
influence. It also remains unclear how and by whom adverse targeting of the 
resolution is to be bindingly determined at the time the resolution is passed. In 
most cases, adverse ESG effects are likely to be ambiguous or to become 
apparent only at a later stage. These ambiguities cannot be reconciled with 
the formal role of company law to ensure legally secure resolutions. In 
 
 
Seite 20 von 21 
 
addition, the regulation violates the system of competences under company 
law, since the risks or opposing measures listed here generally concern the 
management, which does not fall within the competence of the general 
assembly (section 119 AktG). 
 
On the other hand, it would be worth considering for the national legislator to 
provide for restrictions on the validity of multiple voting rights within the 
framework of the competences of the general assembly, especially where 
conflicts of interest between majority and minority could become relevant, 
which are not already covered by existing voting prohibitions. In this respect, 
one could possibly think of the election of auditors, the appointment of special 
auditors, the raising of claims for compensation and the waiver of claims for 
compensation. If necessary, this catalogue could be extended to include the 
vote on the remuneration system and report. This could be regulated along the 
lines of section 33b para. 2 no. 2 WpÜG in such a way that in these cases 
multiple voting shares would only constitute a single vote. This also seems to 
be more reasonable than the link to resolutions with qualified majority 
requirements, which is currently provided for in Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b no. ii) (for 
criticism see above on Art. 5 under no. 1 b)). 
 
Art. 6 Draft Directive - Transparency 
 
Art. 6 of the Draft Directive rightly provides that companies with multiple voting 
shares must publish detailed information on this aspect in brochures on the 
public offer or admission of their shares as well as in the annual financial 
report. This appears to be imperative for the sake of adequate investor 
protection and is already provided for under German law by the disclosure 
requirements in the notes to the financial statements (section 160 para. 1 no. 3 
AktG) and in the management report (section 289a sentence 1 no. 1, 315a 
sentence 1 no. 1 HGB). 
 
Paragraph 2 of Art. 6 of the Draft Directive, however, according to which 
holders of multiple voting shares or proxies who are natural persons are to be 
identified only by their name (e.g. "Joe Brown"), appears half-hearted and 
 
 
Seite 21 von 21 
 
insufficient. Solely the name is often not suitable for identification. There is a 
high risk of abuse with regard to the proposed rule. 
 
More importantly, the information on holders of multiple-voting shares or their 
representatives under Article 6 para 1 are only to be published in the offering 
or listing document and in the company's annual report. It should be clarified 
that any transfer of the multiple-voting shares is subject to notification insofar 
as 3% of the share capital or of the voting rights are affected. As far as 3% of 
the (possible) voting rights are concerned, a notification seems to be 
absolutely necessary, regardless of how many capital shares are transferred. 
A corresponding amendment to the respective Union Acts, in particular the 
Transparency Directive, seems to be necessary.

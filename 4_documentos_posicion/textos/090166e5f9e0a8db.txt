 
POSITION PAPER 
 
The Union of the European Lubricants Industry’s 
response to the public consultation on the Classification, Labelling and Packaging 
Regulation (CLP) regulation revision proposal 
 
March 2023 
 
 
We welcome the Commission’s proposal to revise the Classification, Labelling and Packaging (CLP) 
regulation in its purpose to make labelling more consumer friendly, less burdensome for suppliers and 
easier to enforce.  Indeed, the lubricants industry considers  the CLP regulation as foundational to 
chemical legislation and for downstream users of chemicals. 
 
Nevertheless, due to its wide-ranging impact on sectorial applications, and to achieve the objectives of 
the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability set in 2020, we support the Commission’s proposal to review 
and improve the legislation to keep the labels as simple as possible especially for SME that do not have 
the adequate human resources. In that purpose, and to ensure the best possible implementation of the 
criteria at EU level, we invite the European Commission to consider the following recommendations 
for the revision: 
 
 
I. Labelling requirements 
 
With the new requirements  proposed by the European Commission  to ensure readability, labels would 
need to follow harmonised and strict er rules across the EU  with a common template, i.e. a white 
background, precise font size, space between sentences will become compulsory.  
 
We support the European Commission’s objective to ensure readability. However, in practice, these new 
requirements will have a large negative impact  on supply chain s. Indeed, companies would have to 
dedicate significant human and financial resources, assure new design s, manage the modification s in 
the printing and labelling process, deal with increased complexity in the logistic chain, without counting 
the time needed to re-adapt current labels. 
 
The proposed dimensions on label design, like minimum font size and distance between two consecutive 
lines, are exaggeratedly big and will have important, deleterious  consequences, without having a 
significant impact on safety and awareness . This new requirement will specifically affect SMEs who  are 
dedicating most of their human resources in producing and less in administrative and labelling processes. 
For example, by increasing the size of the hazard communication text, there will be: 
• Substantial impact on costs, resources and the environment that are not in line with sustainability 
goals – for instance, it is estimated to lead to a three-to-thirteen times increase of volume of paper 
and ink (depending on the packaging size).

• A very high  impact on the environment: for cases where text is directly printed on the 
can/spray/cartridge there will be huge increase in plastic and packaging material consumption – 
those cases are specifically critical because there is no possibility to use a fold-out label on the 
receptacle. 
• The number of languages on a label will be drastically reduced due to space availability. This would 
in turn limit the number of countries of commercialisation, increase the number of references per 
article, and lead to disruptions in free movement of products within the Single Market. 
• The number of operations in the plant will increase and will add complexity to stockage and 
logistics within the supply chain , facing problems especially for small and medium sized 
companies.

Another element impacting industry is the mandatory white background of the label. If the text is easily 
readable with sufficient contrast between the background and the text elements, we believe that uniform 
colour background and letters should be permitted. In cases where the text is printed directly on coloured 
packaging (for instance, aerosols cans ), this would require companies to  change the packaging entirely , 
not only the label. Furthermore, labels on products used by consumer and professional workers contain 
marketing and brand elements . As colours of labels for many products will be changed sim ultaneously, 
brand familiarity, key for consumers, may be lost.  
 
➢ We are highly concerned by the upcoming changes in the label formatting as in practice this 
will have a significant impact on resources and the environment which are not in line with 
sustainability goals and will disrupt the free market and the whole supply chain.  
➢ In our view, specific formatting rules should be reconsidered and kept in the guidance 
document, as it is currently the case. 
 
II. Allow a realistic transition period 
 
The CLP revision contains major changes that would force industry to overhaul their internal processes 
and adapt on many critical aspects (for instance modification of all labels along the supply chain). 
Considering the re-design of internal and external processes to adapt to new requirements, complexity of 
supply chain, availability of resources and technical problems in implementation, the proposed transition 
period of 18 months from the entry of CLP revision into force is not enough time.  
The process of changing labels for consumer, professional and even industrial products is very complex 
and time consuming. As p roducts are sold across the EU single market, the proposed changes would  

 
require simultaneous, coordinated updates of various process across  multiple countries and significant 
time for design adaptation. Furthermore, the practical aspects of validation of these changes, availability 
of printing agencies, and the huge number of articles undergoing modification at the same time must also 
be considered. 
➢ This transition period of CLP implementation needs to be realistically adjusted to longer period 
of minimum 42 months (3 ,5 years) in coherence with article 61 (7) for  products already 
produced and labelled  
 
III. Timeline for update of labels 
The new CLP Regulation proposal requires labels to be updated within 6 months in case a new 
hazard class or a more severe classification needs to be assigned to a substance or a mixture, or when 
new supplemental information on the label  is required. This t imeline is too short , especially for SMEs 
facing staff shortages, and inconsistent with current practices which have proven adequate to allow re -
design, re-printing of labels and re-labelling of packages.  
➢ Consistent with current rules, we recommend that 18 months should be the proper timeline for 
all label updates. This is normal timeline for the entry into force of adaptions to the technical 
progress (ATP) when CLH becomes mandatory for specific substances.  
 
 
IV. Ensure the use of fold-out labels is facilitated in practice on all types of 
packaging 
 
The current CLP regulation only allows for the use of fold-out labels when the shape or form of a packaging 
made the readability of a label difficult. As a result of advancements in labelling technologies, it appeared 
that more flexibility should be given to suppliers by allowing a broader use of fold -out labels, which the 
European Commission wished to address in its revision proposal. 
 
In the “explanatory memorandum” of the proposal, the European Commission states that “broader use of 
fold-out labels will be allowed ” (page 11). Yet, companies would  only be allowed to use fold -out labels 
when “application of labels cannot be met due to the shape or form of the packaging or its small size ” 
(point 11, page 23) . Confusingly, the draft proposal in article 29(1)  adds “or a fold -out label ” in the 
provision. It seems that the product, regardless of its size, can have either a “label” or a “fold-out” label.  
Multilayer labels (where the different sheets are stuck onto each other) are also frequently used by 
industry and they should be considered as covered by the term “fold-out labels”. 
Hence, the use of the term “fold-out label” seems unclear. 
When using fold-out labels, the situation where national authorities require the country language to be 
put on the first page should be avoided. As this situation is currently happening  and resulting in market 
disturbance, in particular for SMEs, this should be clarified in the proposal.  
 
➢ We fear that this provision is not explicit enough and would lead to confusion in the 
implementation of the CLP changes and future enforcement in Member States. We believe it 
is important to clearly state in the revision text that fold-out labels are allowed on all types 
of packaging. 
➢ We suggest implementing fold out labels for all types of conditioning as it is stated in the 
GHS and CLP guidelines example. 
➢ Regarding future enforcement, we should not face again situations where national 
authorities require the country language to be put on the first page. 
 
V. New definition of multi-constituent substances 
 
The new proposal for CLP seeks to introduce a definition for multi-constituent substances for the purpose 
of clarifying classification rules for substances that contain impurities, additives or multiple components. 
The introduction of this definition in CLP is confusing and unnecessary as it is in contradiction with how 
multi-constituent and UVCB’s substances have been identified under REACH. Detailed rules are laid out in 
the ECHA guidance for identification and naming under REACH and have been applied from the beginning 
across all REACH registrations. They are the foundation on which the assessment of substances is built.  
A lot of substances are registered as UVCB under REACH and have been tested  as such, meaning the 
classification was established based on the substance as a whole. Substance registration would be 
questioned with major implications on all previously performed testing. 
Furthermore, for lubricants in particular, this change in definition is not feasible, since for complex UVCB 
substances, such as base oils, there are potentially thousands of constituents in varying concentrations. 
Besides, from an international perspective, the definition of multi-constituent substances does not exist, 
meaning it will create market disturbance.  
➢ There is no need to introduce a new definition of multi-constituent substance for the purpose 
of clarifying classification rules. What is important is to clarify on a sound scientific basis 
when data on individual constituents (impurities, additives or constituents) prevail vs when 
data generated on the ‘whole substance’ can be used, and the CLP revision can do this 
without a new definition.   
 
 
VI. Create a clear framework to regulate sales from companies outside the 
EU and clarify the difference between distance and online sales. 
 
The European Commission’s draft revision proposal addresses distance and online sales, by introducing 
provisions and clear responsibilities. This means suppliers would need to ensure that a substance or a 
mixture placed on the EU market through distance sales meets the requirements of the CLP regulation. 
However, the difference between the two types of sales (distance and online) is not specified. 
 
➢ We call on the European Commission to clarify its intention behind regulating both distance 
and online sales, by defining both in Article 2. 
 
VII. Clarify the concept of refill sales for the end users 
 
The European Commission propose s to clarify the concept of refill sales , yet the provisions as stated in 
the proposal are very general. 
 
➢ We call on the European Commission to clarify its intention when it comes to the refill sales 
concept. 
➢ Petrol stations should be out of the scope. 
 
VIII. Clarify the application scope of the advertising requirement 
We request the Commission to better outline the application scope of this requirement. It is not clear if 
CLP labelling elements should appear on materials for products dedicated to consumer  only use, e.g., 
marketing brochures, posters, flyers, and/or technical data sheets/specification. This requirement implies 
a management of change for advertising materials when the CLP classification of a product changes, thus 
creating additional burden and impact on sustainability.  
➢ We believe CLP labelling requirements should not apply to marketing materials. 
 
IX. Address grouping of chemicals 
 
In the revision proposal, grouping is not fully considered.  To allow proper extrapolation of toxicological 
and hazard data between different substances, a careful and scientifically justified implementation of the 
grouping must be performed. Substances assigned to a group which receive a harmonized classification 
might automatically fall under a REACH restriction. There is a real risk behind this concept of substances 
being banned or restricted from the market based on poorly justified precautionary principle s and a 
conservative approach. 
 
➢ We believe grouping of chemicals should be applied with responsibility, and kindly invite the 
European Commission to take the necessary actions. 
➢ The assessment of ‘similarity’ must be based on a review of all available data on the substances’ 
physico-chemical, ecotoxicological and toxicological properties. This review must in clude a 
Weight of Evidence assessment across all relevant criteria for the hazard in question. Such an 
approach will help avoid over -classifying and over -regulating substances based on ‘presumed’ 
properties.

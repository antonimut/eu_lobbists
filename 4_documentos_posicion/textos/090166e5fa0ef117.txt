9 March 2023 
BASF feedback to the public consultation 
regarding the revision of EU legislation on hazard 
classification, labelling and packaging of 
chemicals  
BASF thanks the European Commission for the opportunity to provide feedback on the recently 
published draft on the revision of EU legislation on hazard classification, labelling and packaging of 
chemicals. While we support the aim of updating the CLP regulation and making it more efficient, 
we would like to address some of our concerns with the current version of the text. 
 
 
Introduction of the multi-constituent substance concept into CLP (Article 2(7a)) 
The current proposal implements definition “multi-constituent substance” as “a substance that 
contains more than one constituent”. This approach leads to legal uncertainties. Rules used for many 
years to determine the substance identity and substance sameness in the context of the REACH 
registration will become meaningless. This over-simplified definition effectively overrides provisions 
within the Guidance for identification and naming of substances under REACH and CLP1. As in 
practice all substances contain more than one constituent (for example impurities, by-products), all 
substances would be multi-constituent substances, rendering other well-known terms like mono-
constituent substances or UVCB2 substances obsolete.

Our Key Messages 
 The newly introduced multi-constituent substance definition conflicts with existing provisions 
laid out in REACH and GHS. 
 UVCBs need to be derogated from the multi-constituent substance definitions to make the 
new provisions practically feasible. 
 Compared to current obligations, the suggested fixed deadline of 6 months for updating 
labels is too rigid to meet the needs of the whole value chain.  
 The new formatting rules for labels will pose a significant burden and costs to the duty 
holders without any true benefit to society. Planned further limitations for the use of fold-out 
labels contradicts the EU Commission’s objective to make EU law simpler and less costly. 
 Changes in the CLH process need to be transparent and especially grouping principles must 
be scientifically robust. 
 Increased requirements to the CLI update process will be an additional bureaucratic burden 
on the upstream industry without increasing consumer protection. 
 Empowerment of ECHA to use information provided for other purposes will cause legal 
uncertainties.

Moreover, in conjunction with the new classification rules for multi-constituent substances in Article 
5(3), ambiguity between the multi-constituent substance approach and information requirements for 
substances subject to registration under REACH is created. It will be questionable, if a study can or 
shall be performed when constituents with known hazards are present. The result of such studies 
might not be usable for classification as it might conflict with the newly proposed provisions in Article 
5(3).  
In BASF’s view, it is also not justifiable to omit concentration limits for constituents of multi-
constituent substances comparable to those for mixtures in Article 10(1) and (10). This would lower 
classification thresholds in an undefined way. The new testing rules resulting from the multi-
constituent substance principle seem to contradict Article 8(6) of the CLP regulation, requiring 
substances to be tested in the form they are used in.  
BASF suggests refining the multi-constituent substance principle, for example, by clearly connecting 
this new concept to the above-mentioned fundamental definitions within REACH or guidance 
documents as well as introducing clear concentration limits for the classification of multi-constituent 
substances in the legal text.  
  
Inclusion of UVCBs into the multi-constituent substance definition (Article 2 (7a)) 
UVCBs, as defined in REACH, are per definition substances of variable and complex composition, 
which cannot be analyzed and characterized in the same way as substances and mixtures. Because 
of its variable content the current CLP and GHS3 frameworks allow certain simplifications, for 
example in case of the biodegradation properties of an UVCB, when its constituents are structurally 
similar. Abandoning the definition of UVCB in favour of the multi-constituent substance concept 
within the CLP revision will not only cause unsolvable analytical problems (e.g., for the determination 
of toxicity for aquatic organisms) but also discrepancies to the GHS in the classification for a large 
number of existing substances. 
Globally this would result in a different interpretation/prioritization of classification and registration 
data ultimately leading to identical substances/mixtures having different hazard statements and 
definitions. EU-based companies importing raw materials from outside the EU could lose critically 
important supply chains since these new CLP classification rules would have to be enforced for 
outside EU suppliers.  
BASF suggests derogating UVCBs from certain provisions of the multi-constituent substance 
definition, thereby recognizing their analytical and regulatory differences to other substance classes.  
Vague requirements for suppliers (Article 4(10)) 
The added provisions for suppliers are unspecific and cannot be fulfilled by them throughout the 
whole life cycle of a substance. Suppliers become liable for activities of others without having an 
enforcement mandate. 
Changes in rules for mixture classification (Article 6) 
The addition of new hazard classes (ED, PMT/vPvM, PBT/vPvB) to the specific rules for mixture 
classification will create a misalignment of global supply chains and as mentioned above, different

Changes for labels (Article 30, Article 31, Annex I) 
Although an update period of 6 months is aligned with the requirements for a REACH registration 
dossier update in case of a changed Classification and Labelling (C&L), the actors in the supply 
chain need more flexibility. Depending on the case, a changed C&L is more than just an update of 
the label. It might cause further duties regarding usage, storage, and transport of the product, which 
need to be implemented as well. Therefore, the relabelling of a product is usually a coordinated 
action between supplier and customer to ensure the implementation of required measures before 
the new label for the product is provided. The current CLP text asks for an update “without undue 
delay”, which takes up practical constraints better than a fixed period. 
The newly introduced minimum font size combined with further formatting rules (Annex I) will result 
in logistical issues and significant cost increases without a true benefit. In many cases, required 
information will not fit on labels anymore. Additionally, the new requirements effectively prevent the 
use of multilingual labels due to the size constraints. For warehouses, this leads to relabelling needs 
during shipment preparation and is not practicable from a capacity point of view. We suggest deleting 
the new line-spacing requirement as this does not increase readability and reduce to the minimum 
font size required on labels as the currently envisioned font sizes are impractical and unnecessarily 
large. 
We call for more flexibility regarding labelling requirements. The deletion of the exemption to use 
fold-out labels for small packaging is unacceptable. Instead, fold-out labels should be accepted in 
general. For various applications (e.g. paints, agricultural products), labels with limited size must 
convey more information to the user than required by CLP. Additionally, an acceptance of 
multilingual fold-out labels will promote the free movement of goods in the EU internal market, as 
they reduce relabelling efforts. Frequently, products manufactured to stock must be packaged and 
labelled before knowing the country of the purchaser. Sometimes even units from the same palette 
need to be shipped to various member states. The current requirements do not allow fold-out labels 
for the purpose of providing more than one language on the label. This causes burdensome 
relabelling duties solely for shipments within the internal EU market. 
We acknowledge the attempt to promote digital labelling. However, the current provisions are 
insufficient. It is unlikely that actors in the supply chain will use them often for more efficient hazard 
communication. 
Changes in the process for harmonized classifications (CLH) (Article 37) 
While BASF understands the need to streamline the CLH process for the newly introduced hazard 
classes by extending the right to start such a process to the Commission, it should be pointed out, 
that one of the bottlenecks of CLH processes is the capacity of the RAC. Therefore, the creation of 
a larger number of CLH dossiers may not lead to more substances being classified. 
As a second measure in this regard grouping of substances is foreseen. BASF urges the 
Commission and responsible parties to use scientific, transparent, and robust grouping principles4

Changes in the Classification & Labelling Inventory (CLI) update process (Article 40) 
Already today, updates of the CLI are a considerable burden for regulatory departments even in 
larger companies. However, its use as an information tool for the general public is rather limited, as 
the information published therein are neither verified nor validated. To a large extent, substances 
are both notified to the CLI and registered under REACH. Conflicting information across all C&L 
entities for the same substances causes confusion rather than transparency. From our point of view, 
only the C&L from the REACH registration dossiers and CLH dossiers should be regarded as 
validated and qualified information for the purpose of a CLI. Downstream users of chemicals must 
consider the information provided to them in the safety data sheets. Exceeding notification 
requirements will not bring any added value in terms of transparency and improving the knowledge 
on hazards of used substances. Therefore, the CLI should be focused on the publication of 
harmonized C&L and C&L information retrieved from the REACH registration dossiers. Additional 
burden for companies and authorities due to notification and processing of rarely used data should 
be avoided. Reasons for changes or deviations from classifications within the CLI can be very 
complex. The check of all existing notifications for all hazard classes even for a single substance is 
extremely time-consuming and the result might be questionable due to limited available knowledge. 
Finally, it will be outdated with the next notification of another notifier.  Hence, demanding 
unequivocal reasons for changes or deviations, while also publishing the name of the submitter will 
lead to increased pressure and unjustified name-dropping of companies without an increase in 
consumer or environmental protection. It is also unclear, whether a reasoning for deviation might 
need information on a level of detail in which confidential business information must be submitted 
(e.g., disclosing impurities, which indicate the production process).  
The upcoming polymer registration as well as the multi-constituent substance concept will make it 
even harder to compare and assess the validity of entries within the CLI further limiting its use. 
Concepts within the Chemical Strategy for Sustainability such as One-Substance-One-Assessment 
(OSOA) are currently looking into a harmonization of data and might be a more efficient way to make 
substance data universally available than increasing the burden on industry for CLI updates.  
Empowerment of ECHA to use information provided for other purposes (Article 45) 
We noticed a further empowerment of regulatory bodies using information provided by companies 
for other purposes (here: emergency health response) instead of identifying new possible regulatory 
measures. Such redefinition of data in retrospect causes legal uncertainties.

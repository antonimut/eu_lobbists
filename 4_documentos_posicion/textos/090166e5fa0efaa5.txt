Executive Summary 
The medical technology industry is keenly aware of the need to conserve resources  and shift from a linear 
material model of extraction, use and disposal to  a circular economy model.  That need is expressed in 
international commitments like the UN Sustainable Development Goal 12, customer expectations, as well as 
businesses’ responsibilities and interests. 
MedTech Europe supports the objective of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) 
proposal to prevent or reduce the adverse impacts of packaging waste on the environment and human health. 
We appreciate the acknowledgement of  the sector's specificities and granting  special conditions to  plastic 
packaging of medical technologies under Article 6 ( Recyclable packaging) and Article 7 (Minimum recycled 
content in plastic packaging ). The exemption s granted should ensure the critical performance of the 
packaging for the protection of medical technology and maintenance of its functionality.  
This document highlights the specific considerations of our sector, and we ask for: 
1. Special consideration regarding the transition to full circularity . A minimum of a seven-year 
transition period for implementing the requirements applying to medical technologies' packaging 
will be required  for the sector  to develop solutions for recyclability performance, labelling and 
minimisation to ensure medical technologies continue to b e supplied i n safe and high -quality state to 
patients and end users. Furthermore, recycling technology is currently not sufficiently mature or scalable 
for robust recycling of medical packaging waste or the provision of recycled materials congruent with 
stringent health and safety requirements of sectorial regulation. 
2. Legislative consistency. The requirements should align with applicable legislation and the proposed 
and existing conformity assessment procedures in place under the IVDR1 and MDR2 sectorial legislation. 
3. Harmonisation of the requirements . MedTech Europe welcomes the transition to a Regulation that 
leads to harmonisation at the EU level. It is crucial to ensure greater protection and avoid disruption of 
the EU Single Market by harmonising the different requirements among Member States until the new 
legislation enters into force. 
4. Material-neutral exemption about the requirements on recyclability . Packaging for medical 
technologies is often made of non-plastic materials, which will also require additional time to implement 
the proposed recyclability requirements under Article 6 (Recyclable packaging). 
5. Extension of the scope of the exemptions granted for medical technologies packaging under Article 6 
(Recyclable packaging ) and Article 7 ( Recycled content in plastic packaging ) to include similar 
applications for research use only and investigational devices.  These exemptions should also be 
extended to sales packaging of critical supplies of medical technologies’ components, materials, and 
parts covered by MDR Article 117.

Introduction 
Medical technologies are very diverse,  including a large variety of enabling technologies, materials, use 
environment and end users. They can be everyday objects such as sticking plasters, syringes, surgical 
masks, or latex gloves. They could also be spectacles, wheelchairs, COVID -19 tests, or medical apps. 
Medical technologies also include tota l body scanners, gene mutation tests, combination products 3, e.g., 
adrenaline or insulin injectors and drug -eluting stents, implantable devices such as heart valves and 
pacemakers, and replacement joints for knees and hips. That is also reflected in the p ackaging of different 
medical technologies , as existing regulatory requirements are set and controlled by detailed  sectorial 
legislation: Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on Medical Devices (MDR) and  Regulation (EU) 2017/746 on In Vitro 
Diagnostic Medical Devices (IVDR), including similar applications used in research-use only 4  and 
investigational devices. These regulations govern medical technologies, their packaging, and accompanying 
information about safe disposal. Likewise, they reg ulate the need for packaging functionalities to minimise 
the risks for patients by maintaining sterility and ensuring the function of medical technologies. 
 
Key Considerations 
Transition to Full Circularity 
The transition to the validation of alternative materials with an adequate level of quality and quantity of stable 
material supply  to replace existing ones takes time to safeguard materials' consistency, reliability, and 
biocompatibility. 
Conventional recycling techniques  will degrade polymeric materials, and their composition will be 
compromised. T his impedes the sector from ensuring the safety and performance requirements of the 
product and packaging can still be met due to a lack of control of the composition and performance of the 
recycled content. Virgin -like recycled material suitable for medical technology packaging will unlikely be 
available by 2035. There will be a  huge demand for recycled materials, and it will be difficult for S mall and 
Medium Enterprises (SMEs), which represent 95% of more than 34,000 medical technology companies in 
Europe, to get such materials. 
The sector welcomes the exemption from mandatory recycled content requirements for contact-sensitive 
plastic packaging of healthcare technologies , which is necessary for patient safety. This approach must be 
extended to critical medical and pharmaceutical industries supplied materials . Such materials include, for 
example, packaging components like preformed sterile barrier systems, containers, jars, syringes, cartridges, 
stoppers for syringes or closures for containers o f critical raw materials like active  pharmaceutical 
ingredients/excipients and critical materials for medical technologies to avoid possible contaminations. Many 
of these components and materials are delivered sterile for aseptic processing of parenteral, for instance, or

Time to Transition to New Requirements and Compliance 
When medical technology packaging needs to be changed, several stages are considered by the 
manufacturer and are presented as follows: 
• Research, Design and Development: changing or updating packaging is subject to change control  
under the MDR and IVDR (see previous section Transition to Full Circularity). 
• Documentation and Testing: typical packaging development and validation activities can take at least 
12 months. Revalidation also takes a minimum of 12 months for accelerated ageing if stability testing is 
involved. Real -time ageing over the stated shelf life is an additional requirement and will be started 
simultaneously. In addition, the introduction of such changes may trigger a stability report requirement 
(over two years of activity per material set).  This is compounded by collating technical documentation 
for conformity assessment under the sectorial legislation. 
• Notified Body Review and Approval: depending on the risk class of the medical technology, there is 
a need for prior approval by a Notified Body before the changes can be implemented. Mandatory Notified 
Body assessments would also be required as such changes wi ll be seen as significant, which can add 
considerable waiting and review periods to the timing. Furthermore, the lack of Notified Body capacity 
has resulted in delays in the certification of medical technologies, etc. 
• Post-Market Surveillance:  to ensure pa tient safety increased post -market surveillance of products 
subject to these changes will be required. 
During these rigorous processes, the continued availability of critical medical technologies must be ensured. 
Considering the typical length of medical technology packaging design cycles , measures to systematically 
address sustainable design can realistically be integrated during new packaging development when 
sustainability and key design inputs have been effecti vely assessed and can be acted upon. Therefore, a 
transition period of at least seven years for the medical technologies' packaging should apply. 
 
Policy Coherence and Consistency 
It is crucial to avoid inconsistencies and overlaps in requirements under various regulatory instruments, as 
well as streamline the process for compliance.

Horizontal Legislation:  MedTech Europe calls for consistency between the requirements of the PPWR 
proposal and other horizontal legislation such as Regulation (EC) 1907/2006 on the Registration, Evaluation, 
Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH) , Directive 2008/98/EC on waste and Regulation (EC) 
No 765/2008 and Decision No 768/2008/EC (New legislative framework (NLF)). In this context, ongoing 
legislative proposals m ust also be considered, such as the proposal for a Regulation establishing a 
framework for setting ecodesign requirements for sustainable products (ESPR) for optimum results. 
Sectorial Legislation (MDR and IVDR) : to comply with the safety and performance requirements of the 
sectorial legislation 5, the packaging of  medical technologies is rigorously tested and designed for long use 
cycles to guarantee the safety of patients (see Annex I). The compliance of such packaging within sectorial 
legislation (MDR and IVDR) must take precedence to ensure the safety and efficacy of these lifesaving 
products are maintained while ensuring no disruption to product availability. 
 
Harmonisation of Requirements 
The lack of harmonisation between Member States may potentially lead to disruptions in the delivery of 
critical medical technology to citizens where needed. It is important to ensure that packaging materials and 
packaged medical technologies can move freely within the EU Single Market. It is inherently wasteful to 
create novel packaging for each Member State as it will increase scrappage and inhibit flexibility in logistics. 
Labelling, in particular, is a critical part of medical technologies, and changes may require a review by the 
Notified Body (see Annex I, Section 4 for more information). 
MedTech Europe calls for a harmonised European approach towards packa ging requirements. It is crucial 
to ensure that requirements among Member States remain aligned and the EU Single Market is not disrupted. 
 
Recyclability 
Many medical technologies packaging is made of other non -plastic materials, such as glass, paper, 
laminates, etc., subject to the same restrictive requirements as plastic packaging. Non -plastic packaging for 
medical technologies also requires additional time to implement the proposed recyclability requirements, and 
the exemption granted for medical technologies contact -sensitive packaging should be material -neutral. As 
an example of non -plastic packaging, there is currently no recycling stream for borosilicate glass (Type 1) 
used for vials, ampou les and syringes foreseen (only lime soda glass), so an exemption for the use of 
borosilicate glass is also necessary for safety and quality reasons.

Annex I 
 
1. Requirements for Medical Technologies: Legislation and Standards 
In the European Union, medical technologies are tightly regulated by specific legislation that guarantees their 
safety and performance across their lifetime, pre- and post-market, namely the Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on 
Medical Devices (MDR) and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 on In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices (IVDR). These 
have repealed the preceding Directives (Directive 93/42/EEC on Medical Devices (MDD), Directive 
90/385/EEC on Active Implantable Medical Devices (AIMDD) and Directive 98/79/EC In Vitro  Diagnostic 
Medical Devices (IVDD), which are still applicable for certain medical technologies.  
The MDR and IVDR require manufacturers to conform  to safety principles, compatible with a high level of 
health and safety protection, based on clinical data, taking  account of the generally acknowledged state of 
the art. These regulations introduce rigorous risk and quality management to ensure the safety and 
performance of devices. They require careful selection of qualified materials with demonstrated performance 
and stability, with demonstrated biocompatibility, toxicological, physical, and chemical properties; 
compatibility with the device and labelling system; and sealing and sterilisation processes. In selecting the 
most appropriate solutions, manufacturers shall first eliminate or reduce risks as far as possible through safe 
design and manufacture. If that is not possible, adequate protection measures shall be taken against risks 
that cannot be eliminated. These Regulations are based on the new legislative frame work building on 
harmonised standards to provide a presumption of conformity with the general safety and performance 
requirements and ensure patient safety. Relevant and commonly used standards for medical packaging are: 
• EN ISO 11607-1 & 2 Packaging for terminally sterilised medical devices 
• EN ISO 10993 Series for biological and clinical evaluation 
• EN ISO 13485 Medical devices - Quality management systems - Requirements for regulatory purposes 
• EN ISO 14971 Medical devices - Application of risk management to medical devices 
• Standards for sterilisation processes and sterilisers, like ISO 11135, Sterilisation of health-care products 
- Ethylene oxide - Requirements for the development, validation, and routine control of a sterilisation 
process for medical devices, ISO 11137 (all parts), Sterilisation of health care products — Radiation, EN 
285, Sterilisation - Steam sterilisers, EN 13060, Small steam sterilisers, etc. 
In addition to the horizontal standards listed above, there are a few vertical product standards that would also 
apply to medical technologies, e.g., ISO 11040 -7 (prefilled syringes part 7: packaging systems for sterilised 
sub-assembled syringes ready for filling). 
When designing and manufacturing invasive medical technologies , that come into direct contact with the 
human body or administer medicines, body fluids or other substances, p articular attention shall be paid to 
the choice of materials and substances used, as regards 1) toxicity (in particular, substances which are 
carcinogenic, mutagenic, or toxic to reproduction or having endocrine -disrupting properties); 2) substances 
of animal origin; 3) compatibility between materials and biological tissues, cells, and body fluids and 4) impact 
of processes on material properties.

Special att ention is given to sterility as medical technologies labelled as sterile are to be processed, 
manufactured, packaged, and sterilised using appropriate and validated methods. Since packaging is in 
direct contact with the medical device or there may be patie nt contact depending on the application, the 
biocompatibility and the toxicological attributes must be evaluated and controlled. Packaging solutions 
require rigorous validation with stability and performance testing to verify properties and integrity over the 
shelf life and process validation to provide evidence of process capability to always achieve packaging 
integrity. In addition, packaging must protect the packaged product so that functions are maintained as 
designed over its full shelf life. Some products require almost complete oxygen, moisture, and/or light barrier 
to ensure efficacy (e.g., absorbable sutures). Rigorous testing is required to demonstrate this functionality , 
and the use of current barrier technologies is often based on extensive develo pments and studies. Both 
product and packaging would be discarded if there is a failure to meet requirements and would therefore be 
counterproductive towards reducing the environmental impacts of packaging.  
New or changed materials require extensive studie s and appropriate revalidations. Thus, it is essential to 
ensure that packaging requirements are not in conflict with these Regulations, existing standards, or with 
other legislation such as Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 on the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and 
Restriction of Chemicals (REACH Regulation), CLP Regulation, Directive 2008/98/EC on Waste, etc.  
 
2. Changes to Packaging for Medical Technologies 
To comply with medical technology regulatory requirements and safety principles, the manufacturer would 
first need to implement systems for traceability and risk control and demonstrate the long-term capability of 
recycling processes. Only when these systems are in place and effective, ca n packaging revalidations be 
performed to prove that they will continue to meet the intended use when integrating recycled content. Any 
changes to the source, mix, the introduction of new processes, etc., will require additional revalidation 
(designs, sealing, etc.) and certification of the medical technologies. For existing applications in the market, 
changes to any materials will have to be assessed, and revalidations will be required to demonstrate that the 
validated status is maintained. Reregistration may also be triggered. 
For packaging which provides product protection, checks are undertaken to ensure protection  
(light/humidity/sterility) is maintained and product damage or failure is avoided up to the point of use. Stability 
testing of retained samples needs to be ongoing over the stated life of the product to ensure that the product 
remains compliant.  
Feedstock quality also needs to be monitored to ensure ongoing compliance . Inconsistency in material 
feedstock could likely lead to supply disruption. Other issues that would need to be tested for would include  
contamination; physical and technical properties; mechanical performance; leaching of contaminants and 
checks of gas and liquid permeability/transpiration. All these need to be understood before the material would 
be released for the manufacture of contact-sensitive packaging.

3. Verification and Validation Processes of Packaging for Medical Technologies  
Validations are typically performed over at least three batches of materials considering the normally expected 
material variability. 
The IVDR and MDR require applying the safety principles, which makes the inclusion of post -consumer 
recycled content unlikely with the current state-of-the-art recycling technologies as it would require testing 
every raw material batch containing recycled content and revalidation. These validations would be very time-
consuming with unclear outcomes, which, in turn, will introduce uncertainties in the supp ly of these 
technologies and negatively impact society in addition to business continuity.  
Verification and validation of packaging with alternative materials would  need to include real-time stability 
studies covering the biocompatibility and performance o f the product (transit tests, drop tests, 
stability/sterility, etc.) over the shelf-life of the product and extremes of shipping conditions. 
 
4. Changes to Labels 
Different packaging requirements across the EU Single Market, or additional local labelling requirements, 
impede the use of a single packaging/labelling scheme. This leads to manufacturers having to redesign their 
packaging/labelling to adapt them to indiv idual Member States. These changes affect the labelling process 
and timing. Even if a change is deemed non -substantial and the manufacturer does not need to prepare a 
new submission for approval, such changes take time to implement as change management pro cesses 
involve multiple sorts of expertise. 
Where labelling on the available space of the packaging  is not technically feasible , increased labelling 
requirements result in larger packaging or regional variants of products to accommodate the addition of 
inserts. In addition to increased environmental impact, manufacturers also must examine whether this makes 
economic sense for all products intended for individual countries. This can ultimately lead to restricting the 
availability of medical technologies in t hese countries.  When countries request additional labelling 
requirements, the consequences are that existing products must be either: 
1. Relabelled, with existing codes or through the creation of new codes to meet national requirements. The 
process is likely to lead to further revalidation. 
2. Destroyed: the interruption of established distribution models may lead to the destruction of products 
that cannot be placed on shelves. Resources are thus wasted along the supply chain from raw material 
suppliers to citizens and healthcare institutions  and may create shortages of medical technologies for 
patients.  
3. Redirected to other countries : National labelling requirements can lead to disruptions of efficient 
distribution systems, requiring suppliers to ship unsold goods over longer distances to overpack and 
relabel them per the different national legislations. Additional transport increases fossil fuel consumption, 
negatively impacting climate and air quality.

The U.S. Dairy Export Council (USDEC) appreciates the opportunity to comment on the e 
European Union’s (EU) proposed Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR).1 USDEC 
is a non-profit, independent membership organization representing the global trade interests of 
U.S. dairy farmers, dairy processors and cooperatives, dairy ingredient suppliers and export 
trading companies.  
 
USDEC and its members are committed to the responsible management of plastic packaging 
around the world. U.S. dairy producers continue to produce safe, high-quality, nutrient-dense 
dairy foods while minimizing the environmental impact of dairy production, including by managing 
the use of plastic. Packaging is important both economically and environmentally, and dairy 
packaging, in particular, is a crucial component that protects dairy products from contamination 
and spoilage. It is against this backdrop of the critical role that packaging plays in ensuring that 
dairy moves efficiently and safely through the food supply chain that we provide these comments.   
 
USDEC strongly encourages the European Commission’s (EC or “the Commission”) policymakers 
to work collaboratively with industry groups, foreign governments and other relevant stakeholders 
as this regulation is drafted. The repercussions of the proposed revision(s) to PPWR will 
reverberate throughout global supply chains, and we hope that the EU will approach the 
implementation of these changes with appropriate consideration for practical realities beyond any 
single entity’s control. 
 
Before we provide feedback on specific articles in the proposal, we would like to share an overall 
concern that the regulatory framework focuses almost exclusively on the duties and obligations of 
economic operators, which does not encompass recycling facilities, waste management 
organizations, or consumers. Producers and users of packaging are only part of the complex life 
cycle of packaging, and they cannot control the actions of third parties. Put simply, economic 
operators acting in good faith should not be held responsible for a failure to meet certain targets 
(e.g., recycled content requirements) due to issues that stem from municipal recycling facilities, 
producer responsibility organizations, or other third parties. We request that the Commission 
ensure that a system exists to temporarily relieve economic operators of their corresponding 
obligations whenever municipal recycling facilities or producer responsibility organizations are not 
providing the services required for economic operators to comply under the regulation.   
 
Please find below our concerns with specific articles of the proposed draft: 
 
Article 6: Recyclable packaging 
1. Food contact material delay: Article 6, points 1 and 2, requires all packaging to be 
recyclable by 1 January 2030. Specifically, recyclable packaging must be designed for 
recycling according to criteria adopted by the Commission. Paragraph 10 of Article 6 
exempts “contact sensitive plastic packaging” of medical devices from this requirement

the same “contact sensitive” grace period. 
 
Food contact materials warrant the same treatment as medical devices and must be 
distinguished from non-food contact materials. Like medical devices, food contact 
materials are subject to strict regulation in the EU and packaging options are limited. 
Safety is also a primary concern that must be addressed. Dairy producers select 
packaging with specific functional requirements to account for a product’s sensitivity to 
temperature, moisture, sunlight, and gas. These external factors can affect shelf-life, 
cause undesirable changes to flavor, texture, and color, or lead to spoilage. Therefore, the 
ideal dairy product package must be carefully designed to use the minimal amount of 
material that maintains the quality, shelf-life, and safety of the product, all while in 
compliance with strict food contact material regulations.   
Dairy packaging is also unique because many dairy products are packaged with mixed 
materials (e.g., plastic liners in bags of dairy powders or plant-based cartons with plastic 
coatings), which serve to protect and preserve the food. These complex materials and 
multicomponent packaging systems may require that the packaging components be 
separated in order to permit recycling or may make recycling extremely difficult or 
impossible.  
 
Because of the complexities associated with food contact materials and the limited 
options, we request that the EC provide flexibility as industry works to develop packaging 
that aligns with the Commission’s stated targets. For this reason, we urge the Commission 
to incorporate Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 into Article 6, Point 10 to exempt food 
contact materials from the recyclable requirement until 31 December 2034. 
 
2. Design for Recycling Criteria Implementing Acts: Article 6, point 3 of the Proposal 
states that all packaging must comply with “design for recycling” criteria by January 1, 
2030. Furthermore, the article states that criteria for determining when packaging is 
“designed for recycling” will be laid down in forthcoming delegated acts. However, the 
Commission’s proposed regulation sets no deadline for these criteria to be finalized.  
 
Modern supply chains can be extremely complex and often require many months or years 
of lead time to implement changes. Additionally, decisions surrounding packaging are 
nuanced and must take into consideration the packaging construction, composition, 
manufacturing, delivery, and suitability for purpose. These decisions require extensive 
research and planning to implement and cannot easily be modified on short notice. To 
prevent uncertainty in the marketplace, to avoid food supply chain disruptions, and to limit 
additional waste created by unusable, non-compliant packaging, the Commission must set 
a firm deadline for the promulgation of the ‘design for recycling’ criteria to allow sufficient

3. Design for Recycling Criteria Consultations: Article 6, point 4 of the proposal 
empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts to supplement this regulation and to 
establish design for recycling criteria, in accordance with Article 58. In turn, Article 58 
requires the Commission to consult experts designated by each member state before 
adopting a delegated act. We request that the EC also separately consult with industry 
and other impacted stakeholders before these criteria are adopted. We strongly believe 
that better regulations are produced where viewpoint diversity exists, and we encourage 
the Commission to give fair consideration to industry voices in its decision-making 
process. 
Article 7: Minimum recycled content in plastic packaging 
1. Implementation delay for food contact materials: Article 7 establishes that the plastic 
part in packaging shall contain a minimum percentage of post-consumer recycled (PCR) 
content recovered from post-consumer waste and outlines an increase from 10% to 50% 
from 2030 to 2040 for contact sensitive plastic packaging. Article 3 sets derogations for 
packaging of certain medical devices and similar products, but no such exemption is 
provided for food packaging materials. Although paragraph 10 does empower the 
Commission to permit flexibility on these minimum recycled content requirements when 
“justified by the lack of availability or excessive prices of specific recycled plastics that may 
have adverse effects on human or animal health, security of food supply or the 
environment, making compliance with the minimum percentages of recycled content set 
out in paragraphs 1 and 2 excessively difficult,” how this provision is implemented in 
practice is unclear. We ask that the Commission recognize that a flexible approach is 
needed and ask that Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 be added to paragraph 3 to exempt 
FCM from the minimum recycled content requirements at this time. 
 
Prior to requiring PCR content requirements in future delegated or implementing acts, we 
request that the EC undertake a separate consultation to allow industry the opportunity to 
provide feedback on realistic timeframes for implementation. The proposed targets for 
PCR content in plastics intended for sensitive applications in the proposal are unrealistic. 
Dairy companies are working with suppliers to increase the domestic availability of PCR-
plastics, but an adequate commercial supply is years away based on current estimates 
due to inadequate waste management organizations and lacking consumer engagement. 
PCR-plastics must meet strict health and safety regulations that must take priority. The 
timeframe for approval of new technologies and packaging materials limits options. We 
request that the Commission work with industry to understand and increase the 
commercial availability of PCR plastic before imposing these new requirements. 
 
2. Calculation of recycled content: Article 7 establishes the percentage of recycled 
packaging in the plastic part by unit of packaging. We request a reconsideration of the 
individual packaging unit assessment so that each economic operator may aim to reach 
the target instead on the percentage of products marketed in the EU, thereby providing the 
needed flexibility for companies to allocate recycled content to specific packaging formats

while meeting overall targets. It is important to limit this calculation to products actually 
marketed in the EU to align with the scope of the regulation, as well as point 54 of the 
introduction which indicates that packaging from third countries entering the Union market 
must comply with this regulation. 
Article 11: Labelling of Packaging  
1. Clarification of material composition: This article establishes packaging labeling 
requirements and specific timeframes for various aspects of this legislation, including the 
labeling of material composition. We encourage the EC to clarify that the intention of this 
section is to list ingredients and additives utilized in the packaging itself; processing aids 
and ingredients utilized in the ink printed on packaging are excluded.  
 
2. QR Codes: As the EC develops implementing regulations that detail specific labeling 
requirements, as noted in paragraph 5, we encourage the EC to consider allowing QR 
codes rather than demand a full ingredients list on labels due to space limitations. 
Article 13: Obligations of manufacturers 
1. Timeframe for retention of records: Paragraph 3 requires manufacturers to keep 
technical documentation and the EU declaration of conformity for 10 years after the 
packaging has been placed on the market. This timeframe is excessive. We propose that 
this figure be halved to 5 years. 
 
2. Timeframe for translation: Paragraph 10 requires manufacturers to provide a translation 
of the conformity information to national authorities within 10 days of request. This 
timeframe is very short, and the regulation does not indicate that an extension may be 
requested. We request that the EC extend this timeframe to 20 days and provide for an 
extension of another 10 days at the request of the economic operator. 
Article 15: Obligations of authorised representative 
1. Timeframe for retention of records: Should the manufacturer appoint an authorized 
representative, paragraph 2(a) requires this party to keep the EU declaration of conformity 
and technical documentation for 10 years after the packaging has been placed on the 
market. In accordance with our above comments, we request that this period be shortened 
to 5 years. 
 
2. Timeframe for translation: Point 2(d) demands that this party make available the 
relevant documents within 10 days of receipt of such a request. As noted in our comments 
to point 13, we request that the 10-day timeframe be extended to 20, and that an 
extension of an additional 10 days be allowed upon request. 
Article 16: Obligations of importers 
1. Labeling: Paragraph 3 of Article 16 requires this draft regulation requires importers to 
indicate on the packaging their name and their registered trade name or registered 
trademark as well as the postal address, and, where available, the electronic means of

intention is to require importers to label this information post-import, once goods are in

2. Timeframe for retention of records: Paragraph 8 requires importers to keep the

Article 20: Identification of economic operators 
Paragraph 2 demands that economic operators keep information on identities of any economic 
operators that have supplied them with packaging for 10 years. We request that this timeframe be 
listed as 5 years in alignment with similar requests above. 
Article 26: Re-use and refill targets 
1. Transport packaging:  Paragraph 7 establishes a special requirement for the re-use of 
transport packaging. We request that the Commission specifically exempt transport 
packaging that is in direct contact with food due to food safety concerns. 
 
2. Flexibility requested: Paragraph 6 of Article 26 requires non-alcoholic beverages 
containing milk fat to be provided in re-usable packaging within a system for re-use or by 
enabling refill at a rate of 10% by 2030 and 25% by 2040. We request that the 
Commission remove this requirement and continue to allow single-use recyclable 
packaging for these products. We urge consideration of the following factors: 
 
a. Maintaining food safety must be the highest priority. The re-use of milk packaging 
can lead to hygiene problems and is not a realistic mandate.  
b. The availability of reusable and refillable packaging that can maintain the level of 
hygiene and shelf-stability is not widely available or cost effective for dairy 
products. In view of the potential food safety risks, these requirements may 
ultimately (although unintentionally) limit access to dairy products for all 
consumers. 
c. The costs associated with this requirement would be substantially higher for foreign 
companies than for those based in the EU. This asymmetry is likely to 
disadvantage foreign producers of beverages and create economic distortions 
within the marketplace.  
d. The carbon footprint associated with sending reusable packaging overseas back to 
foreign suppliers will also be substantially higher than for products produced in the 
EU. This requirement could end up having an overall negative impact on the 
environment.

e. I f re-usable and refillable packaging decreases the shelf-stability of dairy products, 
these restrictions may substantially increase the risk of food waste, contrary to the 
European Union’s Green Deal goals. The environmental disadvantages of reusable 
and refillable systems for dairy products are problematic, particularly in light of the 
potential negative impacts on hygiene, food safety, and the shelf life of dairy 
products.   
Should the Commission not immediately eliminate this requirement despite the challenges 
noted above, we ask that an express permission be provided for the use of non-reusable 
or non-refillable packaging where scientific evidence shows that it is more sustainable than 
reusable and refillable packaging, and that a mechanism be developed to obtain such 
determinations.

3. Labeling: Should re-use requirements on beverages containing milk be implemented as 
outlined, we request that the EC establish additional flexibilities to ensure that stickers and

the EU’s labeling requirements. Some details, such as the lot number and expiration date,

Article 44: Deposit and return systems 
Paragraph 1 requires member states to set up deposit and return systems for single use plastic 
and single use metal beverage containers, both with a capacity of up to three liters. We 
appreciate that paragraph 2 exempts dairy products listed in Part XVI of Annex I to Regulation 
(EU) No 1308/2013 from this mandate. In practice, we expect that this exemption likely only 
applies to a limited number of dairy products, including fluid milk of HS 0401 and drinkable yogurt 
of HS 0403.20. Other dairy products are generally not marketed in plastic bottles. We request that 
the Commission also exempt beverages containing milk of HS 2202.99 from the requirements of 
this section.

Articles 6, 7, 26, and 44. Given the importance and sensitivity of the infant food supply chain, 
accommodations may be necessary for recycled content requirements in the event of shortages. 
As we have recently seen in the United States, infant formula shortages have pronounced 
impacts on families who rely on formula to feed their young children. Infant formula can be the 
sole source of nutrition for infants, and infants often need a particular brand or formulation for a 
variety of reasons. It is thus critically important that supplies of infant formula remain available and 
accessible. Thus, in the event of genuine supply shortages of safe and reliable packaging, 
companies or “economic operators” should be able to claim temporary exemptions from 
“recyclable” or recycled content requirements to avoid disrupting the food supply and to ensure 
that infant food availability on the market. This exemption should apply in the lead-up to 
compliance deadlines as well as after requirements are in effect (i.e., compliance with an 
upcoming deadline or continuing compliance after a deadline has passed can be equally 
challenging in the event of supply shortages).

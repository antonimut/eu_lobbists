Finnish Forest Industries Federation's position on the EU Commission's 
Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation proposal  
 
The Finnish Forest Industries Federation (FFIF) is made up of 71 member companies 
that operate in the pulp, paper, paperboard, and packaging industries as well as in the 
wood products industry. The Federation represents Finland’s entire pulp, paper, and 
paperboard industry as well as about 65% of the wood products industry and is a 
member Confederation of European of Paper Industries (Cepi). The forest industry has 
a significant role in Finland by employing 42,000 people directly and twice as much 
when indirect jobs are included.   
 
FFIF supports the European Commission’s objective in Packaging and Packaging 
Waste Regulation (PPWR) proposal to reduce packaging waste and increase the 
reuse, recyclability, and recycling of packaging materials. It is important that recyclability 
and re-use of packaging are seen as complementary solutions and that the operators 
have the possibility to choose the best packaging solution from environmental, end-use 
and context point of view. It is positive that the recyclability of packaging is approached 
through the "design for recycling" guidelines. At the same time, it is crucial to ensure that 
the PPWR and the products placed on the market contribute to the EU Green Deal 
targets. We believe that some improvements are needed to prevent unintended 
consequences on the environment. 
 
 
General views regarding the PPWR proposal 
 
• Recyclable single-use and reusable packaging should be complementary 
solutions to achieve sustainability. So far, the EU's waste legislation has been 
guided by the basic principle that the generation of waste should be avoided. 
The next best option is re-use, then recycling, then utilization as energy and 
finally landfill. However, it is possible to deviate from the hierarchy if it is possible 
to demonstrate e.g., in a life cycle assessment that other options (than reusable 
packaging) deliver better environmental results. Also, according to EU's circular 
economy action plan, all packaging should be either reusable or recyclable by 
2030.  
 
• The proposed regulatory change to strongly focus on re-use creates uncertainty 
and compromises the success of past and ongoing investments in recycling 
technology. For the past decade, legislation has encouraged industry to develop 
recycling infrastructure and recyclable packaging. Industry has invested more 
and more in new technologies and achieved very high (> 80%) recycling rate of 
paper and cardboard packaging in the EU. The key to achieve even higher 
recycling rates would be to develop and harmonize the waste collection 
infrastructure so that in all EU countries packaging is collected separately per 
material (plastic, paper & cardboard, glass, and metal packaging as their own 
fractions). If all packaging waste is mixed, it will negatively affect the quality and 
yield of recycled fibre from paper and cardboard packaging.

• Climate goals, EU climate legislation and the sustainable carbon cycle policy 
framework guide industry to reduce the use of fossil raw materials. The goal of 
the PPWR proposal to increase reusable packaging means in practice favouring 
plastic packaging in transport, food and drink, and HORECA sectors. Hence, re-
use can have the undesired affect to increase the use of finite fossil feedstock for 
packaging, which goes against the EU climate goals.  
 
 
 
Therefore, we would like to highlight these key challenges, and propose the 
following changes: 
 
1. Overly prescriptive re-use goals for different packaging solutions (Article 26): 
Recyclable single-use and reusable packaging should be complementary solutions 
to achieve the best environmental performance, and when recyclability, recycling 
and collection targets are met, fibre-based s packaging should be exempted from 
the re-use targets in article 26. This approach would be in line with the waste 
hierarchy as defined in the Waste Framework Directive (WFD). 
 
2. Ban on certain types of packaging (Article 22): Instead of bans, the regulation 
should guide the future development towards improving the recycling and mandatory 
collection of single-use packaging in member states, and if recyclability, recycling 
and collection targets are met, fibre-based packaging should be exempted from the 
banned packaging in article 22 in line with the WFD.   
 
3. Mandatory recycled content usage in plastic packaging (Article 7): The 
requirement should be limited to packaging consisting predominantly of plastics and 
exclude plastic parts. When calculating the share of recycled plastic, bio-based 
plastic should be equated with recycled plastic. 
 
4. Industry participation in the preparation of delegated acts regarding 
recyclability and recycling at scale (Article 6): It is crucial to find a cooperative 
method, which would guarantee the strong participation of the industry in the 
preparation of the delegated regulations. CEN should be commissioned to work on 
standards related to recyclability and recycling at scale.

Key challenges in the PPWR proposal in more detail 
 
1. Over-ambitious and very detailed re-use goals for different packaging 
solutions (Article 26) 
• Take away beverage and food packaging would be replaced by reusable 
packaging in the future (up to 40/80% by 2040). 
• Very high re-use targets for e-commerce packaging and transport 
packaging (50% by 2040). 
• Intra and inter industry transport packaging must be 100% reusable 
 
FFIF's position: Re-use should be increased where it is best from an 
environmental point of view. However, unjustified, too detailed, and binding re-
use targets may favour an uptake of non-renewable fossil-based materials which 
can prove more difficult to recycle. Reusability may often imply the use of thicker, 
heavier, and washable materials mostly obtained from fossil sources. Reusability 
targets should be set after considering the environmental impact of raw materials 
extractions, use phase and end of life management. Often recyclability represents 
a better solution than re-use, and where that is the case, it should remain the 
preferred product design characteristic. Economic operators shall hence be 
exempted from the obligation to meet the targets in Article 26 provided that 1) the 
packaging types used (defined in Annex II) have reached 90 % recycling rate 2) 
Are recyclable according to paragraph 2 of Article 6. Additionally, in line with the 
article 26 paragraph 17, the Commission must review the 2040 re-use targets after 
taking stock of the status of 2030 re-use targets. When doing so, it will have to 
assess the fitness and feasibility of the current 2040 objectives in consideration 
of the scientific and technical development in material and product design, 
environmental benefits achieved, effectiveness of the reuse infrastructures and 
systems as well as material recycling performance. 
 
Before reusable packaging is categorically preferred for different uses, it should be clear 
that its overall environmental impact in actual use is lower than the impact of single-use 
alternatives. In addition to environmental impacts, the effects should be evaluated in 
terms of hygiene, food health and safety. The proposal lacks a clear framework and 
rationale for the expansion of re-use systems, including information and evidence on the 
impact of existing systems. For example, knowing how many re-use cycles need to be 
performed to compensate for externalities such as the increased amount of material 
required (reusable packaging is larger and heavier), transportation, washing/cleaning/ 
disinfecting, inspecting, and refurbishing would be important. Reusable packaging is 
often thick-walled, which means that their production requires more raw materials than 
disposable packaging. Reusable packaging should also be recyclable when it reaches 
its end of life (in accordance with Article 6). 
 
There is no 'one size fits all' type solution. The transition to a circular economy requires 
a systemic perspective. The economic operator should have the freedom to choose the 
best possible recyclable or reusable packaging solution in terms of functionality and 
environmental impact during the entire life cycle on a case-by-case basis. This is not 
possible if legislation demands the use of reusable packaging in all applications.

In addition, it should be noted that the transport package is not only intended for 
transport, but also for keeping the product undamaged in storage. Similarly, some 
products are delivered and sold in the same packaging. Hence, the definition of 
transport packaging does not make sense from a technical point of view either. 
Therefore, in industrial transports, instead of 100% re-use, recyclability should be 
required from the packaging material, and sections 12 and 13 in Article 26 should be 
removed from the proposal. Binding re-use obligations can also in some cases, such as 
the metal wires around the transport packaging, interfere with well-functioning recycling 
systems. Intra and inter industry transport respond to specific regulatory and safety 
needs and thus, operators should be left to make their choices once they assess the 
packaging sustainability together with health and safety compliance.  
 
 
2. Ban on certain types of packaging 
• Ban on single-use packaging for portions consumed on site in (fast food) 
restaurants from the beginning of 2030 (cups, serving dishes, wrappers) 
• Ban on single-use packaging of fresh fruits and vegetables (less than 1.5 
kg) unless there is a demonstrated need to avoid water loss, loss of 
rigidity, microbiological hazards, or physical shocks. 
 
FFIF's position: Instead of bans, the regulation should guide the future 
development towards improving the recycling and collection of single-use 
packaging. Bans on certain types of packaging and materials are a worrying 
signal that the Commission is starting to choose the winners and prohibit the use 
of materials without a proper factual basis (e.g., even harmful chemicals cannot 
be banned from the market without thorough investigations). Consumption 
reduction targets have already been set for single-use serving packages in the 
SUP Directive, which is pending a thorough implementation and an assessment of 
its impacts. It is not meaningful to create overlapping regulations. Also, the re-use 
requirements on packaging and materials should be carefully assessed. This 
means that alternatives to restricted packaging application should be evaluated in 
relation to their feasibility (setting of re-use infrastructures and systems), safety 
for consumers, and environmental impacts. Once certified to perform optimally 
and deliver better environmental outcomes, they could be considered.  Similarly, 
when recyclability, recycling and collection targets as explained above are met, 
fibre-based packaging should be exempted from the banned packaging in article 
22.   
 
Banning the marketing of certain types of packaging seems imprudent, and we call for a 
clear and exhaustive assessment of the impact of these provisions, especially regarding 
possible food waste, health, and hygiene consequences for consumers. Regarding fresh 
fruits and vegetables, if the requirement is not well measured and technically defined, it 
automatically means increased food waste. It is very uncertain whether these bans will 
bring environmental benefits when considering the package and the product inside it 
together. Demonstrating the exceptional need as suggested in practice is also very 
challenging. The average packaging represents 3-4 percent of the CO2 footprint of the 
product it contains, so it is important that it is used when it can prevent spoilage/waste of 
products (especially food). It is also not acceptable from the perspective of the 
predictability of the operating environment (especially in the current challenging world

situation) that the Commission leaves to itself the power to add other packaging 
solutions (than those listed in Annex V) to the prohibited list (Article 22) through 
delegated acts. Instead of bans, regulation should guide the development of collection 
and recycling of single-use packaging as equal alternative to reuse options. 
 
 
3. Mandatory recycled content requirement in plastic packaging, which also 
applies to plastic parts of packaging other than plastic 
• Contact-sensitive packaging must contain 10% recycled consumer plastic 
by 2030 and 50% by 2040, 
• For non-contact-sensitive packaging, the requirement is 35% by 2030 
and 65% by 2040 
 
FFIF’s position: By far most of the plastic in packaging is used in packaging made 
entirely of plastic. To make the recycled content requirement effective and 
possible to implement, it should be limited to packaging consisting predominantly 
of plastics by mass and exclude the paints, inks, varnishes, and adhesives in line 
with the SUP Directive. In addition, the parts of the packaging defined as plastic, 
which are a small part of the whole, but have a central function in food contact 
(barrier, coating, etc.) should be left out of the requirement due to food safety and 
hygiene reasons. Adding recycled plastic to the protective coating is also 
technically very difficult, if not impossible, and would require a lot of recycled 
plastic from energy-consuming chemical recycling. The availability of recycled 
material also becomes a problem, considering the current level of plastic 
recycling. Additionally, to solve the climate challenge, bio-based plastic should 
also be taken as an alternative to the use of recycled plastic. 
 
Regulations, which set mandatory targets for the use of recycled plastic in packaging, 
must be followed very closely. In particular, the availability of high-quality plastics with 
specific and important functions (health, safety, durability, etc.) used in many packaging 
applications (paper and cardboard, aluminium, glass) must be assessed and targets set 
accordingly. Hygiene and safety cannot be compromised, so the possibility to deviate 
from the requirements of the PPWR must be applied quickly and efficiently when the 
market capacity cannot satisfy the demand for safe and high-quality recycled plastics. 
 
In the long term, fossil raw materials must also be phased out in plastics, and recycling 
alone is not enough to cover demand. To solve the climate challenge, bio-based plastic 
should also be taken as an alternative to the use of recycled plastic. When calculating 
the share of recycled plastic, bio-based plastic should therefore be equated with 
recycled plastic. Currently, the Commission's PPWR proposal does not include a role for 
bio-based plastics in this context but, to our understanding, require that bio-based 
plastic products must also fulfil recycled raw material content requirement.

4. Industry participation in the preparation of delegated acts regarding 
recyclability and recycling at scale 
• Recyclable packaging shall, from 1 January 2030, comply with the design 
for recycling criteria as laid down in the delegated acts adopted pursuant 
to paragraph 4 
• and, from 1 January 2035, also with the recyclability at scale 
requirements laid down in the delegated acts adopted pursuant to 
paragraph 6.  
 
FFIS's position: The legislative proposal contains large number of secondary 
legislation and delegated power to the Commission (e.g., delegated and 
implementing acts) to be adopted in the future. This makes the regulatory 
landscape unpredictable and the proposal and its impacts difficult to fully 
understand and interpret at this point. Therefore, in the processing of the PPWR 
proposal, a cooperative method should be found, which would guarantee the 
strong participation of the industry in the preparation of the delegated 
regulations. Hence, we propose CEN to be commissioned for this work. 
 
CEN is the EU recognised standardisation body. It is already competent in this field, 
having published the CEN Standard 13430 and many other standards related to 
recycling. The European paper and board industry has developed Paper-Based 
Packaging Recyclability Guidelines and 4evergreen cross industry alliance has 
developed Circularity by Design Guidelines1 to support the value chain in the design of 
paper and board packaging that is recyclable. We ask the policy makers to ensure that 
stakeholders from the industry are involved in the drafting process of DfR guidelines in a 
transparent and systemic manner. This should be explicit in the legislation. This would 
enable an open cooperation and exchange of expertise to set ambitious and realistic 
requirements on packaging recyclability. We therefore propose that the CEN is given the 
mandate to develop the new standards, being the deputed body at EU level for that 
activities.

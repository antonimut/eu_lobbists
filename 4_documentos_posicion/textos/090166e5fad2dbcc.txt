Remarks on the proposed Regulation on packaging and packaging waste 
2 
 
Introduction 
 
The European Commission is working, as part of the objectives of the Green Deal and 
the Circular Economy Plan to reduce CO 2 emissions, waste production and the use of 
raw materials, to review the legislation on packaging and waste of packaging. 
The legislative initiative took the form of a proposal for a Regulation that the Commission 
presented on 30 November 2022. 
The document is very substantial and articulated (65 articles and 13 Annexes) and these 
provisions will have important and transversal impacts for chemical companies as a 
whole (producers of plastic raw materials and users of packaging), as well as for som e 
specific sectors. 
 
 
Key messages 
 
The purpose of this document is to highlight Federchimica's remarks on the Proposal for 
a Regulation on packaging and packaging waste. 
Therefore, the key messages are set out below, developed in more detail later on in the 
document, where the main critical issues of the proposed Regulation and some sectoral 
peculiarities are set out. 
 
 
Safeguard virtuous experiences 
 
The proposed Regulation foresees that the reuse of packaging takes priority over the 
recycling of packaging waste , prohibiting applications and dictating binding reuse 
objectives to be achieved, without considering the risk of compromising the functions 
that packaging must ensure to protect the products and avoid damage and waste and, 
above all, without making the most of the virtuous experiences that have  been 
developed in some countries, such as Italy through the so-called “CONAI System 
(National Packaging Consortium) ”, to achieve the packaging waste recycling 
objectives dictated by the current European legislation. 
The Italian model of packaging waste management from a circular economy perspective 
represents excellence on the European scene. 
In 2021, 73% of the packagi ng placed on the market was recycled, reaching the 
recycling targets set by Europe for 2030 (70%) 9 years in advance.

The need for proportionate measures 
 
• The proposed measures determine a model of production and consumption according 
to which the container becomes more important than the content to be protected, 
conserved, used or consumed in a correct and appropriate manner and – sometimes 
– from which the user/consumer must be protected; 
 
• the proposal for a Regulation not only establishes very ambitious objectives 
(collection, reuse, recycling, content of recycled material in packaging) but it also 
chooses the technologies with which to achieve these objectives thus determining 
a heavy impact on the entire production sector  (producers of raw materials, 
producers and users of packaging), which over time has developed increasingly 
advanced packaging to better protect the product and at the same time, where 
possible, improve the environmental performance of the product  (for example 
by reducing food waste) or of the packaging  (lighter, more easily sortable or 
recyclable packaging, etc.); 
 
• the measures of the Regulation do not take into account the important role of 
packaging in preventing the production of food waste, its safety and food safety, 
nor the need to ensure hygiene in the use of products. Likewise, with reference to 
dangerous chemical products, the Regulation does not adequately take into account 
the existence of specific Regulations that dictate the characteristics that packaging 
must possess ( Regulations for the transport of dangerous goods) or the safety 
information that they must convey; 
 
• in relation to the percentages of recycled material that must necessarily be 
present in the packaging, it does not appear that the actual availability on the market 
of the quantities of recycled materials necessary to ensure the percentages proposed 
has been verified, considering the current yields of the collection, selection and 
recycling. 
More generally, an assessment of the economic and social impact of this proposal 
and of the real environmental benefits associated with the proposed measur es has 
not been carried out; 
 
• the obligation is envisaged for the operators concerned to adhere to Extended 
Producer Responsibility Systems in each of the Member States on whose 
territory the operator places packaging or packaged products , significantly 
increasing the burdens on the companies without increasing the level of 
environmental protection; 
 
• the proposed Regulation refers in several articles to delegated acts that will be 
published later, without providing for sufficient transitio nal periods for 
companies to adapt to the new provisions; 
 
• chemical recycling, and its role in achieving recycling targets, needs to be properly 
and clearly framed within the proposed measures. The chemical industry is facing the 
circular transition and is  currently investing to develop large chemical recycling 
capabilities. A clear legal framework therefore needs to be created: predicting that the 
criteria for accounting for chemically recycled materials will be defined by an 
implementing act by 31 December 2026 is too far in the future and unnecessarily 
prolongs legal uncertainty , thus hampering transition planning in progress. As 
recently requested by all the European Associations of chemical and plastics

General remarks 
 
1. Ensure technological neutrality 
 
For chemical companies, packaging is not just a container to be used for transport, but 
it is a means of protecting the product and avoiding its deterioration, to ensure correct 
use and dosage, to transfer information on the composition, safe use, indications for the 
management of any emergencies, safety advice and uses advised against. 
Over time, packaging and plastic materials for packaging have undergone an evolution 
in a logic of sustainability: packaging has become lighter, they have been made - at least 
in part - with recycled or biodegradable materials, they have ensured product 
conservation times always longer or have helped the professional user or the final 
consumer to dose or use the product better (or in a safer way). 
Innovation and technological development are the essential elements that have led to 
the management of products in a more efficient and sustainable way, to improve the 
environmental performance of the product or its packaging and, when possible, to 
improve the sortability and recyclability of packaging waste. 
 
The Regulation proposal jeopardizes what has been done so far, by imposing a model 
of production and consumption according to which the container becomes more 
important than the contents and the reuse of packaging and the recycling of packaging 
waste at all costs risk going into contrast with the functions that packaging must ensure 
to protect the products and avoid damage and waste. 
In fact, the proposed Regulation not only defines several very ambitious objectives but 
also chooses the technologies (reusable packaging instead of disposable packaging) 
with which to achieve these objectives. 
 
Strong opposition is therefore expressed towards the bans on the use of certain types of 
packaging (e.g. small-sized packaging for the hotel sector, for which there is a specific 
chapter below, and catering). Indeed, the restrictions for the items listed in Annex V are 
not justified by any impact assessment and do not take into account either the properties 
of the materials or the effecti veness of the recycling systems already consolidated in 
some Member States, such as Italy, which allow for sustainable management even when 
used in single-use applications. 
This approach therefore risks nullifying the efforts and investments made by the mo st 
virtuous industrial sectors and countries which have led to the achievement and 
surpassing of the European packaging recycling targets to date. 
 
Furthermore, the possible elimination of numerous single -use packaging with the entry 
into force of the Regu lation represents an unjustified market intervention capable of 
creating obstacles to international trade and appears to be in contrast with the rules 
established by the World Trade Organization, in particular the Article 2.2 of the 
Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade. According to this provision, the technical 
standards must not be more restrictive than what is necessary to achieve a given 
objective. In the specific case, the objective of reducing single -use packaging waste 
could be achieved through t he application of other and less restrictive measures, such 
as, for example, the introduction of mandatory requirements for separate collection and 
recycling of single-use packaging products in the HORECA sector. 
The introduction of bans on the use of sing le-use packaging in retail distribution and in 
the HORECA sector does not appear to be justified by an analysis of the life cycle and 
does not comply with the hygiene, health and food safety requirements established by 
European legislation.

Considering this, the proposed restrictions should be reviewed and their impact on 
human health properly assessed. 
 
The measures of the Regulation do not take into account the important role of 
packaging in preventing the production of food waste and its safety, which must be 
carefully evaluated to avoid undesirable consequences on consumer health and food 
safety, nor the need to ensure hygiene in the use of cosmetic products in hotels. This 
assessment does not seem to have been carried out in the formulation of the propo sal: 
a “generalization” that leads to a total underestimation of the “diversity” of food products, 
or in general perishable and fragile products, which are protected only thanks to a highly 
performing and “tailored” packaging. The possible consequences on consumers’ health 
and on the waste of resources and on the production of waste appear enormous. 
 
Finally, marketing and consumer acceptance should also be taken into consideration 
within the Regulation. Otherwise, the proposal could lead to the standardization of the 
packaging, thus limiting the differentiation of the brand and, with it, the presentat ion of 
the products, with clear repercussions on the added value created, since the packaging 
is not only a container. 
 
 
2. Avoid unnecessary complexity 
 
Packaging compliance 
A rigid system of demonstrating the compliance of the packaging with the “ecodesign” 
requirements established by the Regulation is envisaged, with significant administrative 
and bureaucratic burdens for the operators. 
The process of assessing and demonstra ting the compliance of packaging with the 
requirements established by the Regulation appears very similar to the processes 
envisaged for certain products, which are much more complex or which present 
dangerous characteristics, which deserve particular attention to allow them to be placed 
on the market. 
This system appears excessive and unnecessarily burdensome, especially if we think of 
types of packaging widely used for transport such as wooden pallets and cardboard 
packaging. 
 
Environmental labelling 
Although the transition from Directive to Regulation could be an opportunity to achieve a 
correct harmonization at European level of the provisions relat ed to the environmental 
labelling of packaging, which currently differs from country to country, it wou ld be 
desirable to standardize the provisions at EU level, using as a basis what already exists 
and in part applied by the Member States and to clarify already in the text of the 
Regulation the possibility of using digital tools to fulfil these information obligations (in 
line with what has already been done by the Italian Government regarding the 
environmental labelling of packaging). 
Furthermore, the mention to Member States that have additional labelling requirements 
in Article 4(5), in addition  to those  set out in Article 11, should be deleted, as it is 
inconsistent with a harmonized labelling and it contributes to market fragmentation 
hindering the free movement of goods and their packaging. 
 
Systems for the reuse of packaging and Extended Producer Responsibility Systems 
The requirement to set up organized systems for the re -use of packaging  is greatly 
strengthened; this includes  transport packaging such as pallets and boxes, and the 
obligation – for affected individuals – to join Extended Producer Responsibility Schemes

This provision and this approach appear excessively rigid, very difficult to apply and

3. Reuse and recycle 
 
In general, the mandatory recycled material content targets  proposed for plastic 
packaging seem to have been identified without an assessment of the actual feasibility, 
disregarding a mechanism for verifying the real availability on the European market of 
recycled material suitable for the purposes (it would therefore be appropriate to include 
a safeguard mechanism). 
The proposal to set recycled content targets at the level of the individual plastic 
packaging unit is an essential requirement, failure to mee t which will prevent non -
compliant packaging from being placed on the EU market. It should be noted that for 
some types of packaging, such as flexible plastics (which cannot be used in contact with 
food unless they come from chemical recycling processes ), it will be impossible to 
demonstrate compliance at unit level. A recycled content target calculated as the 
average of all plastic packaging placed on the market  by an economic operator is 
the only way to enable the required result for this type of packaging to be achieved. This 
will give manufacturers the flexibility to allocate recycled content based on available 
supply and technical feasibility and give them a realistic chance of achieving the target. 
Additionally, it is crucial to consider the geography of where recycling processes are 
possible, as this could still be harmful to the environment if materials have to be shipped 
significant distances to a limited number of recycling plant sites. 
These targets also do not take into account bottlenecks affecting the availability, quality 
and cost of recycled plastics. Furthermore, pending the adoption of chemical recycling 
legislation and given the limited availability of recycled polymers which could be included 
in “touch sensitive” applications (e.g. food, ph armaceuticals and cosmetics), the 
proposed objectives cannot be achieved without compromis ing the functionality of the 
packaging and therefore the protection of the product. 
 
Finally, it should be highlighted that it is not desirable that certain types of packaging 
waste that have contained dangerous substances/products are reused, in consideration 
of possible problems of “contamination” of the product by the residues of the previous 
content, or that the secondary raw materials deriving from recycling are used to produce 
packaging to be used in particular applications, such as packaging for medicines. 
 
As regards the reuse objectives, taking into account that, if reuse is already, in fact, a 
proven reality for some materials such as glass (in the HORECA channel, with returnable 
containers), the transition to a system of reuse of plastic packaging for drinks, and in 
particular for water, would require massive investments by companies, in addition to the 
complete overhaul of industrial plants. It should absolutely not be forgotten that Italian 
industry has, in fact, invested in eco -design and eco-planning in recent years, so much 
so that today it has the lightest plastic bottles in Europe. This virtuous model would be

penalized by the introduction of mandatory transversal reuse targets for each type of 
packaging. 
 
Among others, the provisions related to the re-use of transport packaging  appear 
critical: first of all, imposing obligations to re -use transport packaging, associated with 
obligations to set up re -use systems, would lead to serious complications and 
bureaucratic and operational burdens in the logistics sector. Furthermore, it must be  
considered that there are transport packaging which, by their nature, is not reusable: this 
is the case, for example, of the plastic film for palletisation ( “pallet wrapping”) which is 
cut upon the arrival at the destination of the goods, thus losing their characteristics, and 
subsequently sent for recycling. 
 
Despite the challenges that economic operators will have to face in terms of 
infrastructure investments necessary to implement reuse systems, the proposed 
Regulation further increases the ambition of all the reuse objectives set by art. 26 starting 
from 2040. However, it prescribes obligations for the reuse of packaging quotas, 
regardless of the material with which they are made, their characteristics and the 
characteristics and peculiarities of the models of each Member State, without taking into 
account in any way the results alre ady achieved and, therefore, individual industrial 
propensities. This is not believed to be the correct approach. 
 
Furthermore, the operations of preparation for reuse require significant 
consumption of water and energy, therefore making reuse not always t he choice 
with the lowest environmental impact. 
 
The introduction of any reuse objectives should only be envisaged where it is clearly 
demonstrated that this makes sense from an environmental and ecological point of view, 
therefore on the basis of verifiable and comparable technical-scientific evidence. 
 
 
4. Scope 
 
It is considered appropriate to exclude packaging for the transport of dangerous 
goods from the scope of the Regulation. 
The Regulations governing the transport of dangerous goods, in fact, are different for 
each mode of transport. 
The land transport Regulations (ADR road, RID rail and ADN inland waterways) are 
regulated by European Directive 2008/68/EC and subsequent amendments which occur 
every two years and which require national transposition; the Regulations for maritime 
and air  transport are worldwide and are applied in every country with provisions of 
national nature. It is therefore difficult to have a unitary reference for these Regulations. 
Dangerous goods must be transported using approved packaging, i.e. the prototypes of 
these packaging must pass tests filled with the material to be transported or with similar 
materials to verify their resistance to the stresses to which they are subjected during 
transport. They therefore have very high construction standards, specific to the 
characteristics of each individual material. 
For the construction of plastic packaging, the Regulations themselves provide for the use 
of recycled material from used industrial packaging, but these materials must satisfy 
specific construction, mechanical and technical properties that make them suitable for 
containing new dangerous goods (see standard ISO 16103:2005). 
Furthermore, the transport Regulations already provide for and regulate with specific 
procedures the possibility of reusing, reconditionin g and reconstructing packaging 
containing dangerous goods.

5. Transition period 
 
Foreseeing a ban on non -recyclable packaging from a certain date without having yet 
issued the recyclability criteria (DfR) involves the risk of stopping investments, especially 
in the search for innovative solutions, and the difficulty for companies to adapt to the new 
provisions in time. 
 
Furthermore, the proposed Regulation does not provide for the introduction of measures 
aimed at building adequate infr astructures for the selection, collection and recycling of 
packaging waste in Europe, to allow their recycling in practice. Designing recyclable 
packaging alone is not enough if it is not supported by a system capable of triggering 
investment in recycling infrastructure across Europe, thus enabling Member States to 
meet their recycling rate targets. 
 
Given the impact on production processes and product innovation for adaptation to new 
legal requirements, it is recommended to allow industry, which operates w ith complex 
supply chains, an adequate timeline, also in view of the need to develop harmonize d 
standards. 
 
 
6. Enhancement of chemical recycling 
 
The chemical industry is facing the circular transition  and is currently investing to 
develop large chemical recycling capabilities. Major investments (on a commercial scale) 
covering different recycling technologies have already been announced. 
The plastics industry, in fact, in recent years has not only incr eased the quality and 
volumes of mechanical recycling, but has developed the so -called chemical recycling 
technology which in fact complements mechanical recycling and which allows, through 
appropriate processes, to return to the original raw material (the  monomer), to then 
produce again the same polymer as the one from which we started. However, this 
process is currently struggling to be recognized as a possible way of recycling plastic 
materials, without taking into account the scientific evidence and the advantages it could 
bring to the reduction of waste and the increase in packaging recycling. In addition, 
therefore, to the approval of chemical recycling as a recycling system, it would then be 
necessary to establish the shared calculation methods to est ablish the percentage of 
material from chemical recycling used in the production of the polymer. 
In perspective, chemical recycling could take care of everything that does not go to 
mechanical recycling, ensuring the full circularity of plastic products. A s already 
mentioned, all the European associations of chemical and plastics companies (about 30) 
have asked to define by the end the calculation criterion of the recycled content coming 
from Chemical Recycling that uses the so-called “Mass Balance” method. 
 
Foreseeing that the criteria for accounting for chemically recycled materials will be 
defined in an implementing act by 31 December 2026 is too far in the future and 
unnecessarily prolongs legal uncertainty . To promote the circular economy, it is 
important to clarify the legal framework from the outset, as major investment decisions 
depend on legal certainty. 
 
Finally, it will also be necessary to clarify to what extent different recycling technologies, 
such as chemical recycling, will be taken into account in the calculation of the minimum 
recycled content, to avoid blocking the investments that have already been announced.

For example, the European association PlasticsEurope, which represents producers of 
plastic raw materials, has communicated a signifi cant increase in investments planned

7. Clearly and correctly define the “substances of concern” (SoC) 
 
The chemical industry fully supports the European Commission's goal of making 
products sustainable by bringing products to the market that are suitable for a climate -
neutral, resource-efficient and circular economy, but in order to achieve this goal it is 
essential to: 
- maintain any restrictions on chemicals for chemical safety, under the REACH 
Regulation; 
- limit the scope of the restrictions in the PPWR and limit the definition of substances 
of concern (SoC) to substances that are relevant for the recyclability and reusa bility 
of packaging. In this regard, there is strong concern about the extremely broad 
definition of SoC foreseen by the proposed Regulation on eco-design of sustainable 
products (ESPR); 
- have clear and transparent processes, with decisions based on impact assessments; 
- consult the interested parties during the process. 
 
It should also not be forgotten that, since substances in production processes outside 
the EU cannot be controlled, regulating them only within the EU may put EU producers 
at a disadvantage compared to non -EU operators, as it would not be possible to 
guarantee a level playing field. 
Therefore, the requirements on SoC should only be applied if the following criteria are 
met: (i) a substance is present in the packaging placed on th e market and its presence 
can be measured and (ii) the presence of the substance creates direct consequences 
for the sustainability or recycling.

Sectorial issues 
 
In addition to the general comments set out above, there are also some specific sectorial 
critical issues, which are reported below. 
 
 
1. Pharmaceutical products and medical and in vitro diagnostic medical devices 
 
At the moment it does not seem possible to foresee a timeline for the application of the 
recyclability requirements to pharmaceutical packaging, medical devices and in vitro 
diagnostic medical devices. 
Pharmaceutical packaging has the function of preserving the integrity and quality of 
medicines to protect the health and safety of patients and healthcare professionals and, 
for this reason, the composition of the primary packaging is determined by specifi c 
Regulations/guidelines and it is part of the authorization dossier that regulatory agencies 
must approve before the drug is placed on the market. Furthermore, the modification or 
introduction of a new packaging involves a research and development process that can 
last up to 10 years to determine its absence of impact on the quality, safety and efficacy 
of the product, with significant investments by pharmaceutical companies. 
Similar considerations are also applicable to medical devices and in vitro diagno stic 
medical devices, whose packaging plays the essential role of preserving the 
characteristics of the device or in vitro diagnostic they contain, as established by the 
manufacturer (in some cases also with preserve the sterility of the product contained) . 
The recently applied European Regulations 2017/745 and 2017/746 impose specific 
requirements for the packaging of medical devices and in vitro diagnostics, the 
compliance of which is, in the majority of cases, subject to review by a competent Notified 
Body for assessment of product conformity for the consequent issue of the CE certificate. 
Also, technical standards are applicable to the design of the packaging of the a bove-
mentioned products, to which the device manufacturers already refer for the 
development, design and validation of the product packaging. 
The subjection to the Regulation also of these types of packaging is critical, as 
pharmaceutical products, due to their characteristics, can influence the possibility of 
recyclability of the packaging t hat contains them, especially in the case of plastic 
packaging. 
Therefore, while acknowledging the urgent challenges for sustainability, given the 
current specific regulatory framework regulating drugs, medical devices and in vitro 
diagnostics and R&D time required for new packaging, the timelines proposed in Articles 
6 (recyclable packaging) and 7 (minimum recycled content in plastic packaging) do not 
appear realistic and it would be necessary to make the derogation permanent for the 
application of the rec yclability requirements of pharmaceutical packaging, medical 
devices and in vitro diagnostics. 
 
In addition, the plastic packaging used for medicines must be of a degree of purity such 
as not to allow any contamination from the plastic material. In conside ration of this, the 
current guideline of the American Food and Drug Administration (FDA) “Guidance for 
Industry Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics ” 
explicitly prohibits the use of recycled plastic in the production of primary packaging. Also 
considering the reasons already expressed on the exemption from the recyclability 
requirements of pharmaceutical packaging, medical devices and in vitro diagnostic 
medical devices, it is recommended that the exemption on the minimum conten t of 
recycled plastic in plastic packaging is confirmed and made permanent.

2. Cosmetics 
 
Packaging for cosmetic and personal care products  requires the use of high quality 
secondary raw materials to ensure the highest consumer safety standards, as required 
by the European Regulation on Cosmetic Products (Regulation (EC) n. 2009/1223). For 
this reason, it is essential to keep the inclusion of  cosmetics within the proposed 
definition of "contact-sensitive packaging" (article 3, point 40). 
For cosmetic and personal care products, the packaging plays a fundamental role in the 
conservation and presentation of the product and the provisions of article 9 on the 
“Minimization of packaging” are critical. 
A relationship between “weight minimization ” and material composition would be 
needed, to distinguish between the variety of materials available in packaging and their 
different weights. 
Furthermore, recycling targets should be set at company/portfolio level rather than at 
packaging unit level, in order to compensate for product categor ies where integrating 
recycled content into packaging may be less feasible. It will also give manufacturers the 
flexibility to allocate recycled content based on available supply and technical feasibility 
and give them a realistic chance of achieving the target. 
Finally, there is no need to prohibit the placing on the market of cosmetic products for 
hotels with a nominal content of < 50 ml for liquid products and < 100 ml for non -liquid 
products, as envisaged by art. 23, with reference to Annex V. 
 
 
3. Gas cylinders  
 
Cylinders for “technical” gas (e.g. oxygen, nitrogen, argon, etc.) or LPG do not only have 
the function of “containing” the “gas” product intended for sale and distribution. In fact, 
the gas cannot be used without its confinement system and the cylinder is equipped with 
“working” and “functional” devices for the use of the product (valve, pressure gauge, 
pressure reducer, rupture disc, etc.). For this reason, the European TPED Directive 
(Directive 2010/35/EU, on transportable pressure equipment,  transposed in Italy with 
Decree 12 June 2012, n. 78) considers them Transportable Pressure “Equipment”. 
 
Refillable cylinders, used for the transport of compressed, liquefied and dissolved gases 
(including LPG and methane), even if classified as packaging, should be excluded from 
the environmental labelling obligation as the labelling and punching already required by 
law currently in force (TPED Directive and ADR) uniquely identify the metallic material 
and the owner of the container, for which the cylinders represent a patrimonial asset, to 
which they must be returned after use for subsequent fillings. At the end of their life, they 
are sold directly to foundries as scrap metal after being put out of use and cut according 
to a procedure established by the reference standards. 
 
 
4. Compostable and renewable bioplastic packaging 
 
The use of compostable and renewable bioplastic packaging should be encouraged, 
since these are materials that contribute decisively to having a high quantity and quality 
of organic fraction collected and sent for recycling, an aspect on which Italy excels in 
Europe, as evidenced by specific data. 
In particular, for compostable and renewable bioplastic packaging, it is required: 
- that they are made up of at least 60% renewable raw materials; 
- that they are not subject to obligations to use reusable packaging;

- that they do not encounter limitations of use for all applications in contact with food, 
except the compliance with current European legislation on the matter. 
 
It is also important to underline that the role of compostable bioplastics can combine 
hygiene and food safety. The EC proposal, instead of enhancing them, ends up 
penalizing if not prohibiting these applications. 
 
Furthermore, we want to underline a further critical  issue, linked to the treatment of 
“coffee capsules”, which have revolutionized the use of coffee for millions of people and 
which constitute a rapidly expanding sector. 
In fact, the aluminium and plastic capsules preserve the quality of the coffee they contain 
for a long time, optimizing and limiting the waste of the “packaging” material and its 
contents. Furthermore, the capsules are easy to recycle, through already tested and 
effective systems, schemes and supply chains. Discriminating applications in aluminium 
and plastic would violate the principle of neutrality in the choice of material (a cornerstone

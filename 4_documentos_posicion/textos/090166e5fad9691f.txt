Minderoo presentation 
Minderoo Foundation is one of the largest philanthropies in Australasia. Since its inception in 2001, it has invested 
more than AUD$2.6 billion funds in philanthropic causes and has supported over 230 initiatives at global level.  
In the European Union, via our No Plastic Waste Initiative, we are actively advocating for laws and policies which 
eliminate the negative impacts of plastics on people and the planet" through increasing transparency in the 
plastic value chain, investing in new technology and innovations, and by working with  industry and policymakers 
on reduction and recycling of plastic waste.  
Minderoo vision for the packaging and packaging waste regulation 
Minderoo welcomes the Commission's proposal fo r a revision of EU legislation for regulation on Packaging and 
Packaging Waste (PPWR proposal), to prevent negative environmental impacts of packaging and bolster reuse 
and recycling. 
 
The European Union has shown a commitment to transitioning towards a circular economy and production and 
use of safe and sustainable chemicals. This was shown in its Circular Economy Package II, its Chemical Strategy 
for Sustainability i and the plastics strategy  with the common goal 
 circular economy in which th e 
design and produ ction of plastics and plastic products fully respect re -use, repair, and recycling needs and in 
which more sustainable materials are developed and promoted
 ii. Now, this proposal offers  an important 
opportunity for the much-needed transition towards safer, more sustainable, and circular packaging systems at 
a time where it is crucial to prevent emissions, environmental pollution, biodiversity losses and non-renewable 
resource use in all sectors.

For a regulation that takes consumer and citizen health into account 
The inclusion needs of safety criteria within regulation. 
The PPWR proposal such as the proposal for Ecodesign for Sustainab le Products Regulation (ESPR) xxv are too 
vague on substances of concern and do not incentivise the elimination of harmful chemicals in the design stage 
of packaging. Indeed, the health issue need to be at the core of this regulation, linked with Chemicals Strategy for 
Sustainabilityxxvi objectives to 
 reduce negative impacts on human health and the environment associated with 
chemicals, materials, products and services commercialised or introduced onto the EU market. In particular, the 
ambition of the CSS is to phase out the most harmful substances and substitute, as far as possible all other 
substances of concern, and otherwise minimise their use and track them.
  
 
Plastic is not inert. Chemical additives are usually not chemically bonded to the polymer and leach from plastics 
during use and from waste xxvii. They can enter the human body through ingestion, inhalation and/or skin 
absorption, as well as into the developing foetus through maternal blood supplyxxviii. There is increasing evidence 
of animalxxix and human health impacts related to plastic chemical exposure xxxxxxixxxiixxxiii. More than 2,400 of the 
approximately 10,500 substances used in plastics are identified  as substances of potential concern xxxiv. 
Fragmentation of plastic through use or following disposal leads to the production and release into the 
environment of microplastics. A number of studies have reported the detection of microplastics in human colon, 
lung, liver, blood and placentaxxxv. Even smaller particles - - - nano plastics - - - are probably also entering the bodyxxxvi, 
and blood xxxvii but accurate detection methods that are also free from sample contamination still need to be 
developedxxxviii. The work is underway to do thisxxxix. 
 
In our assessment, the global social cost s resulting from plastic-related human health effects are significant. A 
recent study conducted by Minderoo Founda tion in partnership with U nited Nations Environment Programme 
(UNEP)Principles of Sustainable Insurance, law firm Clyde & Co., and risk analytics experts Praedicat, examined 
the social costs and potential corporate liabilities associated with plastic poll ution. It estimated that the global 
social costs from plastic-related pollution already amount to hundreds of billions of dollars each year xl. Much of 
the estimated costs are driven by bodily harm from exposure to plastic chemicals. As an example, the Minderoo-
Monaco Commission on Plastics and Human Health  estimates health costs of $490 billion for deaths from 
exposure to di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), $166 billion for coronary heart disease from exposure to BPA and 
$62.4 billion for cases of stroke from exposure to BPA (US damages only, 2015 PPP). Certain harms - - - including 
the impacts from micro - and nano-plastic- - - while not yet fully understood  due to limited measurement in the 
general population, the ocean and soils, are potentially catastrophic in the long term xli. Humans can be directly 
exposed to plastic additives and legacy POPs from use of plastic products as well as indirectly via the food chain. 
Human exposure to microplastics via the food chain may also occurxlii 
 
In this regard, we call on co -legislators to include a clear link to the REACH restrictions process and Chemical 
Strategy for Sustainability when a risk to human health or the environment is identified arising from the use of a 
substance in the manufacture of packaging or packaging components, or from a substance present in packaging 
or packaging components when they are placed on the market, or during their subsequent life cycle stages, 
including the waste phase - - - including therefore the recyclable feedstocks and materials - - - such as bioplastics - - - 
that also use hazardous chemicalsxliii.

Our proposal for a negative list of unsafe packaging ecodesign choice 
Linked with the Farm to Fork Strategy of the European Commission  and the work of the Commission in the 
Chemicals Strategy for Sustainabilityxliv, the European co-legislators need to restrict from use in packaging those 
substances recognised in the EU as substances of concern and very hig h concern. Indeed, the elimination of 
chemicals known to harm human health supports European ambitions towards safe material circularity, including 
increased food packaging recycling rates, since known hazardous chemicals would then not be used in virgin 
materials and therefore not perpetuated in recycled packaging materialxlv. 
 
Since the REACH regulation revision xlvi and the Food Contact Material Regulation xlvii are facing unprecedented 
delays, Minderoo supports the inclusion of a negative list  of unsafe packaging ecodesign choices that identifies 
substances to  ban in the EU internal market by 2025.  This negative list would give agency to  packaging 
companies to avoid or limit negative impacts on the environment and human health , including occupational 
safety, in their choice of packaging materials and chemical additives. It can also elevate the value of EU products 
with assurance of safety standards.  
 
We propose a negative list include substances of very high concern as defined in Article 57 of Regulation (EC) No 
1907/2006 (REACH Regulation)  or ident ified in REACH Annex XIV -Authorization List xlviii. It would include 
substances and mixtures relevant to packaging that are classified as carcinogenic, mutagenic and reprotoxic 
(CMR), persistent, bioaccumulative and toxic (PBT) , very persistent and very bioaccum ulative (vPvB) , or 
persistent, mobile and toxic (PMT) xlix. This is consistent with EU legislation regarding Classification Labelling and 
Packaging of substances.lTaking a precautionary approach, potential substances of very high concern based on 
structural similarityli, should also be listed until sufficient evidence for safety is provided. 
 
Chemical safety hazards of packaging can result from leakage from the material or leakage of the material itself 
into the environment. If a chemical is persistent, increas ing concentrations in the environment will increase the 
probability of occurrence of known and unknown effects and once adver se effects are identified it will take 
decades or longer to remediate, or, in worst cases may be irreversible.lii

For an ambitious EU policy on the prevention and reduction of packaging   
As underlined by Minderoo with ECOS, European Environmental Bureau, New ERA - New European Reu se 
Alliance, Recycling Netwerk Benelux, Reloop and Zero Waste Europe in a joint statement published 11April 2023 
for ambitious EU policy on reuse on the prevention and reduction of packaginglxv.  
 
The EU has the opportunity to prevent waste and scale up reus e for one key sector, packaging, with the 
negotiations of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). Indeed, prioritising prevention and reuse 
can significantly contribute to reducing emissions and achieving climate targets, while also preventing pollution, 
drastically cutting on energy and resource use, and benefitting SMEs and communities. As an examplelxvi , scaling 
up reuse systems in Europe by just 20% in just three promising product groups (food and beverages takeaway, 
e-commerce and household care products) by 2027 would decrease 1.3 million tonnes of CO2 -equivalent 
emissions, save nearly 2.5 billion cubic metres of water, and use 10 million fewer tonnes of materials annually.  
Furthermore, studies show that reuse systems provide the highest op portunity to reduce plastic pollution (a 
reduction of 30% by 2040) by replacing some of the most problematic and unnecessary productslxvii.  
 
The annual impacts of scaling these systems by 50% by 2030 would prevent 3.7 million tonnes of emissions from 
being re leased, conserve 10 billion cubic metres of water, and avoid the use of ne arly 23 million tonnes of 
resources. These estimates are based on conservative estimates for Europe alone, which means far greater 
benefits if reuse systems are deployed worldwide, at scale.  
 
The EU gave the first signal on plastic and packaging reduction with the Single Use Plastics (SUP) Directive and 
can now assume a leadership role by pushing targets and measures on prevention and reuse at the EU level, and 
at international scale with the plastics treaty negotiations.

Reuse - single use : consumers habits and demand 
Consumers are becoming more aware of the impact that their packaging choices have on the environment, 
particularly in relation to single-use and non-recyclable packaging. Slowly but surely, the culture of "use, discard, 
pollute" is being challenged. As stated by a recent report lxviii , 70% of European consumers are actively taking 
steps to reduce their use of plastic packaging. Moreover, packaging preferences are also changing towards items 
that are reusable. Another survey carried out by WWF France in 2019 lxix showed that 88% of consumers favour 
systems which allow the reuse of bottles and containers. 
Indeed, consumers are becoming mo re interested in sustainable options lxx , this is why the PPWR presents an 
important opportunity to establish a sound framework for reusable packaging to become the norm and thereby 
change the general consumer habit in the EUlxxi . 
Reuse - single use : 
 he environmental impact? 
While niche examples can always be found where a reuse system may not be the most effective environmental 
choice along one of the environmental indicators (e.g. carbon impact, plastic consumption, water use, etc.), 
reusable packaging is in general a more sustainable choice compared to single-use packaginglxxiilxxiii.  
 
Researchlxxiv shows that when scaled effectively, reuse systems have a lower carbon impact per use, higher 
resource efficiency per gram, and lower likelihood of contributing to mismanaged waste such as littering. Reuse 
is identified by leading organisations such as UNEP lxxv WWFlxxvi and Ellen Macarthur Foundation lxxvii as critical to 
addressing the current environmental crisis. This is also confirmed by large coalitions of public and private 
operators, such as the Global Co mmitment 2022lxxviii endorsed by more than 500 organisations and the Plastic 
Pacts signed up in many European countries. However, the reality is that reusable packaging systems do not face 
a level -playing field because producers an d users of single -use packagin g do not pay for the environmental 
impact of their products. Existing extended producer responsibility and other systems do not adequately level 
the playing field either. 
 
The setting of mandatory reuse and refill targets for certain packaging formats and markets is a good starting 
point. However, we believe there is scope for more ambitious targets. Moreover, there are important issues that 
have been overlooked in the proposal and that need to be taken into account by co -legislators to achieve truly 
sustainable, convenient and efficient reuse systems. 
Reuse and refill targets should not be combined 
Article 26 of the proposed PPWR refers indistinctly to reuse and refill targets f or food and beverages in sales 
packaging, leaving the choice to the operator to meet the objectives either by implementing a system for reuse 
or enabling refill. As recognised in the definitions in Article 3, reuse and refill are different approaches to 
packaging. Refill means an operation by which an end -user fills its own container. In this sense, the container is 
not a packaging but a consumer -owned product. Therefore, the action of refill by a consumer should be 
considered as a measure to prevent packaging waste as underlined by recital 65 of the proposallxxix. On the other 
hand, reuse means an operation by which reusable packaging is used again for the same purpose for which it was 
conceived. In this case, reusable packaging is an asset owned by the system  operator, which ensures it is 
collected, washed and refilled. Due to their clear differences and distinct levels of maturity of data collection, the 
targets for achieving one or the other should not be combined in order to avoid the risk of huge  margins of error 
and unreliable datalxxx . 
 
In addition, reuse and refill targets are at two different levels of the waste hierarchy, which is particularly important 
when it comes to the implementation and enforcement of the measures.

The main issues arising from merging reuse and refill targets are:  
✓ different calculation methods and metrics. Reusable packaging in systems for reuse is easily traceable 
by units (e.g. with a serial number or similar). However, it is very difficult to measure refill through 
consumer-owned products. Combining both targets will lead to a  huge margin of error and unreliable 
data.  
✓ challenge for market operators to guarantee that the reuse/refill targets are not being reached with 
single-use packaging, in the case it is provided on the side (even if not for free). This could risk erroneous 
inclusion towards the reuse/refill targets, which will make the data even less reliable.

For the standardisation of reusable packaging 
The proposed PPWR barely refers to standardisation when announcing that the European Commission will ask 
the European standardisation organisations to come forward with harmonised standards for reusable packaging. 
There are basic standards for reusable packaging at EU (EN 13429:2004) and international level (ISO 18603:

competent authorities t o develop mandatory standards on the shapes and forms of reusable conta iners is 
nothing but a mammoth task. Rather, the European standardisation bodies should first focus on elaborating 
standards on the characteristics needed to deploy well-functioning reuse systems. It is important that 
standardisation bodies do not start their work from scratch, but consider the current EU standard as well as the 
stakeholder dialogues on the topic that are taking place at international and national level (e.g. PR3 Standardslxxxi).

To recognise and reinforce the EPR missions within a strong regulatory framework 
Based on the polluter pays principle, Extended Producer Responsibility makes producers and industrials 
responsible, including financially, for the end -of-life of the products they put on the market. The first EPR 
schemes were created to meet this obligation. EPR has several advantages in the specific management of plastic 
products. On the one hand, it encourages companies to enter a circular economy logic in the design of their 
products thanks to an eco -contribution that can be modulated according to bon uses/malus, thus encouraging 
the use of fair packaging and recyclable materials. On the other hand, based on a partnership between 
companies and local autho rities under the aegis of the State, it allows the sustainable financing of the public 
service of waste management, while reinforcing its efficiency. Finally, it can encourage citizens to sort their waste 
and to turn to more responsible consumption patterns. Each actor of the value chain is thus invested in the good 
circularity of products and the reduction of their pollution.  
Minderoo agrees with the European Commission's definition of producers, which means that brands and 
distributors are no lon ger solely responsible but also producers lxxxiv, which is one the recommendation of 
Minderoolxxxv to make the upstrea m part of the value chain responsible  by contributing to the  Producers 
Responsibility Organisations  (PROs). The European Commission will nevertheless have to ensure that this 
paradigm shift is harmonized at the European level and is stable from a legal point of view, in particular by allowing 
better traceability of imports and exports within and outside the internal market.

The deposit refund system, a complementary tool to accelerate the deployment of reuse systems 
As underlined by the OECD in its last circular economy report, deposit refund systems have proven effective in 
increasing collection rates and reducing littering. Countries are increasingly interested in implementing Deposit 
Refund System (DRS) for certain products, combined with ot her Extended Producer Responsi bility policy 
instruments covering broader waste streamsxcii. 
 
According to Minderoo, DRS is the reference scenario to reach the 90% recycling target for plastic bottles and 
cans.  DRS is indeed the reference scenario to reach th e 90% collection for recycling target for plastic bottles 
and cans. DRS systems are a very  effective way to implement an EPR policy and can lead to synergies, as they 
can improve the quality and quantity of recycling, enable reuse systems and incentivise e co-design. DRS also 
helps to address littering and influence consumer behaviour, which is difficult to address with other mandatory 
EPR policy instruments. 
 
✓ In Australia and US , in coastal debris surveys in the US and Australia the proportion of beverage 
containers littered on the coasts was found 40% lower in states with a DRS legislation for these 
containers than in states without a DRS xciii. 
✓ In Estonia, after the introduction of a DRS for beverage containers, the share of beverage containers 
amongst littered items along roadsides dropped from 80% to below 10%xciv. 
✓ In Germany, the share of beverage containers amongst total litter dropped from 20% (in 1998) to 
almost 
zero
  two years after the introduction of a DRS on one-way beverage containers in 2005. 
 
With this perspective, minimum requirements on DRS within PPWR should help to achieve the targets on 
recycling, reuse or recycled content by material or type of packaging.  
 
Regarding the scope of DRS for single use packaging, we recommend to the co-legislators to add the single-use 
beverage containers with a capacity of up to three litres and the single-use beverage cups with a capacity up to 
50 centilitres in order to simplify the system for consumers and increase the separate collection figures of single 
use beverage packaging, especially for the on-the-go consumption.  
 
DRS can also be helpful for enabling reuse of packaging by giving consumers an incentive to return products, 
thus facilitating the necessary physical movement between consumers and producers. In addition, the return of 
products needs to be made easy and possible by adequate and accessible logistics for consumers. We therefore 
call on the  European co-legislators to include measures to encourage the increase of systems to enable re-use 
according to Article 45 such as the use of a deposit return system for packaging mandated by Article 44 of the 
proposal (which is currently only for single-use plastic beverage bottles with the capacity of up to three litres and 
single-use metal and aluminium beverage containers with a capacity of up to three litres). In this perspective, all 
DRS systems should be designed to accept both reusable and single-use packaging to improve convenience for 
consumers, reduce the need for additional reverse vending machines, and reduce costs. Infrastructure could be 
given a reasonable period of time to adapt existing DRS to this scenario.  
 
Furthermore, as we propose for the EPR, we recommend including a measure asking to DRS for single use 
packaging to dedicate a mi nimum of the ir budget to finance reduction and prevention actions, and reuse 
infrastructure for the deployment of systems for reuse.

 
Position Paper on PPWR Commission Proposal  Pro Mehrweg e.V. 
 
Position Paper of Pro Mehrweg on the Commission proposal for a 
new Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) 
Pro Mehrweg welcomes the EU Commission's proposal for a new and comprehensive legal 
framework for packaging and, in particular, its conversion from a Directive into a Regulation 
that applies equally in all Member States. Furthermore, Pro Mehrweg expressly welcomes the 
fact that for the first time, concrete policies to address waste prevention and reuse have been 
proposed, in line with the EU waste hierarchy.  We strongly support the intention of 
strengthening the circular economy by setting clear rules for the implementation and 
expansion of reusable packaging systems throughout the EU.  
However, the proposal currently lacks ambition, particularly with regard to the level of reuse 
targets. In part, the proposal does not provide the necessary regulatory clarity, e.g. regarding 
important cornerstones that can ensure the expansion of truly effective reuse systems. 
Most importantly, many of the provisions contained in the current proposal are, fully or in 
part, inappropriate and not applicable to the traditional German reuse system for beverage 
packaging. These rules thus have the potential to destroy the world's largest existing 
reusable packaging system, by rendering its continuation economically, ecologically and/or 
practically impossible. 
Our main points and concerns are as follows, and will be explained in further detail below: 
• Reuse targets lack ambition 
• Rigid organisational structure would destroy traditional reuse systems 
• Mandatory serial labelling is superfluous and endangers established reuse systems 
• Strict regulatory separation of reuse and refill is necessary 
• Take-back of reusable packaging must be regulated and incentivised 
• Number of minimum rotations must be defined 
• Reusable packaging must be exempt from recycled content obligations 
• Empty space requirement must not apply to reusable packaging 
• Too many regulatory details are outsourced to delegated or implementing acts  
• Decision to no longer charge VAT on deposits is welcomed 
• Proposal contains terminological inaccuracies and imprecisions 
Pro Mehrweg e.V. (Association Pro Reuse ) consists of and represents  associations and 
companies of the German beverage industry, beverage wholesalers and retailers, their 
supplier industries, as well as environmental associations and individuals. The association 
acts as a platform for all those who contribute to the preservation and continuous 
development of the unparalleled German reuse system for beverage packaging. Our 100 
members advocate the use of reusable packaging wherever it makes ecological and 
economic sense. Pro Mehrweg believes that legislators at the national and EU level have a 
duty to take appropriate measures to establish and protect reuse systems in the beverage 
industry in order to prevent waste, preserve finite resources and protect the climate.

Reuse targets lack ambition  
Pro Mehrweg considered the ambitious reuse targets in the originally leaked version of the 
proposal to be very positive. It is highly regrettable that these targets were considerably 
weakened before the publication of the final Commission proposal – e.g. by 50 percent for 
most beverage packaging ad dressed. We therefore call on the EU institutions to raise the 
ambition of long-term reuse targets in the PPWR to allow for large-scale implementation of 
environmentally-friendly reusable packaging systems . This is not only necessary from an 
environmental point of view, but also realistic an d economically viable: The German market 
share of reusable beer bottles, for example, has been stable at around 80 percent for decades, 
well above the currently proposed long-term targets. 
At the very least, it must be ensured that Member States are enabled go beyond the 
ambition of PPWR targets. The latter could be achieved by explicitly allowing Member States 
to set more ambitious targets through an opening clause in Article 26.  This is particularly 
essential in the case of Germany, where a reuse target of 70 percent for beverage packagin g 
has been in force since 2019.  A weakening of this target, and thus a step backwards for 
Germany, must not be the result of efforts to harmonise EU waste policy.  
Pro Mehrweg furthermore regards it as crucial that the legal basis of the Regulation is 
extended on Article 191 (protection of the environment), instead of only referring to Article 
114 TFEU  (internal market). Both articles should be considered of equal importance, thus 
allowing Member States to go beyond the measures set out in the Regulation. 
In addition, we would like to point out that the German retail sector also puts large quantities 
of take-away ready-prepared food on the market and that Article 26 (3) should therefore not 
solely apply to HORECA companies, but to the entire retail sector. 
 
Rigid organisational structure would destroy traditional reuse systems 
Articles 23 and 24 set mandatory requirements for reusable packaging and reuse systems. 
These requirements are  further specified in Annex VI.  However, the mere differentiation 
between "closed-loop" and "open-loop" systems does not do justice to the common practice 
of reus e systems , which distinguishes between open and closed pool systems as well as 
individual systems and "packaging-as-a-service" models. 
Furthermore, Pro Mehrweg considers the mandatory requirements provided in Annex VI to be 
counterproductive and inappropriate with regard to existing reuse systems, particularly in the 
beer/mixed beer beverage and non-alcoholic soft drink sectors in Germany. Reuse systems in 
these sectors are open to all market participants and have no central management structure 
in place. Their performance is excellent, with a high market share and exemplary return rates.  
The German reuse system for beverage bottles  and its sub -systems, in their complex and 
unique structure, ha ve developed over 120 years based on commercial practice s and 
agreements rather than regulation. Pro Mehrweg deems it unrealistic that the rigid obligations 
regarding e.g. centralised organisation, governance and reporting proposed in Annex VI (A)

can be implemented in these exemplary systems. On the contrary, such rules would jeopardize 
and destroy these systems.  Yet again, that cannot be the aim of the harmonisation of EU 
regulations. Pro Mehrweg therefore considers it imperative to exempt existing and well -
performing reuse systems, like the traditional German reu se systems for beverage 
packaging, from the provisions set out in Annex VI.  Alternatively, Annex VI must at least be 
formulated in a way to allow for all, and to ensure not to unreasonably discriminate against 
any, forms of reuse systems: closed pool syste ms, open pool systems without a rigid 
management and governance structure, individual systems and packaging -as-a-service 
models. 
 
Mandatory serial labelling is superfluous and endangers established reuse systems 
As Pro Mehrweg understands the provisions set out in Article 11, paragraphs 2 and 4, the 
proposed PPWR intends to make a serial, unique and indelible labelling of every individual 
reusable c ontainer obligatory . We consider this obligation unnecessary and potentially 
harmful, at least for reusable beverage packaging.  
Established reuse systems for beverage packaging  have been managed efficiently and 
effectively for decades. Consumer information as well as the calculation of average rotations 
have been successfully achieved without individual serial labels on bottles or crates. Easily 
removable labels are in fact an important characteristic of reusable pool bottles, which are 
jointly used by several economic operators, as only the label indicates the brand.  
Furthermore, r eusable beverage  bottles are subject to constant cleaning and logistics 
processes. In our view, permanently affixing a label or QR code to such bottles is not technically 
feasible, let alone necessary. A mandatory introduction of serial labelling, even if it should be 
technically possible, would lead to the premature destruction of countless reusable containers 
that could be circulating for many more years . Furthermore, it would significantly increase 
system management costs without providing any significant advantages. This in turn only leads 
to a further disadvantage of reusable systems compared to single -use packaging , and a 
potential failure for existing reuse systems to stay in operation. 
We therefore demand that reusable beverage packaging be exempted from the labe lling, 
marking and information requirements of Article 11 (2) and (4).  
 
Strict regulatory separation of reuse and refill is necessary 
Through mandating combined „reuse and refill“ targets, t he current proposal fails to 
appropriately differentiate reusable packaging systems from „bring your own“, i.e. the refilling 
of consumer -owned containers. We consider this dangerous for man y reasons, and it has 
serious implications on e.g. regulatory clarity and feasibility, liability, reporting, and 
enforcement. By allowing for significant gr ey areas, it can even undermine the large -scale 
implementation of true and effective reuse systems. 
Reusable packaging is company-owned packaging that circulates in a reuse system, where it is 
collected, cleaned, refilled, and, according to Article 22 of the regulation,  used again for the

same purpose for which it was conceived. Thus, market operators bear responsibility for the 
reusable containers and the functioning of the system. This is a dramatically different 
operation from „refill“, where , according to Arti cle 3 (28), an end user fills hi s or her own 
container, which in that moment fulfils a packaging function. 
These significant differences make it necessary to strictly sep arate the two approaches in 
regulation, e.g. for the following reasons: 
• A reuse target, to be fulfilled by way of a reuse system, can be addressed to market 
operators such as producers and retailers and thus has a clear norm addressee to fulfil, 
measure and report on the target. Since ”refill“ is an end-user-driven operation, using 
end-user owned containers, it cannot be integrated in such a quantitative target. Such 
a “refill“ target does not have a clear norm addressee, and cannot be sanctioned or 
enforced. Its achievement can certainly not be the responsibility of market operators  
like producers and retailers, who only have very limited power to ensure that it is met. 
• Reuse systems and “refill“ options furthermore would rely on very different calculation 
methodologies. Reuse targets are already in place in some Member States, the highest 
being the one for beverage packaging in Germany, and solid and proven calculation 
methods exist to measure these targets. „Refill“ of consumer-owned packaging, on the 
other hand, can only be estimated. This would lead to inaccurate data and a significant 
margin of error.  
For all these reasons, combined reuse and refill targets can undermine the large -scale 
implementation of true, effective and environmentally-friendly reuse systems for packaging. 
We therefore call for a strict regulatory separation of the concepts “reuse systems” and 
“refill”. Reuse targets should only be achieved through reuse systems , and not through 
“refill“. In our point of view, this is particularly necessary in the case of beverage packaging, 
which is also supported by the fact that the originally leaked Commission proposal envisioned 
reuse systems as the only means to reach reuse targets for beverage packaging. 
 
Take-back of reusable packaging must be regulated and incentivised 
To guarantee the functionality of reuse systems, the new PPWR must contain rules that ensure 
high return rates of reusable packaging. This needs to be achieved through two measures: 
Firstly, through take-back obligations for points of sale in order to avoid island solutions and 
compromised consumer convenien ce. Secondly, through linking reusable p ackaging, by 
definition, to a mandatory incentive that ensures i ts return. Both aspects are missing in the 
current proposal: 
• While the return of reusable packaging is mentioned in Article 44, the proposal lacks 
clear regulations to ensure the latter. That is problematic, since m aking the return of 
containers easy for consumers is one of  the key success factors of reuse systems. 
Therefore, the Regulation must contain a take-back obligation for points of sale: All 
points of sale that put a certain type of reu sable packaging on the market  (e.g. 
reusable beverage bottles), must be obliged to take this kind of reusable packaging

back. A similar regulation is in place for the German DRS, regulated by the German 
Packaging Act, and can serve as an example. 
• Secondly, reusable packaging must be defined in a way in which it is mandatorily linked 
to an incentive that ensures it is returned by end users. Without this, high return rates 
are unlikely and reuse systems will not be able to operate in an efficient manner.  The 
definition laid down in the German Packaging Act , which we expressly support,  
describes reusable packaging as follows: “Packaging that is designed and intended to 
be used multiple times for the same purpose and whose actual  return and reuse is 
facilitated by suitable logistics, and encouraged by an appropriate incentive system, 
usually a deposit .“ It should be avoided at all costs that this strong definition, which 
significantly contributes to the success of German reuse systems, is weakened by 
harmonised EU rules.  
Therefore, Pro Mehrweg strongly recommends to make an incentive for return 
mandatory for reusable packaging, as part of its legal definition. The mere reference 
to an incentive for return, as it is made in Annex VI (A) of the  current proposal, is not 
sufficient. 
 
Number of minimum rotations must be defined 
Sufficient rotations are an important success factor of reusable packaging systems. A 
mandatory number of minimum rotations is therefore another essential element of a sound 
definition of such systems. The current wording of Article 10 b), according to which „as many 
trips or rotations as possible“ should be achieved, lacks precision and causes legal uncertainty. 
We therefore propose to set a minimum of 10-15 rotations for reusable packaging in Article 
10 b). Our reasoning behind this suggestion is as follows: 
• According to a comparison of 32 life cycle assessment studies, the break-even point of 
reusable vs. single-use packaging is usually exceeded after 10-15 rotations.1 
• If market operators have to guarantee this minimum number of rotations, they will be 
incentivized, for economic reasons alone, to set up systems that achieve as many 
rotations as possible. A reusable glass bottle in Germany, for example, completes up to 
50 rotations. 
 
Reusable packaging must be exempt from recycled content obligations 
Article 7 of the proposed Regulation introduces recycled content targets for packaging. In the 
current version, these apply equally to reusable packaging. This does not take into account the 
specifics of reusable packaging: It usually stays in the market for much longer than single-use 
packaging (up to 20 years) and usually requires more material in order to be robust enough to

would endanger reuse systems for the following reasons: 
• A sudden and excessive demand for recyclates will arise, when, in order to fulfil the

the market for the first time  within a short time period. Exorbitant prices for, and

• In addition, with entry-into-force of recycled content targets, significant amounts 
of reusable packaging would have to be taken off the market prematurely , which 
would be counterproductive economically and ecologically. 
Therefore, reusable packaging that is already on the market when the targets in Article 7 
enter into force  needs to be exempt from these provisions. Furthermore, reasonable 
exemptions and/or transition periods should apply to reusable packaging placed on the 
market after entry-into-force of recycled content targets. 
 
Empty space requirement must not apply to reusable packaging 
It is our understanding that the objective of the requirement to limit empty space in packaging 
is to reduce the excessive waste of material often found in single-use applications, for example 
in e-commerce. The requirement is therefore not aimed at, and should not be applied to 
reusable packaging.  
The proposed regulations in Article 21 , stating that grouped or transport packaging  may no 
longer contain empty space that exceeds 40 percent of the total packaging, cannot be met by 
the millions of reusable beverage crates currently in use throughout Europe. The proportion 
of empty space in the respective packaging is generally much higher than 40 percent. Even if 
these reusable crates were redesigned, the proportion of empty space could not be reduced 
to the 40 percent mentioned. Furthermore, in order to reduce and optimize the proportion of 
empty space, the currently used and functional reusable beverage crates - some of which have 
a lifetime of 15 years or more - would have to be replaced suddenly and prematurely. This, 
again, would be counterproductive from an economic and ecological perspective. 
In addition, we would like to mention that it is not  at all foreseeable wh ich effects such a 
measure would have on the stackability and transportability of such crates on pallets, in trucks, 
etc., which are dimensioned and designed according to current customary and well-conceived 
packaging units. 
We therefore call for reusable packaging and, in particular, reusable beverage crates to be 
exempt from the provisions in Article 21. The same should apply to the provisions on sales 
packaging in Article 9, since reusable beverage crates can also be regarded as sales packaging 
within the context of the proposed Regulation.

Too many regulatory details are outsourced to delegated or implementing acts  
Too many relevant regulatory details are to be laid down at a later stage in secondary 
legislation, such as implementing or delegated acts. It is therefore, partly or fully, not possible 
to assess the impact of these regulations. This causes a great deal of uncertainty a mong 
businesses, and will impede or delay important investments. Furthermore, this process is not 
sufficiently transparent and involves too few different stakeholders. The approach of defining 
elementary details in delegated/implementing acts should therefore urgently be reconsidered. 
At the very least, Pro Mehrweg strongly suggests the reuptake of the clause contained in the 
leaked version of the Commission pr oposal, which provided for  the installation of a 
packaging expert committee to enable an institutionalized exchange with the Commission. 
In addition, at least the delegated act laying down the calculation rules and methodology for 
reuse targets should be a dopted significantly earlier than proposed (at the moment by 
December 2028), in order to create investment security for businesses.  
 
Decision to no longer charge VAT on deposits is welcomed 
We expressly welcome the provision in Annex X (m), which states that deposits are exempt 
from sales tax , as this has been a demand of Pro Mehrweg for decades . The current 
requirement to charge VAT on the deposit amounts of beverage packaging between different 
retail levels, but to charge a net deposit amount that includes VAT to the end user, regularly 
causes confusion and leads to a considerable administrative burden without any recognizable 
benefits. 
 
Proposal contains terminological inaccuracies and imprecisions 
The draft Regulation contains inaccuracies in the definition and use of certain terms. In some 
cases, terms are used inconsistently and are therefore misleading. This includes  the terms 
"manufacturer" and "producer". Conceptual inconsistencies also arise, for example, from the 
unclear differentiation between the terms “reuse” and “reusable”. 
Furthermore, the German translation of the proposal completely lacks the term “Mehrweg” 
or “Mehrwegverpackung” (=reusable packaging), which is clear, distinct, well established and 
widely used in Germany, not least in the legal definition of reusable packaging in Section 3 (3) 
of the Packaging Act. The same applies to the term “Umlauf” (=rotation), widely established 
in German usage, which is replaced by the hitherto unknown term “Kreislaufdurchgang”. 
We strongly suggest that the proposal is revised with the aim to provide terminological 
precision, and that well -established and distinct terminology is  used in the German 
translation.

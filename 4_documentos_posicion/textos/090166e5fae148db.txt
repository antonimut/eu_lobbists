EXPRA POSITION PAPER ON PPWR 
The Extended Producer Responsibility Alliance (EXPRA), representing 32 non-profit national EPR  
organizations across Europe and beyond, supports the Commission’s proposal to review the 
current packaging and packaging waste directive (PPWD). We believe that it is of crucial 
importance to set up a forward-looking and ambitious regulatory framework for packaging waste 
management enabling national and local entities to implement effective and efficient packaging 
and packaging waste management systems. 
The adoption of realistic and holistic policies is necessary to ensure packaging functionality is 
safeguarded whilst contributing to reducing the climate and environmental impact of packaging. 
Not all circular policies have an equally positive effect on the climate , and a scientifically robust 
approach based on life-cycle assessments should be favored. Regulatory predictability is key to 
ensuring investment security, especially in waste management infrastructures. 
Therefore, we would like to offer a series of recommendations to enhance the proposal, based on 
the extensive expertise of our members in EPR, waste management, and data collection 
throughout Europe. 
Key Takeaways 
• The definition of the obliged part of the packaging value chain should be 
clarified to avoid fundamental changes in the structure and governance of all EPR 
systems. 
• Targets on recycled content , reuse and refillable solutions should be set at a 
national level (like the current recycling targets) instead of at a company or unit level.  
• Separate collection of packaging waste at source should be the prefer red 
solution to be accompanied by other systems, such as DRS, allowing Member 
States to decide regarding the collection of packaging waste, taking into 
account local conditions, existing investments, and accomplishments. 
• In relation to recyclability criteria, a traffic-light system tied to the costs of recycling 
could be introduced to simplify the eco modulation of EPR fees. It is important to 
carefully define recyclability at scale to avoid hindering innovative packaging and 
recycling solutions.  
• The specificity of industrial and commercial packaging should be recognized, as 
the proposed Regulation has been drafted considering mainly household packaging. 
This also concerns the development of Design for Recycling criteria, the ‘recycled at 
scale’ definition and the use of recyclates.  
• A level playing field must be ensured  tackling non-compliance of EPR 
obligations especially  on online sales  and online market places  using existing 
legislation in some EU Member States. 
• In light of the large amount of secondary legislation planned, connect the deadlines 
with the publication of the related secondary legislation while an EU technical 
committee on packaging and packaging waste must be established.

Our estimated analysis1 showed that EXPRA members’ recycling practices save the CO² 
equivalent of an annual car trip from Paris to Amsterdam per capita. Efficient waste management 
systems thus make a key contribution to limit ing climate warming, which could be drastically 
improved by implementing best practices across all EU Member States. Producer Responsibility 
Organizations (PROs) are currently the only actors in a neutral position to enable, facilitate, and 
assess full circularity. Where PROs have a say in the design of the waste management systems, 
they are key in help ing industry to improve packaging sustainability, establish state of the art 
treatment infrastructures, collect data, and ensure public awareness on the benefits of recycling 
of consumers.  Therefore, EPR  plays an essential role in ensuring that the circular economy 
measures have a positive impact on the climate under the right enabling conditions, which should 
be strengthened by any new legislation. 
1. Clarify the definition of the company obliged under EPR in compliance with WFD 
According to Art. 40 of the PPWR proposal, “producers of packaging” shall fall under EPR for the 
packaging they place on the market , which can be understood as only those companies 
manufacturing the packaging. Up until now, in the Waste Framework Directive and the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Directive, a Member State had a margin of maneuver concerning which 
actor along the packaging value chain is to be made responsible  for the fulfillment of the EPR 
obligations. In this framework,  most Member States mandated the value chain part filling the 
packaging with goods/products. We believe that ensuring a reference to Article 3, paragraph 10 
of the PPWR proposal is important for more clarity, especially regarding prioritization with regards 
to responsibility. Therefore, the definition of producer should be clarified and made coherent with 
the current legislative framework to avoid governance and structural changes in over 20 Member 
States. 
2. National targets vs individual targets (by company or by unit) 
In the PPWR proposal, several new targets and obligations have been introduced (like minimum 
targets for recycled content  (RC), re-use and refill) and, imposed on companies or packaging 
units. We believe that such individual targets would lead to unfair competition between companies 
and  other sectors which were already using recyclates, leading to very high prices for recyclates.  
As there is already a functioning precedent in the SUPD, where the plastic part of packaging must 
contain a minimum RC target of 30% in PET bottles (calculated as an average for all PET bottles 
placed on the market on a Member State), we think the same calculation method should apply to 
the new RC targets proposed in the PPWR to avoid double reporting and compliance. National 
targets should also apply to re-use targets, as these place SMEs  under pressure, due to the large 
investments required to run both single use and reusable systems. 
Overall, national targets will minimize competition distortion between large and small companies. 
This would also apply to the upcoming competition between mechanical and chemical recycling 
processes, safeguarding the related R&D long term investment plans in order to ensure wide

accessibility and availability of recyclates (Article 7.1 and 7.2 on m inimum RC in plastic

We believe that setting these targets at national level (like the packaging waste recycling targets) 
would be better suited to have a centralized solution. PROs could play an important role, given 
their experience and knowledge of the packaging market. Competing private parallel solutions 
will have a negative environmental impact eliminating the potential  benefits of reuse and refill  
systems. 
3. Separate collection of packaging waste and mandatory DRS  
Mandatory DRS for recycling is included in the proposal but not with the same obligations, targets 
or performance standards as imposed on EPR systems. It should be recognized that DRS is just 
another capture tool, as much as separate collection  systems at source. It must remain up to 
Member States to decide on the implementation of EPR or DRS systems to reach the separate 
collection and recycling targets .  Mandatory DRS systems  should be under strict scrutiny and 
subject to the same performance  targets (including recycling)  as EPR  systems. Further 
requirements should be set to strengthen the transparency for all stakeholders, including costs, 
unredeemed fees, etc. 
Bearing in mind the social, cultural, economic and local specificities of Member State s, EXPRA 
strongly supports a technological neutral approach for the achievement of packaging circularity 
objectives. EU Member States have built different models of packaging waste management  
considering their own specificities. For instance, the Italian model, represented by CONAI, and 
the Belgian one, represented by Fost Plus and Valipac, have both achieved high collection and 
recycling rates despite having very different systems in place. Both systems have been 
appropriately tailored according to regional specificities, consumer needs and the number of 
inhabitants. This means that, if a scheme is applied to different contexts than the one it was 
created for, it would not deliver the same results. Therefore, we call on the European Commission 
to acknowledge that a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach is n ot feasible , but  would hamper the 
effectiveness and reliability of already existing and successful national systems. 
Finally, the new legislation should be future proof and allow room for innovation and new  
technologies. With regards to DRS, many start-ups have developed different digital DRS and 
reward systems which are currently being tested and rolled out in several Member States , like 
Belgium and Austria. Additionally, industry has developed rewarding solutions motivating people 
to recycle in a similar way to DRS systems. 
4. Recyclability 
Until now, o nly one third  of M ember States have introduced eco -modulation of EPR fees 
according to WFD requirements (Article 8a). Nowadays, it is still not clear how to organize fee 
modulation within a system of competing PROs , especially considering  that the respective 
guidelines foreseen in the WFD have not yet been adopted . Basically, packaging with a lower 
waste management net cost due to better recyclability is already being eco-modulated.  
In the Regulation proposal, there is a five-step differentiation for a group of over 30 packaging 
materials/categories, resulting in a drastic increase of administrati ve burden, reporting , and

control, while the impact of each grade on costs and whether there is a clear difference regarding 
recyclability is not clear . It is important to highlight that  EPR fees are usually defined by the  
(activity-based) costs that a specific packaging incur s for its respective collection, sorting and 
recycling and not based on RC as the amount of RC does not mean less waste management 
costs. We therefore suggest a simpler traffic -light system tied to the costs of recycling , 
differentiating between levels of recyclable packaging  tied to existing eco-modulation schemes. 
In such a system, there would be a red, orange, and green category of recyclability, which can be 
used to justify a differentiation of EPR fees. 
Regarding recyclability at scale, we are also concerned that it will hamper i nnovation and future 
investments as it requi res that the same recycling solution is available for 75% of the EU 
population. It is important that packaging for which an innovative recycling solution in one or 
several Member States is offered, will be consid ered as ‘recyclable at scale’. These innovative 
solutions are the result of years of research and large investments that should be taken into 
consideration. If this will not be the case, investments in new technologies will not be done in a 
Member State as it will not be clear whether other Member States will make the same investment 
to make it “recyclable at scale”. The diversity of recycling solutions in Europe should  be 
considered. The current proposal implies  that economic operators would indirectly become  
responsible for the development of infrastructures, which is not sensible. 
The recyclability at scale definition sets out that the collection, sorting and recycling of waste must 
cover 75% of the EU population, but the specific guidelines to assess this will consider the amount 
of packaging placed on the market in the EU as a whole and also in each Member state. It is not 
clear as to how this approach will work in practice and it should be clarified ( art 6.6). 
The approach through Implementing/Delegated acts to assess the recyclability of packaging and 
to develop design for recycling guidelines, which will happen at a much later stage, will also lead 
to legal uncertainty, resulting in a negative investment environment. This is also a key problem for 
commercial and industrial  packaging. If the Commission wants to sit on the chairs of the 
producers, it has to assure that a legal framework? (i.e. secondary legislation) is published at 
least two year s before the implementation date, and renewed every year following the 
implementation date.  
In addition, i t is important to highlight that recyclability should take into account a more holistic 
approach and consider open and closed loop  recycling options as to guarantee a better CO2 
performance, a higher yield, and less generated waste, which is usually the case with open-loop 
recycling. 
Finally, we believe that when there are sufficient recycling solutions for a packaging type, there 
should be no mandatory requirement for such packaging  tobe only compostable .  This would 
apply to e.g. the mandatory industrial composability of all beverage system capsules in 20262.As 
long as a recycling solution is available, organic recycling should not be imposed.

5. Commercial and Industrial Packaging 
The proposed Regulation has been drafted with the context of household packaging in mind, 
neglecting to fully incorporate the specificities related to commercial and industrial (C&I) 
packaging. However, C&I packaging is fundamentally different from household packaging as 
regards e.g. the collection, sorting, and recycling systems in place. Companies need a tailor-made 
collection service carried out by private waste management companies. Although the amount of 
single use  C&I packaging is almost equal to household packaging, its composition is 
fundamentally different, with carboard and wood a s dominant materials. This implies that C&I 
packaging is less complex than household packaging and  almost all of it can be reused or 
recycled . 
 
Many provisions of the Regulation are simply not applicable to C&I packaging, i.e., recycling at 
scale, waste prevention targets, reuse targets, Design4Recycling, etc. We strongly request that 
the distinction between these two types of packaging is taken i nto account when setting the 
targets and to specify whether the text applies to C&I packaging or not. We believe that the 
specifics of C&I packaging should be recognized regarding the development of Design4Recycling 
criteria, the definition of “recycled at  scale” (as collection of C&I packaging occurs usually at 
individual companies), and the use of recyclates (the obligation to incorporate RC will lead to 
thicker packaging).  
6. Minimum EPR requirements and compliance of online sales/ 
marketplaces  
We believe that the current proposal does not sufficiently  prevent further freeriding of EPR 
obligations and fees on online sales. It is of key importance to ensure that all sellers or companies, 
especially from outside the EU, comply with EPR requirements . We suggest t wo measures to 
tackle this issue. 
We propose to add rules for online sellers so that they are only allowed to sell to an EU consumer 
if they can prove that th e seller  is registered in the country of purchase or if the marketplace 
fulfills the EPR obligations on behalf of this (non-EU) client. The existing legislation adopted in 
France3 and Germany4 could be used as a model. Since 2022, if a company sells its products in 
Germany and/ or France, operators of marketplaces must ensure that the company is fully 
complying with the EPR  obligations in these countries. Otherwise, the marketplace has to fulfill 
the EPR obligations on behalf of the online seller.

Second, there is a need to strengthen PROs to ensure that a level playing field is set up and can 
be enforced  by the authorities . To achieve this, only reliable PROs should be accredited by 
Member States. An additional minimum requirement should be added to Article 43(3) on Return 
and Collection Systems”, requiring the transparent publication of the collected, sorted and 
recycled packaging waste so it is shown where and how the material is finally used for . 
Furthermore, there should be an equal level of enforcement in each Member State. Enforcement 
authorities should be obligated to exchange their experiences, substantiated objectively with data. 
7. Connecting deadlines and Implementing/Delegated acts and 
Involvement of industry in technical work 
Several deadlines and obligations in the proposal are based on delegated acts that the 
Commission will have to develop after the approval of the PPWR. However, it is crucial for industry 
to know the content of these delegated acts to be able to fulfill their obligations within the deadline. 
If the timing is too short between the publi cation of the delegated act and the deadline, it will be 
very difficult to comply and legal uncertainty arises. Therefore, we are proposing to connect the 
targets with the publication of the respective secondary legislation. 
Our experience in running a global platform for EPR systems  has been that sharing best practices 
and expertise from different perspectives are a significant added value and enabler to the 
evolution of a successful circular economy. Considering the significant amount of Implementing 
and Delegated acts that will be discussed in the coming years, we suggest the establishment of 
an EU-level Technical Committee, managed by the Commission, composed of representatives 
from national authorities as well as the whole packaging value chain  including PROs and EPR 
systems. The expertise of such a committee would be of immeasurable value for the large amount 
of highly technical secondary legislation that the PPWR envisages.

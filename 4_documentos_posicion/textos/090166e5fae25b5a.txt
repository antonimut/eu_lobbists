Personal Care Products Council Input to the EU’s  
Public Consultation on the Proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation  
On behalf of the Personal Care Products Council (PCPC),1 we are pleased to provide our input to 
the Public Consultation on the Proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation 
(PPWR).  
As noted by the European Commission2, the European Union and the United States have the 
largest bilateral trade and investment relationship, and our economies are the most integrated, of 
any in the world. U.S. cosmetic and personal care products manufacturers have invested heavily 
in manufacturing and other operations in the EU and U.S.-EU trade in cosmetic and personal 
care products exceeded $10.5 billion in 2019, contributing to the strength of industries on both 
sides of the Atlantic. 
PCPC and our members share the Commission’s goals of preserving the environment and 
reversing climate change. We support the objectives and vision of the European Green Deal, as 
well as those of the PPWR, to reduce packaging waste and ensure that packaging is reusable or 
recyclable. 
It is in this context, and the recognition of the potentially significant impact of the PPWR on U.S. 
exports of cosmetics and personal care products to the EU, that we offer the following comments 
and suggestions. PCPC also works closely with our sister industry association Cosmetic Europe 
(CE) and would like to express our strong support for CE’s submission to the Commission.  
Ensure Harmonized Packaging and Labeling Requirements Across All EU Member States 
We welcome the proposal for a PPWR with a full internal market legal basis, as it is an 
important step towards achieving greater harmonization of packaging issues across Member 
States. However, we remain concerned that some provisions in the proposed PPWR would allow 
Member States to introduce specific and divergent national requirements, thus preventing the 
proper functioning of the EU Single Market. 
For example, divergent labeling requirements can become a trade barrier, cause consumer 
confusion, increase material needs for additional labeling, and increase waste due to larger than

Maintain Inclusion of Cosmetics Within Scope of “Contact Sensitive Packaging” 
The EU Cosmetic Products Regulation requires that cosmetics and personal care products uphold 
the highest level of protection for consumer health and safety. Manufacturers must use very 
high-quality secondary packaging materials to assure consumer protection. The PPRW’s 
inclusion of cosmetics and personal care products under the definition of contact sensitive 
packaging is therefore critical.  
Recognize Alternative Recycling Methods and Materials   
The present limited availability of high-quality secondary raw materials globally will undermine 
industry’s ability to meet PPWR’s proposed targets. Recognition of alternative recycling 
methods and materials would support increased commercial investments and alleviate supply 
chain shortages so that high-quality secondary raw materials are available in sufficient quantities 
and at a reasonable cost. We urge the Commission to consider the following: 
• Recognize advanced/chemical recycling within the scope of recycling in the PPWR. 
Chemical recycling broadens the scope of resin that can be utilized, especially for those 
packages that cannot be mechanically recycled in current infrastructure.  
• Recognize post-industrial recycled (PIR) and pre-consumer plastics as recycled content. 
• Recognize bio-based biodegradable plastics as equivalent to recycled plastics. This would 
have the added advantage of reducing dependency on virgin fossil-based plastics. 
Adjust Target Calculation Approach for Recycled Content in Plastic Packaging  
PCR packaging has its limitations and cannot always be used for certain types of plastic 
packaging due to various regulatory and performance-related criteria. For example, 
polypropylene (PP), polyethylene (PE) or polyethylene terephthalate (PET) may not be suitable 
for all parts of contact sensitive packaging. To address this issue, our industry supports setting 
PCR targets as an average of all plastic packaging placed on the market by a producer, rather 
than per unit of packaging.  
This approach would give producers the flexibility to allocate recycled content based on 
available supply and technical feasibility and provide a means of achieving the targets 
established in the PPWR. 
Take into Consideration Small Packaging Recycling Requirements in DfR 
While small cosmetic packaging can be designed to be recycled, recycling facilities are not 
always equipped to treat these packages. We urge the Commission to take account of the specific 
attributes related to small packaging and recycling facilities in the Design for Recycling (DfR) 
criteria. Guidelines should be reviewed regularly to take account of technological advances. Our

Allow the Use of Digital Labeling  
The use of digital solutions can offer an efficient and effective way to provide consumers with 
detailed information about a product without compromising its sustainability. This is particularly 
relevant for cosmetics and personal care products with significant on-pack space limitations, but 
which nevertheless must comply with extensive labeling requirements under the EU Cosmetic 
Product Regulation. 
Digital labeling also supports the movement of goods across borders, enabling companies to 
meet labeling requirements with fewer delays and costs.  
For the reasons stated above, PCPC requests that packaging-related information is allowed to be 
conveyed by digital means to every extent possible.  
Avoid Forcing Uniform Packaging   
While we appreciate the need to limit excess packaging, we are concerned that overly restrictive 
requirements in this regard could result in forcing all companies to use uniform packages. 
Effectively requiring unique EU packaging would raise costs for exporters and discourage US-
EU trade. Moreover, requirements for uniform packaging would limit companies’ abilities to 
distinguish their products, thereby undermining their competitiveness. Packaging design is a 
critical component in building a brand’s image and identity with consumers. Therefore, 
packaging minimization criteria should be aligned with the PPWR’s definition of packaging, 
which recognizes the function of the packaging as the presentation of the product.  
Allow Sufficient Transition Periods   
Any significant revision of a regulatory framework, such as envisioned with the PPWR, will 
require industry and authorities to make substantial changes to policies and procedures as well as 
budgetary provisions. Reasonable timeframes for the implementation of PPWR requirements 
must be established to ensure business continuity. Specific recommendations are outlined below:   
      Labeling Requirements  
Manufacturers of packaged products must be allowed at least 36 months after publication of 
implementing acts to adjust their product labels and manufacturing processes. In addition, 
complete stock disposal and exhaustion of existing packaging that was manufactured or 
imported should be allowed to prevent unnecessary waste.  
Packaging Minimization  
The proposed 12-month period after EIF does not provide sufficient time for manufacturers 
to take the necessary steps to prepare. This will include time to develop design alternatives, 
adjust manufacturing lines accordingly, and phase out existing packaging. PCPC urges the

Conclusion 
Once again, we appreciate this opportunity to share PCPC’s support for the overall goals of the 
PPWR and our suggestions that we believe would enhance industry’s compliance with this 
important measure. We note that for the vision and goals of PPWR to be realized, and for 
industry to achieve the proposed targets, considerable EU investment in infrastructure may be 
necessary. This will include, for example, investments in collection, sorting, and recycling 
systems throughout the EU, as well as encouraging consistency in systems and practices. We 
look forward to future Commission proposals in this regard.

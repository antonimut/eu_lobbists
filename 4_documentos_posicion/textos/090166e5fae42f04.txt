Introduction 
The juices and gazpachos sector shares the Commission's ambition to continue moving 
towards packaging sustainability, innovating in eco -design to improve its recyclability, 
promoting the prevention of packaging  waste, and increasing the content of recycled 
material, thus favoring the market for secondary raw materials.  
The sector is committed to the concept of "circular packaging" especially for beverage 
packaging, which meant to be a circular, sustainable, and waste-preventing alternative 
to traditional throwaway models, and that could even be a better and wider concept than 
only “reuse”. 
Therefore, before imposing obligations that are extremely restrictive  and technically 
unfeasible in some sectors and cases, it is necessary to carry out a thorough evaluation 
that allows to deeply analyse the feasibility and benefits of the measures  to determine 
which is the best option in each situation, from an environmental, technical, economic 
and social perspective, w ithout jeopardizing food security and giving companies 
sufficient time to adapt to possible changes that entail relevant impacts on production 
processes and high investments. It is important to provide a margin of flexibility to 
companies to be able to com ply with the objectives, as well as try to minimize 
bureaucratic burdens.  
This regulation should also ensure the integrity and proper functioning of the single 
market, avoiding the proliferation of national or regional legislation that entails legal 
uncertainty for businesses. 
Zumos y Gazpachos comments on key matters

We consider it is essential during this process to insist on the fact that reuse should only 
be considered an option when life-cycle analysis prove that it is the most beneficial from 
the environment  point of view  and cost -efficient. Before proposing targets, it is very 
important to analyse the specificities of the different categories: for example, their volume 
of consumption, characteristics of the distribution channel that can make the reverse 
logistics process very complicated, location of the factories, size of the companies or the

Thus, the different beverage sectors are at very different starting points. The volume of 
the juice category is lower to make reuse viable, and its indirect distribution channel 
formed by numerous intermediaries between the supplier and the end customer makes 
the reverse logistics process particularly complicated. Furthermore, the reconditioning of 
the juice bottles poses additional problems due to the density and organic content of the 
product. Due to the viscosity and pulp content of the fruit, the residues deposited on the 
walls and bottom of the container tend to solidify over time due to  moisture loss, a 
phenomenon that would be aggravated in the hottest months  and with long times and 
distances related with reverse logistics, which would imply the need for many washings 
with the consequent expense of water, energy and chemical cleaning and disinfection 
products, and could also significantly increase the risk of contamination by foreign bodies 
due to the loss of asepsis. Indeed, the juice sector is very similar to the milk and milk 
products sector and the exemption from the re -use targets to milk is also essential for 
the juice sector. 
Moreover, the economic investments involved are currently unaffordable, without 
forgetting that the reuse lines require a significant amount of space for the additional 
stages of the process, which many companies do not currently have because of the way 
they have been designed. 
Due to the location of the factories, the distribution of products in reusable packaging 
throughout the territory would mean that the empty packaging would have to travel long 
distances, so the environmental impacts generated by the return logistics would be quite 
high. 
We also believe it is necessary to include the concept of “circular packaging” (single-use 
or reuse) as a circular, sustainable and waste -preventing alternative to traditional 
throwaway models. Circular packaging is that which is collected separately at a high rate, 
refilled or recycled multiple times for the same use; so when recycled, it should contain 
a high percentage of recycled material originating from packaging, thereby contributing 
to material resource efficiency and litter prevention.  Instead of focusing only on re -use 
as the unique alternative, circular packaging is a wider concept that includes single-use 
and refillable/returnable packaging as valid solutions in terms of waste prevention and 
environmental impact minimization.  
Single-use circular packaging is an option technically, economically and environmentally 
useful, effective and feasible (even more than reusable packaging for long supply and

impact of packaging and to prevent packaging waste generation.  
Recycled content (art. 7)  
To meet their recycled content obligations laid down, the producers need to have access 
to a sufficient amount of affordable, high-quality recycled food-grade plastic.  
Recycled PET is highly demanded by other sectors to incorporate it into lower quality 
applications, such as textiles or tires, where it will generally no longer be recyclable. To 
avoid this downcycling, producers – and any other sector that must be subject to 
mandatory minimum recycled content targets – should have priority access to a fair share 
of the recycled materials they put on the market.  
On the other hand, the targets lay down in Article 7 apply for the plastic part of the 
packaging, and after clarification by CE, any part of plastic packaging, no matter how 
much there is of it, will  need to comply with these targets. However, so far, PET is 
practically the only recycled plastic generally authorized for use in food contact material. 
It is expected that EFSA will be authorizing new processes for other kind of plastics, but 
there is no timeline so, currently, the only real market for recycled plastic suitable for 
contact with food is PET. This provision “for the plastic part in packaging” should only 
apply when EFSA not only assessed the processes of recycling for other plastics as safe, 
but authorized it, and these plastics will be available on the market.  
We believe that t argets could be  set per economic operator as an average of their 
product portfolio placed on the market, in a manner consistent with the SUP Directive, 
rather than per unit of packaging.  
Unlock (early authorisation of) new and innovative recycling technologies such as 
chemical recycling, in order to have food-grade recycled material available. For this, we 
want to promote this more quickly, taking into account that th e creation of "fuel" would 
not be counted as recycled, as established by Spanish legislation, and support the 
balance of masses and the credit method.  
All operators of the food and beverage sector with mandatory recycled plastic targets 
should be granted priority access to recycled material from packaging placed on the 
market (Right-of-first Refusal), thus avoiding downcycling.  
Plastic parts in non -plastic dominated packaging, like paper and metal, should be 
excluded from the recycled plastics targets.  
Restrictions on packaging (Art. 22, Annex V) 
Article 22 establishes restrictions on the use of certain packaging formats (annex V), for 
example, single use grouped packaging such as collation films or shrink wraps. 
It is important to note the need to have material with adequate mechanical strength and 
elastic limit to perform in applications that require stretching of the material. It is therefore 
 
 
4 
necessary to ensure the stability and protection required, both in its distribution process, 
handling during trade as well as by the final consumer. 
Companies cannot afford to change plastic by other materials to group packaging 
completely because other options imply drastic changes in packaging lines, sometimes 
not affordable economically nor viable due to the lack  of space or the characteristics of 
the material. Moreover, the weight of other materials could be much greater and would 
affect the ergonomics of load handling because plastic is waterproof and not permeable 
to liquids, gases and vapours protecting the products during transport. 
Measures in this case should focus on incorporating recycled plastic, plastic of biological 
origin or renewable raw materials to the grouped packaging (the threshold fixed at the 
present technically possible). This would be more consistent with measures already 
taken by some national authorities and would reduce the placing on the market of virgin 
material.

I. Introduction 
The juice sector is closely linked to the fresh fruit sector and in particular to stone 
fruit, citrus and pome fruit, it generates some 4,500 direct jobs and 10,000 indirect 
ones. The juice industry has an important backbone role in the fruit and vegetable sector 
and contributes to its proper functioning, transforming approximately 20%  of the 
production of fruit and vegetables at the national level, which is key when it comes to 
managing the flows and decongest the fresh market. From the primary sector, volumes 
of fruit unsuitable for marketing fresh are transferred to the industry for its transformation 
into juice, thus avoiding food waste and the cost of its management as waste. 
Changes such as those proposed through this proposal in matters such as reuse would 
seriously endanger the survival of a large part of the companies, especiall y affecting 
small and medium-sized companies. This would cause serious damage to the interests 
of producers of citrus, peaches, grapes and other fruit and vegetable sectors, since a 
possible decrease in juice production would also affect this highly import ant producing 
sector, both due to its contribution to GDP and employment, as for being a brake on the 
rural exodus and the emptied Spain. It should be remembered that many of these 
companies are located next to rural areas in the East and Northwest of Spain. 
The juices are aqueous solutions, with a high fermentable sugar content apart from other 
nutritional components (vitamins and minerals, polyphenols and carotenoids) extracted 
directly from the fruit. There are numerous scientific studies that support th e health 
benefits of consuming juices, precisely because of this high nutritional density 1 and it is 
recommended by the health and health authorities of various countries as well as in the 
5-a-day schemes 2. However, these same characteristics favor the m ultiplication of 
altering microorganisms that produce the fermentation of the sugars and consequently 
the degradation of the organoleptic characteristics of the juice, for which reason a heat 
treatment is necessary to ensure the stability of the product. I n addition, oxygen, heat 
and light are factors that limit the shelf life of the juice. For this reason, the main objective 
of the packaging is to protect the juice from these microbiological alterations, as well as 
to avoid the deterioration of its nutriti onal and organoleptic properties during the shelf 
life. The packaging material must have an effective barrier against oxygen, light, the loss 
of aromas and the entry of microorganisms. The containers used in the juice sector are 
mainly the cardboard beverage packaging (brik) followed by PET and glass bottle. 
II. Comments from the fruit juice sector about reuse 
based on scientific evidence 
Zumos y Gazpachos believes  that waste policies should be oriented towards the 
promotion of packaging with less environmental impact, regardless of whether it 
is single-use or reusable packaging, so the promotion of reusable packaging should 
 
3 
not become a goal per se. We believe that it is necessary to take into account the results 
of the life cycle analysis (LCA), so that any single-use container with an environmental 
impact equivalent to that of a reusable container has the same treatment in the legislation 
and its use is not penalized just because it is a single-use container. 
LCA studies that compare single-use containers with reusable containers do not provide 
decisive data that justifies that reusable juice packaging have a lower environmental 
impact compared to single-use one. For example, in a 3meta-analysis of various life cycle 
assessments of beverage containe rs, including juices, the authors found better 
environmental performance of beverage cartons compared to alternative packaging 
types (single use multilayer PET bottle, single use glass bottle and reusable glass bottle). 
Another life cycle study carried out  by the Heidelberg Institute for Energy and 
Environmental Research (IFEU), meeting the requirements of the Federal Environment 
Agency (UBA) published in Germany (2020), concluded that the beverage 4cardboard 
scores significantly better in the “weather pro tection” category than reusable glass 
bottles. According to this study, a 1L reusable glass bottle emits 45% more CO 2 during 
its life cycle than a car dboard packaging of the same size, which is the majority single -
use container in the juice industry. In the study, the researchers compared the respective 
packaging throughout the entire "life cycle", from raw material extraction to packaging 
manufacturing, from filling to recycling, including all transport processes. On the other 
hand, another meta-analysis study carried out by the ICTA 5(Institute of Environmental 
Science and Technology), based on a matrix of technical criteria, and with a Spanish 
data source, concludes that monolayer plastic bottles with 100% rPET and rPET and 
PET (50% and 50%) give the best results in carbon footprint, material efficiency and 
recycled origin of the material compared to other containers, including glass bottles, 
which also present the worst results for the footprint carbon. 
It is necessary to indicate that the result of LCA are very susceptible to various 
parameters, depending on the products or the scope of application itself (temporary and 
geographical scope, transport distances, end -of-life scenarios, and many more)  or the 
choice of methodology. Therefore, while the aforem entioned results allow for the 
interpretation that, on average, the global warming potential is higher for glass bottles, 
including reusable ones, this does not automatically translate to be true in all cases.  
Thus, a Reloop 6study, which summarizes data from 32 analyzes carried out between 
2001 and 2020, where it is recognized that the results cannot be extrapolated, since 
these studies are generic and do not take into account primary data of the processes 
involved with the exception of some cases7. The LCA reviewed in this study found that 
reusable glass bottles have lower emissions than single -use bottles made of glass, 
polyethylene terephthalate (PET), or aluminum, when reused for a certain number of 
cycles (which varies depending on the material). Regarding beverage cartons or bag-in-
box containers compared to reusable containers, the latter had fewer emissions when

the transport distance was extremely reduced (in this case less than 100 km). However, 
the national territory and the juice sector a re not considered, that is, the collection, 
classification and end -of-life systems (recycling, incineration, landfill) and the 
particularities of the juice sector have not been considered. Of the 32 studies, only 7 
report data related to the beverage indus try including mineral waters, wine and soft 
drinks, but in no case the juice industry. These studies consider the particularities of the

Canada, with important differences with res pect to Spain. Other fundamental 
differences not considered are the usual type of transport (by road in the case of 
Spain), the low availability of water resources and the deficient state in the 
generation of energy typical of Spain . 
Given that LCA do not  yield conclusive results regarding the greater environmental 
goodness of reusable packaging, the innovation policies developed so far have 
supported circular and biobased materials, and the  industry together with the 
Administration, has oriented its efforts in this direction. In the CIDEC report on the rPET 
market and packaging circularity 8, various projects are cited and are focused on the 
development of biobased and recyclable materials, the creation of more efficient 
recycling processes and the monitor ing and removal of hazardous substances and 
pollutants from recycled plastics, the use of by -products and their up-cycling aimed at 
developing compostable packaging, financed under European programs such as FP7, 
H2020 and LIFE. This is proof of how Europe has opted for recyclability and the 
renewable origin of raw materials for packaging. In fact, we are now in the phase in 
which these projects and initiatives bear fruit and reach their industrial and 
applicability phase.  
It should also be noted that reusable beverage packaging have suffered a decrease 
in number in Europe in recent years , as reflected in this study 9published on the 
Reloop platform website , with data from 34 countries, which also includes non -EU 
countries (Canada). For various reasons, di stributors are increasingly choosing to sell 
their products in single -use packaging. Although some countries have implemented 
measures such as taxes on single -use packaging to try to maintain the level of use of 
reusable packaging, the results have not bee n satisfactory. Germany, which has 
traditionally been a country with reuse, also shows a downward trend in reusable 
beverage packaging, although it maintains the highest levels in all of Europe. For 
Germany, the data provided by this study, broken down by beverage sector, includes 
beer, water and soft drinks. Of the 34 countries included in this study, there is only one, 
Croatia, where there is a higher percentage of beverages in the categories of beer, soft 
drinks and water in refillable glass or PET containers compared to single-use containers. 
Thus, the reality of the market, sectoral quality standards, consumer demands or logistics 
characteristics have made the sector adapt its models, both in commercial distribution

III. The juice sector in the different distribution 
channels  
When considering reuse targets, it is essential to know in depth how the juice sector 
operates in the different distribution channels and the differences that exist with respect 
to other beverage sectors  (water, soft drinks and beer)  where reuse is already 
implemented in HORECA. 
For example, compared to other sectors with a greater presence of a specific brand with 
a certain volume in different economic operators, in the case of juice, several juice 
suppliers tend to supply the same operator with a certain variation over time. For 
this reason, completing replacement cycles for the same manufacturer efficiently would 
be complicated because the time elapsed from when they are distributed until they are 
withdrawn from the establishment, which would be very high and would also affect t he 
availability of bottles by the manufacturer to continue manufacturing. Also, the volumei 
managed by the juice sector is much lower than other types of beverages, which 
makes the implementation of the system very difficult due to the lack of volume. 
On the other hand, it is a sector with a very high variety of packaging formats, which 
makes ma nagement very difficult.  The data provided by the scrap ECOEMBES of 
declared packaging (in % by weight and according to material, year 2019) by companies 
in the juice sector are the following: beverage cardboard  45.9%, glass ( single-use) 
33.5%, plastic 18.8%, and metal 1%. Another characteristic is that our sector operates 
with many references and flavors with small volumes instead of large volumes with 
few references. For example, orange juice, pineapple juice, apple juice, tomato juice, 
must, peach nectar, orange nectar, pineapple nectar, multifruit, etc.  
Directly, this diversity affects the reverse logistics that reuse requires . The sector

Aspects related to reverse logistics 
The reverse logistics process becomes especially complicated in a sector with an indirect 
distribution channel made up of numerous intermediaries between the supplier and the 
end customer, which makes it extremely difficult to recover the packaging and has its 
consequences in terms of sustainability and carbon footprint. 
According to our research, reverse logistics would increase the number of routes by more 
than 15% compared to the current system. In this data, only the use of full trucks for the 
collection of reusable packaging has been estimated, so that the cost per pallet is the 
minimum possible, as well as gathering all the containers of a province to complete the 
shipments and the costs of potential storage of these containers have been ignored and 
their transfer to provincial centers for their return to the factory. 
All the life cycle analyzes developed in the Reloop 2 study for reusable packaging agree 
that the transport phase is the one with the greatest impact for the category of 
greenhouse gases. On the other hand, the greater weight of reusable glass has a direct 
impact on its transport, since it implies a greater consumption of fuel and, therefore, an 
increase in the emissions of polluting gases into the atmosphere. A half-liter glass bottle 
weighs about 400 grams, while a plastic, aluminum or cardboard packaging of the same 
capacity does not exceed 20-30 grams. In addition, the very characteristics of the glass 
container prevent the optimization of its transport, unlike other packaging such as the 
brik, which are transported in rolls that allow a more efficient use of space and requires 
less energy.

long time, due to the low volume of the sector, the collection of empty packaging 
would not take place with a fully loaded truck with the consequent increase in 
carbon footprint and therefore, increasing impact on the emission of greenhouse 
gases. According to the study developed by Forbes Insight 10 “The empty space 
economy” each year at least 122 million tons of CO2 are emitted by sending 
empty space in the elements of transport.

Aspects related to economic sustainability.  
From a manufacturer's point of view , the change from single  use to reusable 
packaging would imply heavy investments. On the one hand, in terms of infrastructure, 
it would mean the complete change of production lines due to the need to install washing 
machines for the reconditioning of reusable glass containers, as well as purification 
 
8 
plants for the treatment of washing water. The required investment would not only affect 
the new equipment but also the need to expand the facilities for the space needed by 
the reconditioning and washing water treatment plant. In parallel, the costs of energy, 
water, and chemical products necessary for both washing and label removal would 
increase. 
It should also be underlined that the reuse lines require significant space due to the 
additional phases of the process that would imply th at the companies in the sector did 
not currently have the necessary space for a possible implementation. 
The process of washing or reconditioning the juice bottle differs from that of other 
types of beverages depending on the fiber, sugars, and other speci fic elements 
16that could require more intensive treatment. The impact caused by this phase is not 
negligible, especially considering different indicators of the carbon footprint such as 
terrestrial and aquatic ecotoxicity and the consumption of resources (7,11). In this sense, 
the study carried out by Gallego -Schmid et al 7 concludes that at least 208 reuse cycles 
would be necessary to equalize the impact of single-use packaging in terms of resource 
consumption, which is unfeasible for a glass bottle due to wear and tear from use and 
washing. In terms of terrestrial toxicity and the required washing conditions, the reusable 
alternative would never improve the performance of the single-use alternative. 
On the other hand, as pointed out in the comparative study of Reloop2, it is necessary 
to develop detailed cost analysis for the transition from the single -use to the 
reusable packaging system in a competitive manner . Of the 32 studies selected for 
the comparison, only 5 presented cost analysis and identified the initial investment as 
the major limiting barrier for the implementation of the reusable packaging system. These 
5 studies focus on reusable transport packaging whose reconditioning systems differ 
significantly from those of primary packaging. They also point out that the potential long-
term savings that reuse could generate are unknown. Therefore, more in-depth financial 
studies are necessary to guarantee the economic viability of the transition pr oject from 
single-use packaging to a reuse system. In a recent publication that summarizes the 
trends and status of the sustainability of the packaging reuse system, 17the limitation of 
data referring to cost studies available in the scientific literature is also pointed out. The 
financial study should delve into the repercussion of the energy cost and the uncertainty 
in the transition towards a decarbonized model in which the current system finds itself. 
Since the process of reconditioning the juice packaging would be energy intensive, 
sustainable energy alternatives should be considered in its implementation. The Deloitte 
study "A sustainable energy model for Spain in 2050" 18 points out that in this transition, 
the significant volume of investments to be m ade, the time needed for its amortisation 
and the uncertainty about when certain technologies will be sufficiently mature in terms 
of benefits and costs for its massive deployment , jeopardize the economic efficiency of 
the transition. The study concludes that there are significant uncertainties in the transition 
 
9 
to a decarbonized model, which is why solid and flexible policies are needed, such as 
the one demanded by the juice sector. 
Aspects related to environmental sustainability.  
Spain is the most arid country in Europe and in fact desertification is a real problem. 
Drought is a normal and recurring phenomenon in our climate , and therefore the 
actions to be taken in these situations must be based on planning, through "risk 
management", and not on emergency measures such as "crisis response". The drought 
produced in most of Spain between 1991 and 1995, with very negative socio economic 
consequences, acted as a trigger for this change of mentality when managing droughts. 
Hence, Law 10/2001, of July 5, of the National Hydrological Plan, laid the foundations, 
in its article 27, of this management plan of droughts. These Drought Management Plans 
are regularly reviewed 19. 
However, far from helping, reuse would lead to a significant increase in water 
consumption by bottling companies, either from the municipal connection or from wells 
owned by the companies, necessary in the washing p rocess. In parallel, the 
consumption of chemical products  (and the consequent generation of waste) and 
energy would increase. 
In addition, the reconditioning of juice bottles poses additional problems than those of 
other beverages whose density is lower and their organic content too, requiring a more 
intense washing process and similar to that of reconditioning ready -to-eat meal 
packaging. In this sense, the study carried out by Gallego -Schmid et al 11 reports that 
at least 208 reuse cycles would be necess ary to equalize the impact of single -use 
packaging in terms of resource consumption. In terms of terrestrial toxicity and the 
required washing conditions, the reusable alternative would never outperform the single-
use alternative. Depending on the indicators, the main lo ads of the reconditioning 
process are associated with electricity consumption, heating of the washing water, and 
water consumption. The following graph, extracted from the referenced public source 
Gallego - Schmid et al., illustrates the number of rotations of reusable packaging in which 
the environmental impacts are equalized compared to those of single-use packaging for 
different impact factors in plastic packaging of takeaway.

As can be seen in the graph, the determinant factors of the environmental goodness of

In relation to washing, in another study 20, the impacts of reusable trays and single -use

had the lowest environmental impact in all categories, with the exception of water 
consumption. The problem of higher water consumption for reusable packaging 
compared to single-use ones is one that is frequently raised when comparing the impacts 
of these systems. 
It is also necessary  to consider that the reconditioning plant generates residual water 
and solid waste made up of discarded caps, labels and damaged bottles, as well as 
sludge from the process. This increase in the volume of waste would force a review of 
its management. For example, to avoid increasing the landfill fraction, recovery systems 
should be assessed.

possible reusable packaging for sensitive beverages  like juices, it is reusable glass. 
Other possibilities are being studied, such as bottles of  PET 22, which until now in its 
reusable form have been used for water and carbonated drinks, but this is still not a 
commercial reality in juices and dairy products . The growing demand of returnable glass 
units might lead to a significant increase in raw material demand. In this case, it is also 
critical that it is a secondary raw material, the cullet, which is obtained from the separate 
collection of glass bottles. According to FEVE (European Federation of Glass Container 
Manufacturers), Spanish production of g lass containers represents 9.49% of glass 
container production in Europe23 (last data available from 2014) and has recovery quotas 
of 72% 24 (last data available from 2017), which are among the lowest in neighboring 
countries, which implies the need to import 14% of raw materials, as reported by the 
Spanish Association of Glass Container Manufacturers (Anfevi, 2015)25. In some sectors, 
such as glass containers, the per centage of cullet, depending on the color, can reach 
very high values, with the average being around 50%. The use of cullet in the glass 
industry, especially in some types of glass, is limited by external factors. As pointed out 
by Anfevi, more efficient selective collection programs would be necessary together with 
treatment plants to adapt the quality of the cullet to the production processes, to 
guarantee a higher use of it in melting26. The point is that the use of cullet (recycled glass) 
to manufacture new containers prevents from the use of raw materials such as sand, 
soda, or limestone, which means a reduction in CO2 emissions. The use of this recycled 
material to manufacture new packaging saves 53% of CO2 equivalent emissions 
compared to the use of r aw materials. This is because recycled glass melts at a lower 
temperature than sand, soda and lime would need in the kiln. In addition, emissions 
associated with extraction and transportation that would be necessary to extract raw 
materials from nature are  avoided. Recycled glass keeps its properties stable and, in 
addition, saves 38% of energy throughout the process. This is due to the fact that there 
is no extraction of raw materials, their impact on transport is lower and the reduction of 
the necessary temperature in glass furnaces. When we use recycled glass we avoid soil 
erosion and the extraction of sand from nature. And this is key to reducing the 
overexploitation of resources. For each ton of recycled glass, a saving of 1,200 kilos of 
raw materials and 130 kilos of fuel is generated. 
 
Therefore, a massive change to reusable glass containers would require a period of 
adaptation by the glass industry and improvements in the collection program s, in order 
to respond to the sharp increase in demand for cullet as a secondary raw material. These 
changes would require manufacturers to adapt not only to the increase in the number of 
packaging, but to the type of format, forcing these industries to make heavy investments 
(ovens, machinery, etc.) difficult to quantify now and which would be based on the 
volume of glass packaging demanded, the design of the container, etc. These changes 
 
13 
would require a period of adaptation that could lead to supply problems in the short and 
medium term. 
 
On the other hand, the global energy crisis is fully affecting the Spanish market. The 
uncertainty of the market does not make it advisable to invest in highly energy -
demanding industrial systems, such as the reconditioning of the juice container and the 
fuel consumption associated with the increase in land transport. 
IV. Failure to implement of reusable packaging 
Within this Association we can report the case of one of our companies, which undertook 
the implementation of the reuse mode l for the HORECA channel in the 90s, for what 
were then its business lines: beer, soft drinks and juices. The total investment costs 
were approximately 100  million pesetas (former Spanish currency ) which included 
washing machine, unpacker and return packer and technical installations (water, 
electricity, adapted heads,... ). The highest cost came from the washing machine, which 
also occupied around 100 square meters , that is, a very significant amoun t of floor 
space. However, for juices this model had to be discarded for various reasons. In 
HORECA, empty juice bottles represented a real hygiene problem,  which was tried to 
solve by increasing the number of visits on routes to collect boxes with empty b ottles 
dirty with the remaining of product that have been inside.  As pointed out in this report, 
the product is more difficult to clean, which meant more costs for water, electricity and 
chemicals. It went from 3 to 5 wash ings for food safety, so the bottles wore out, and 
with this, in the subsequent reuses, the vacuum was lost, since the glass thread did 
not seal well with the stopper, so it turned out in food safety problems. To alleviate this 
problem, the weight of the glass had to be increased, and consequently, the weight 
of the plastic boxes used as transport packaging. Finally, the volume of consumption 
was not sufficient to the amortization the washing machine. 
In contrast, the reuse model turned out to be viable for beer and soft drinks. In th ese 
cases, the bottles were easier to clean and the costs related to  (electricity, water, 
chemical products) were much lower. In addition, pest problems were not generated in 
the HORECA establishments and the volume of consumption made it possible to assume 
the amortization and the expenses associated with washing. 
V. Conclusions  
From this analysis that we present, it can be concluded that the implementation of a 
reuse system in the juice sector would be practically an unfeasible challenge. In recent 
years, the sector has worked on improving its processes, so that they are increasi ngly 
sustainable and circular. Projects and investments to reduce waste in general stand out. 
In the case of organic by -products, their up-cycling has been studied and put into 
 
14 
practice. In the case of packaging, the sector has worked intensely to continue improving 
ecodesign on a voluntary basis. For example, by reducing  the weight of the 
packaging, by incorporating recycled material and improving its recyclability. We 
have also worked against the clock to comply with the legal requirements established by 
the SUP Directive, such as the replacement of the plastic straw attached to our 
containers by paper straw and caps attached to their containers. These processes 
have meant a notable economic investment for our companies, which have had to work 
to adapt their machinery, carry out tests with alternatives and acquire new materials that 
also have a significantly higher cost. 
It is important to point out that the different beverage sectors are at different 
starting points in order to face the fulfillment of the proposed reuse objectives . In 
some sectors, which also have had ministerial orders for many years, either because of 
the volume of product managed in the HORECA channel or because of the 
characteristics of the product contained or the geographical location of the factories, the 
reuse of containers is broadly implanted. While, in sectors such as juice, reuse would 
mean a total change in the business model that has not incorporated reusable packaging 
up to now, due to the technical complexity derived from th e contained product (high 
density and organic content) and the low volumes sold compared to other categories of 
drinks. These lower volumes together with the high number of SMEs in the sector, really 
complicate the situation. The proposal of Regulation  includes objectives and 
measures that could compromise the viability of many companies in the sector. A 
sector that in recent years has suffered from a drastic reduction in consumption 
and the effects of the pandemic.  
In short, setting reuse targets in the juice sector would imply: 
• Strong investments in new filling systems, container washing equipment, 
improvements in the purification of industrial water or expansion of space to 
manage the entire recovery process. 
• The redesign of supply and return logistics that is currently done through 
wholesalers or intermediaries, due to the low volume of product consumed 
compared to other beverage categories, which would generate environmental 
impacts that need to be analysed. 
• In most cases, manufacturers are in rural areas, where the raw material used as 
the basis for the manufacture of each product is harvested. As these are sectors 
with such a specific location, the distribution of products in reusable containers 
would imply that the empty container would have to travel long distances, so the 
environmental impacts generated by return logistics would be very high. 
The report made shows that: 
 
15 
• Life cycle analysis do not show that reusable packaging is the best option from 
an environmental point of view. 
• The reuse model in the juice sector has been scarcely implemented except for 
local producers and with a predominant type of juice (Germany and apple juice). 
However, for other types of drinks it has been working for many years now. 
• There are several drawbacks: 
o Reverse logistics: Insufficient volume and periodic variation of suppliers 
that hinders the loads and time management for the disposal of bottles by 
the different manufacturers. 
o Reconditioning of the container: Consumption of water, chemical 
products and associated energy cost. Facilities for the treatment of waste 
waters and increase in the management of sludge. 
o Costs of the n ew infrastructure: packing area, unpacking area, washing 
and filling line. 
From our Association, we believe that it is essential that uncertainty is not generated 
and that product or packaging bans are avoided without technical or 
environmental justification that prevent companies from continuing to work on eco -
design and improving recyclability through their already planned investments, as well as 
good management of the resources.  
We do not consider that the establishment of mandatory targets for certain sectors 
is the best way to achieve an increase in reuse rates, since it is a discriminatory 
measure that also creates competitive disadvantages for sectors or companies (mainly 
the smallest ones). 
It is important to bear in mind that reuse is one more option that should not be 
assumed without a rigorous technical analysis that includes the entire life cycle 
of the container. In fact, reuse is not always the preferable option to recycling from an 
environmental point of view, so it would be convenient to carry out a case -by-case 
analysis to determine which option is more beneficial from an environmental, technical 
and economic/social point of view. before imposing measures. 
For all these reasons, we understand the promotion of reuse through incentive 
measures that support and facilitate its implementation and application based on 
technical feasibility studies instead of through unaffordable objectives, according to the 
demand of society and the limitations logistics.

Position Paper on the Proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Regulation 
 
AIJN – the association representing the European fruit juice industry – fully supports the EU’s objective 
and ambitions to address the challenges regarding packaging and packaging waste. Further, the sector 
believes that the PPWR can play a decisive role in driving greater circularity of its packaging. 
The future enforcement of the revised PPWR will undoubtedly have an eno rmous impact on the entire 
fruit juice value chain. AIJN, its member associations and companies operating in the fruit juice s and 
nectars industry would  thus like to harmoniously draw attention to the  fact that the Commission’s 
Proposal has not recognised fruit juices and nectars as sensitive products . Indeed, due to this  
microbiological sensitivity, these products require very specific packaging formats and packing 
processes, which explains why certain mandatory requirements will increase the sector’s risks of food 
loss, food safety and increase the potential negative impacts on the environment.  
 
1. Mandatory re-use targets are not a feasible solution for microbiologically sensitive products 
Concerns from the sector arise not from the level or the existence of the proposed re -use targets, but 
from the very nature of fruit juices and nectars. Indeed, the role of their packaging is crucial to present an 
effective barrier against oxygen, light, loss of aromas, and entry of microorganisms. Unlike soft drinks 
and natural mineral waters, with which fruit juices and nectars currently find themselves with in the “non-
alcoholic beverages” category, fruit juices and nectars are more microbiologically sensitive and easily 
fermentable.1 They require specific sterilisation/pasteurisation processes to ensure a longer shelf life . 
Regarding the storage period, packaging plays an especially crucial role because producers are not 
allowed to add any preservat ives to fruit juices and nectars .2 In consequenc e, in inappropriate 
packaging, there would inevitably be more spoilage, food waste, and risk to consumer safety. 3 These 
requirements apply to all microbiologically sensitive products, of which liquid milk is an example of, and 
they are already exempted from the present targets. 
This explains why the sector’s current packaging mix is made up of, on a European level : 60% aseptic 
cartons, 31% plastic, 8% glass, and 1% other (such as aseptic pouches and cans),4 similar to the liquid 
milk’s packaging mix.5 Thus, an objectively low proportion of reusable packaging is motivated by 
the specific requirements of final products, and logistics which make re-use systems difficult to 
put in place.

Indeed, because of the products’ characteristics, there are serious limitations for setting up re -use 
systems and in the choices for reusable packaging. The only returnable packaging, technically feasible 
for fruit juices and nectars today, is returnable glass. Therefore, complying with the proposed targets 
of achieving 10% and then 25% reusable packaging use, means companies need to move 
exclusively to glass packaging. This lack of choice is especially difficult because it entails investments 
solely in glass packaging and glass bottling lines, along with washing stations (in-house or outsourced).  
 
Furthermore, unlike other non-alcoholic and non-sensitive beverages, the fruit juice industry does 
not have the option to use reusable PET bottles. Reusable PET bottles, as technology is today, cannot 
withstand the necessary heat treatments to pasteurise fruit juices and nectars: the bottles present cracks 
after they are washed and refilled, which let microorganisms enter and thus spoil the product. 
Notwithstanding the trials industry has conducted for the past 15 years, in Germany for instance, currently 
this is still the case.  
 
3. Lack of environmental benefits for mandatory re-use requirements  
Following a thorough analysis of the European Commission’s Proposal, Annex and Impact Assessment, 
there is no evident environmental study on the consequences of installing reusable packaging and re -
use lines. There is also no proof of the undeniable benefits that re -use would bring when compared to 
other packaging solutions, and methods of dealing with packaging waste.  
Cleaning reusable packaging from fruit juice is not comparable with the non-alcoholic beverages they are 
categorised with. The process automatically demands more washing cycles, water, and cleaning agents 
as fruit juice and nectar residues - solidified pulp due to a loss of moisture - cling to the bottles’ interior. 
Reconditioning the packaging to be effectively re -used will automatically increase the industry’s water 
footprint and chemicals use. Added to this are higher CO2 emission levels due to the transport of reusable 
glass packaging across Europe.6 Together, a mandatory shift to reusable packaging, which for the 
sector means only glass, will unavoidably increase negative effects on the environment and go 
against the fruit juice sector’s ongoing commitments to reaching net-zero emissions by 2050. 
 
4. Increase packaging circularity with complementary re-use and recycling measures 
 
Re-use where it makes sense:  The European fruit juices and nectars industry welcomes the needed 
changes and ambitions from this Proposal, but calls on legislators to make them feasible. Re-use is one 
of the many directions to deliver on its objectives, but only if it is a complementary measure to other 
solutions, such as recycling. A top-down, one-size-fits-all approach for re-use fails to take into account 
Member State specificities as well as past investments fr om industry, both in collection and recycling.  
This is all the more important as the sector is small compared to other non-alcoholic beverage industries,

priority access for the beverage industry would support high -quality recycling, avoid downcycling and 
encourage different sectors to invest in their own circularity.

and nectars as microbiologically sensitive products and, for the aforementioned reasons, 
regulate them as such. 
The PPWR Pro posal has the potential to bring about much  needed changes, however, current and 
additional enablers need to support Member States, industry and citizens alike. Future investments in 
ever-increasing circular packaging and systems need to be feasible to avoid significant economic value 
destruction, and they need to be  implemented when and where they make the most sense,  to avoid 
compromises to food safety or increased environmental impacts.

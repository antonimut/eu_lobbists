Consultation response | Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation Health Care Without Harm (HCWH) Europe welcomes the European Commission's proposed Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). We cannot afford more delays and need strong and binding legislation on packaging and packaging waste that prioritises prevention and reuse. The targets must be ambitious and the legislation must be enacted without delay during the current parliamentary term.  It is crucial that the PPWR addresses packaging waste in the healthcare sector. Nationally, healthcare waste represents approximately 4% of total generated waste and plastic waste audits indicate that nearly half of municipal waste from healthcare is disposable packaging. Considering the scale of packaging waste in the sector , the PPWR must set specific targets for preventing, reusing, and recycling healthcare packaging waste while implementing measures to improve waste management.  HCWH Europe is a network of thousands of hospitals, healthcare leaders, and healthcare professionals. We have conducted plastic waste audits in hospitals across Europe and found that nearly half of plastic waste lacks labelling information on the material or plastic type (page 20). This makes recycling difficult or even impossible.  Our members are concerned about the amount of packaging, overpackaging, unnecessary packaging, and overuse of plastic in healthcare. Waste represents a large financial and environmental cost for hospitals. Reducing packaging waste while preserving the quality of the product is therefore very important - not just for the healthcare sector, because this would also enhance public well-being by reducing the associated negative health and environmental impacts. Considering the significant amount of waste generated by the healthcare sector, it is crucial that the PPWR introduces measures to reduce packaging waste in healthcare, helping to achieve overall waste reduction in the EU.  While the PPWR proposal is promising, it contains several loopholes and exemptions that could limit its effectiveness in protecting human health and the environment. HCWH Europe is particularly concerned that the proposal does not adequately address the issue of harmful chemicals in packaging that pose a significant health risk. We ask the European Commission to implement the following five recommendations to ensure that the PPWR will make a real difference.

Recommendations for an impactful PPWR Increase reduction targets for packaging waste Packaging waste reduction targets must be significantly more ambitious to achieve the necessary reductions in waste. HCWH Europe proposes a 15% waste prevention target per capita by 2030 instead of the proposed 5%, with sub-targets for different materials, e.g. plastic, paper, glass, metal packaging.  These prevention targets should apply to the healthcare sector, where significant reductions in unnecessary packaging can be achieved. Excessive packaging is used in the healthcare sector and beyond, and the current proposal limits the potential for preventing packaging waste. Some of our member hospitals have successfully reduced plastic packaging through collaboration and discussions with suppliers, but not all hospitals have the resources to support such sustainability initiatives. The PPWR must therefore ensure that no unnecessary packaging is used in the healthcare sector. Reducing unnecessary single-use items in the healthcare sector can also complement the reduction of unnecessary packaging.  Increase reuse targets and strengthen reuse systems The ‘Systems for re-use’ definition should include an incentive to return packaging, e.g. a deposit scheme. Reusable packaging will not deliver environmental savings without well-designed reuse systems. Incentives should ensure reusable packaging return rates exceed 90%.  The reuse targets proposed in the PPWR, such as transport or beverages, must be significantly increased to achieve meaningful progress. Several Member States already have significant experience with reuse systems for beverages that can be scaled up, and these experiences should be taken into account when setting targets. In addition, reuse targets should be expanded to cover packaging in other sectors, e.g. textiles (such as laundry services), non-perishable foods, cleaning products, personal hygiene products, and milk.  The Yorkshire Ambulance Service NHS Trust (UK) replaced 8,000 plastic milk bottles per year with reusable glass bottles at no extra cost. Similarly, the Centre Hospitalier Angouleme (France) has reduced plastic in their maternity food services by purchasing food items in bulk. Other hospitals have reduced packaging in laundry services by replacing single-use plastic bags with reusable options. There is significant potential to reduce waste with reusable and refillable items such as sachets for personal hygiene products, which are used in large quantities in healthcare.

Eliminate hazardous chemicals in packaging to protect human health The current proposal does not effectively protect human health from harmful chemicals and fails to incentivise a phase out of hazardous substances in packaging. The PPWR must restrict substances of concern and very high concern in all packaging present on the EU market and align with REACH regulation.  Moreover, it is concerning that the current proposal’s recyclability requirements do not allow for the restriction of substances in packaging based on chemical safety. HCWH Europe strongly advocates for adopting a preventive approach in the PPWR, especially considering the delayed Food Contact Material Regulation. The protection of consumers' health should be ensured by implementing ambitious measures that restrict the use of substances of concern in packaging.  The regulation must include strict requirements on chemicals, including mandatory reporting on chemical content and strong mechanisms for phasing out the most harmful substances, such as those proposed in the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability (i.e chemicals that cause cancers, gene mutations, affect the reproductive or the endocrine system, or are persistent and bioaccumulative. This is essential as the proposed regulation emphasises reuse, recycling, and recycled content.  Include medical device packaging in recycled content and recyclability targets Packaging for medical devices should not be exempt from recycled content targets. If these requirements apply to contact-sensitive food contact material, the safety of recycled plastic materials used in other contact-sensitive applications shall be ensured by adequate legislation. Similarly, medical device packaging should not be exempt and should be recyclable. There are already recyclable options available for healthcare packaging on the market and healthcare facilities are increasingly demanding more sustainable options for healthcare packaging, such as the Nordic criteria for sustainable packaging for healthcare products.  Strengthen Extended Producer Responsibility The Extended Producer Responsibility requirements should incentivise more circular products and business models. Current public waste management costs are too high. Reducing waste and management costs can improve the allocations of public financial resources to the healthcare sector. The PPWR should include Extended Producer Responsibility schemes with fees that address unsustainable packaging designs and reward sustainable ones. Producers should fully pay for packaging recovery and treatment, which should be achieved through regulation rather than voluntary schemes. In addition, EPR schemes should dedicate a minimum of 10% of their budget to finance infrastructure for reuse systems.

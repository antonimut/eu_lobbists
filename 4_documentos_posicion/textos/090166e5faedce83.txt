Submission of Energy Drinks Europe (EDE) 
on the proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on packaging and packaging waste, 
amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 and Directive (EU) 2019/904, and repealing Directive 94/62/EC (Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Regulation, PPWR) 
 
KEY SUMMARY 
• Energy Drinks Europe (EDE) is the leading representative trade association of European energy 
drinks producers.  
• The European Energy Drink s industry is a European success story, contributing billions of EUR in 
revenue since the invention of the category in Europe over 30 years ago. 
• Almost all energy drinks are sold in small format aluminium cans, between 250ml to 500ml. This 
is because energy drinks are intended as functional beverages  for specific occasions (like coffee or 
tea) for consumption “on the go”.  
• Due to the damage high pressure washing would do to the lining of aluminium cans, small format 
reusable aluminium cans do not currently exist for the energy drinks industry. 
• Consequently, the European energy drinks sector is uniquely affected by mandatory reuse targets 
considered in the European Commission’s proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Regulation.  
• The proposed reuse obligations would lead to a perverse incentive to shift to larger PET or glass 
reusable containers with lower recyclability characteristic, and a significant negative economic 
impact on a native European industry that has become a global success story worldwide.  
• The PPWR could be amended to provide an exemption for small format  “on the go” containers 
that meet mandatory circular criteria, supporting the environmental objectives of the proposal while 
protecting the complementarity of reuse and recycling  as equal and self -reinforcing policy 
solutions. Similarly, Member States with existing or prospective high quality closed loop circularity 
schemes should be granted the ability to derogate from Article 26 of the proposed PPWR.  
• Alternatively, a sectoral approach for the achievement of reuse targets should be favored, to protect 
the competitive dynamics of the beverages sector as a whole.  
 
EDE members are committed to improving the environmental footprint of their beverage packaging and supporting 
the objective of the European Green Deal. Thus, EDE fully supports the European Commission’s general ambition 
to improve circularity included in the proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (“PPWR”).  
EDE strongly believes that a transition to a circular economy in Europe will not succeed with a one-fits-all approach 
to packaging and packaging waste. Instead, various levers based on a mix of complementary solutions is required. 
In line with public comments made from other sectoral bodies, we believe that recycling and reuse solutions must 
exist together to contribute collectively to the most effective approach to the management of packaging waste  
(and valuable recyclable material) enabled by the PPWR. 
As currently designed in the draft PPWR, mandatory reuse targets would risk this multi -solution approach and 
lead to detrimental environmental, economic, competitive, and social impacts, for the reasons explored below.

FULLY RECYCLABLE SINGLE-USE PACKAGING SUPPORTS PACKAGING CIRCULARITY  
A. Reuse is not environmentally beneficial under all circumstances and brings significant economic 
costs 
Reusable beverage packaging only makes sense under certain conditions and for certain packaging types. From 
an environmental perspective, reuse packaging does not always guarantee better environmental results than 
single use packaging. 1 Many factors enter in the ecological assessment of recyclable and reusable packaging 
such as packaging weight, inclusion of recycled material, transport and distribution wa ys, consumption habits, or 
cleaning process. As for reusable packaging, the general advantage of this system is usually only beneficial when 
the consumption, distribution and washing/refill process take place in the very same limited regional area. EDE 
has concerns that the economic impact assessment of the European Commission has not fully taken these 
considerations into account.  
For example, a report by Price Waterhouse Coopers, commissioned by UNESDA, the  European trade association 
for soft drinks producers, underlines that significant changes would be required compared to the present day with 
respect to:  
• Manufacturing processes; 
• Changes to the set up and operation of bottling lines; 
• Logistics and reverse logistics arrangements; 
• Additional lines and or production facilities. 
Based on these elements, the report goes on to determine that:2 
The shift towards more refillable packaging represents major investments and significant changes 
in the beverage packaging value chain, production, distribution, logistics and retail. In addition, the 
environmental impact of refillable beverage packaging, compared to its single -use circular 
alternative, may only prove to be positive in certain situations. 
The cost of this business model change to the soft drinks sector is estimated to be €18.7bn for a 
market share of 20% reusable PET bottles by 2030 at EU level: €12.5bn in estimated additional 
capital expenditure and €6.2bn in estimated additional operating expenditure. 
EDE notes that the report was commissioned only with a 20% target for recycled PET as the focus of the study, 
and therefore does not consider the economic impacts of reuse on other packaging material types at higher rates. 
For the reasons above, EDE is concerned the impact on can packaging could be significantly higher.  
Moreover, the above-mentioned factors might vary significantly from one country to another, and from one product 
category to another.  EDE is concerned that the current PPWR Impact Assessment might not reflect accurately 
the actual real impact in different Member States and on specific packaging formats. More detailed data is needed 
at national level to ensure that  reuse targets are only introduced where it makes sense  from an environmental 
and economic perspective.

B. Fully circular single-use packaging is a key part of the energy drink sector’s packaging ambition, 
for both environmental and consumer safety reasons 
Energy Drinks are intended mainly for on on -the-go and single-use consumption, in certain occasions, and in 
small formats (250ml to 500ml). The reason for this choice of packaging for energy drinks, is because small format 
aluminum cans are:    
• Indefinitely recyclable with minimal quality loss; 
• Offer high circularity characteristics (96% recycling yields), when properly collected and sorted; 
• Are already highly collected and recycled, 76% of all aluminium beverage cans are recycled in 
Europe;3  
• Are light weight in transportation as well as  for carrying by consumers , allowing significant 
reductions in carbon footprint; 
• Are sanitary, as they provide a total barrier against light and oxygen , and unbreakable, impact 
resistant and puncture resistant, enabling their use as a safe packaging for the di stribution of 
beverages.  
 
EDE is concerned that a regulatory framework that mandates a transition into different formats will not make sense 
for all sectors, in all circumstances. Such a “one size fits all” approach undermines carefully considered 
environmental and product safety considerations. 
C. One size fits all reuse targets undermine the complementarity of reuse and recycling initiatives  
 
In several Member States, collection schemes for beverage containers have been operating successfully for many 
years. Closed-loop systems support the circular economy as they prevent valuable recyclable material ending up 
in the environment as litter, or in the general waste stream. 
The implementation of mandatory DRS in each Member State by 2029, as wished by the European Commission 
(Article 44 of PPWR), seems a natural next step to strengthen existing closed loop beverage collection schemes 
in the European Union. 
However, by imposing mandatory reuse targets applicable for the very same type of beverage packaging already 
captured by DRS, the PPWR will seriously undermine the required DRS set-up and affect existing well-functioning 
schemes. This point has been made specifically by the Deposit Return Scheme Association, representing thirteen 
National Deposit Return Scheme Operators in Europe: 4 
The mandatory reuse targets (article 26) for the same type of beverage packaging will undermine the 
required DRS set-up and put an unreasonable financial burden on the producers as well as affecting 
existing and well -functioning sustainable material loops. Today , refillable and single -use packaging 
typically coexist within the scope of DRS and the flexibility is an essential prerequisite to breweries’ and 
importers’ existence on small and medium sized ma rkets. This flexibility with positive market influence 
would thus be negatively affected by mandatory reuse targets. Finally, the reuse targets may even 
undermine the well-established environmental recycling loop for aluminum cans resulting in distortion 
of competition between different packaging materials. 
Indeed, by creating a perverse incentive for manufacturers to shift out of aluminium and into other less recyclable 
formats, the environmental and economic efficiency of existing high performing schemes is undermined. This will 
nullify the investments made so far and penalize a recycling solution that performs well.

BROAD MANDATORY REUSE TARGETS WILL HAVE UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES 
A. Mandatory r euse quotas applying to each economic operator undermine European 
competitiveness 
As explained earlier there is a significant economic impact associa ted for a transition to reuse at the levels 
foreseen in the Commission’s proposal. 
 
While multinational companies will be able to absorb these costs, for small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs), 
representing 95% of beverage companies in the EU, this transition is far more severe and can be life-threatening 
for SMEs. EDE, who has both large and small companies within its membership, has concerns regarding the 
fairness of this “one size fits all” transition and the impact it could have on the existing competitiveness and mere 
existence of firms within the EU. Moreover, should the proposal proceed in its current form, larger companies may 
look to acquire existing companies that ha ve reuse solutions, to quickly acquire volume that would satisfy their 
legal obligations. This acquisition of beverage manufacturers could lead to a further concentration of beverage 
manufacturers within large multinational companies, resulting in a less competitive European market.  
 
Finally, the perverse incentive to abandon highly recyclable (and valuable) materials and shift into other formats 
would have a downstream impact  across the packaging value chain: Producers of single use packaging that 
cannot be integrated into a reusable system as well as raw material providers , and the collection or waste 
management networks that rely on the sale of collected material to fund their operations coul d be similarly 
impacted. Again, these concerns have not been reflected in the Commission’s economic impact assessment.  
B. Reuse quotas will result in increased use of virgin plastic 
As mentioned above, due to the high-pressure washing requirements for reusable containers (that would destroy 
the required sanitary lining of aluminium cans) , no current reusable solution exists for small format aluminium 
containers that could be deployed by industry at scale.  
 
Therefore, manufacturers currently using aluminum cans will be forced to move the total volume of their operations 
to reusable packaging like reusable PET , leading to an increased demand for plastics. This specifically impacts 
products mostly consumed on-the-go, as these products still have to be convenient for consumers, which would 
not be the case if distributed in heavier formats like glass. Moreover, the demand for virgin plastics increases 
even further as for reusable PET-bottles, more PET is needed for a reuse solution because reusable PET-bottles 
must be thicker and therefore have more weight.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
COMMON SENSE SOLUTIONS TO ENSURE RECYLING AND REUSE COMPLEMENTARITY  
 
For all the above mentioned reasons, EDE recommends  the PPWR to be amended so that the complementarity 
of high quality recycling and reuse is protected . The following proposals aim at ensuring  a maximum flexibility in

packaging choice and  the viability of well performing packaging types and recycling schemes, and make sure

A. Exemption of small format aluminum cans, as fully circular packaging, from reuse targets  
Single-use packaging designed to be circular and used by consumers in on-the-go occasions should be exempted 
from the calculation of reuse targets under Article 26 of the proposed PPWR. 
However, it is important that any single use packaging continues to meet the objectives of the Circular Economy 
and the PPWR as a whole. Therefore, packaging should only be exempted from the calculation of the reuse 
targets where that packaging: 
• Is in a small packaging format intended for on-the-go consumption (i.e. 500ml and under); and 
• Is already subject to a mandatory collection system, such as a deposit return scheme; and 
• Reaches a 90% collection rate, recycled content rate, and recycling rate.  
EDE’s proposed approach is reflected by a best practice example from Austria, who will be introducing a reuse 
scheme in January 2024. Under that scheme, there will be targets on final distributors to offer for sale packaging 
in reusable formats, that differ between product categories. However, waters, juices, and non-alcoholic soft drinks 
that are sold in single-use-containers, but which are  the subject of Austria’s incoming Deposit Return Scheme  
and contain no more than 500ml, are excluded from the calculation of the quota.  
The rational for such an exemption was twofold. First, it was to ensure that smaller containers typically purchased 
for “on the go” consumption could remain an important consumer experience, but one that remains firmly with the 
circular economy. Second, it meets the practical concern that the weight of the packaging plays a significant role 
for consumer s. Experience from other jurisdictions such as Germany shows that reusable packaging is often 
larger and heavier. Without this “on the go” exclusion, there would be a perverse incentive towards less practical 
packaging for this consumption occasion.  
B. Member States possibility to derogate from reuse targets when demonstrating high ly efficient  
DRS in place  
 
As explained above, a one size fits all approach is not appropriate when the diversity of European waste 
management systems for packaging is considered. Therefore, the PPWR should reflect the  efforts and 
investments already made in different Member States to improve beverage packaging circularity.  
Therefore, Member States in which mandatory packaging waste management schemes are operational or 
planned, should be granted with the possibility to derogate from the obligation to fulfill the reuse requirements , 
under specific conditions. 
These could include the demonstration that a high-performance collection and recycling system is in place, and  
that the performance of that scheme would be as beneficial, both from an environmental and economic 
perspective as compliance with the reuse targets in Article 26.  
C. A sectoral approach to the achievement of the targets should be considered or the responsibility 
should reflect existing European reuse schemes 
 
To protect the European industry and in particular SMEs in the entire beverages sector , EDE believes that a

sectoral approach, based on shared collective responsibility, should be taken when it comes to the responsibility 
for the achievement of the reuse measures. This would align with the ap proach taken by the beverages sector 
with respect to commitments or targets regarding health, nutrition, and advertising.  
As explained by the Price Waterhouse Coopers report above, a transition to reuse places significant economic 
pressure on European industry, but this burden would not be felt equally across all companies. Placing the burden 
of mandatory reuse targets on each economic operator does not reflect  the significant diversity of beverage 
operators in Europe (including energy drink producers) in respect to their size, portfolio, use of  materials, or 
logistics or geographical location.  
The investments required to develop  the offer of reusable solutions are extremely  high. On the contrary, a 
collective approach in the achievement of the objectives  would allow all firms to participate in the transition to 
reuse in a fair and balanced way.  
Alternatively, the responsibility could reflect existing scheme s in Europe for reuse, such as that seen in Austria. 
For example, in Austria, the obligation of reuse quotas lies on final distributors in food retail 5. Food retailers are 
obligated to offer a certain quota of the targeted product categories in reusable packaging. This is an “offer” quota, 
not linked to the percentage of products actually sold to consumers. This enables a greater indexation of reusable 
packaging in the consumer environment, but also provides economic operators with legal certainty as ultimately 
only the consumer can determine if a purchase is  made or not . To provide additional options,  the Austrian 
legislative framework also allows final distributors to fulfil their obligation by selling at least 25% of the total volume 
sold in reusable packaging across all beverage categories.  
CONCLUSION 
EDE is of the view that the above solutions would be a welcome common-sense approach to a more practical 
implementation of the reuse targets in the PPWR. Such solutions would go some way to significantly addressing 
the concerns of the diverse and uniquely impacted European Energy Drink s industry, which is one of Europe’s 
home-grown success stories.

European Power Tool Association (EPTA) statement on the European 
Commission’s proposal for a Regulation on packaging and packaging waste 
EPTA, the European Power Tool Association, represents 25 European power tool manufacturers and 
90% of all corded and cordless power tools sold in Europe. Power tools are used by professionals, 
skilled tradesmen and DIY consumers. EPTA members are committed to the highest possible 
standards of quality and safety of their tools. Innovation and advanced technologies as well as 
customer-friendly applications are key to our companies’ economic growth, competitiveness and the 
creation of jobs. More information can be found at http://www.epta.eu/.    
As an industry that is already heavily investing into innovative, high-quality and sustainable packaging 
solutions, we welcome the European Commission’s ambitious push for a more circular and 
sustainable European economy. In this context we also appreciate the approach to harmonise 
European rules for packaging using a Regulation with a single market legal basis  as an instrument. 
The proposal for a Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) can avoid fragmentated 
national efforts, hindering the free movement of goods in the European single market , and align the 
different approaches of countries in a multi-speed Europe. This is especially important as national 
labelling schemes and similar requirements (e.g. on recyclability, re-use, recycled content) can lead to 
an increased burden on economic operators and negative effects on the environment.  
To avoid such downsides, the best solution from our point of view is a harmonised European label, 
which does not use colour-coded or language-dependent signage. Different methods of application 
should be usable, such as printing, engraving, or casting and most importantly a QR code. This QR 
code should be compatible with similar digital data carriers required in other European legislation to 
avoid a multitude of QR codes on the packaging or product. In this regard, a single QR code for both, 
product and packaging should be able to be used but should not be mandatory.  
A uniform European label for packaging would also have the added value of being understandable to 
all European citizens everywhere in the Union and stop the confusing effects of a multitude of labels 
on the same unit of packaging, facilitating its positive effects on for example sorting. We believe 
fragmented national labelling requirements lead to additional efforts such as more complex logistics, 
causing higher energy use and CO2-Emmissions. Additionally, a sufficient transitional period for a 
harmonised label should be given for industry to adapt. 
Lastly, we also want to stress that as an industry, the power tools sector is already now mainly using 
sturdy boxes, kit boxes and containers, that serve to contain, transport and protect the power tools 
during their entire lifetime, and which serve as an integral part of our products to our customers. These 
boxes, kit boxes and containers are used on a daily basis to transport the wide array of power tools 
necessary for a given construction site to the site in vans and other vehicles and are then again used 
to distribute the tools on-site, to make sure that the required accessories such as different drills or 
sawblades, batteries and chargers are always accessible. In this regard, we call on the legislato rs to 
include power tool boxes, kit boxes and containers as an indicative example of non-packaging in 
Annex I, in the same way as boxes for traditional mechanical tools. 
We as EPTA want to thank the European legislators for this chance to be in dialogue and are looking 
forward to further involvement as stakeholders in the PPWR legislative process. More details 
regarding our main concerns can be found in the attached joint position paper.

 Electro and Digital Industry Association 
 
Statement on the European Commission’s proposal for a 
Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation 
March 3, 2023 
 
General remarks  
We welcome the possibility to comment the European Commission’s proposal for a new Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). ZVEI e.V. - Germany’s Electric and Digital Industry association and 
EPTA – the European Power Tool Association represent many companies that are concerned by the draft 
proposal. Packaging is a fundamental part of our industry since it protects goods and helps to convey 
information in the b2c and b2b sector. Over the last years, the approach to packaging itself has fundamentally 
changed: It is now not only seen as a way of protecting goods, but also as a relevant aspect when it comes to 
the protection of our environment. The European Union strives to achieve a circular economy ; consequently, the 
whole world of consumption needs to adapt. It is no longer only about the product itself, but also about the 
materials it comes with.  
The electrical and digital industry has already been working on improving products’ impact on the environment 
for years, driven by ecodesign requirements and energy labelling. Now, the European Commission plans to 
review another aspect, packaging. After carefully having analysed the draft and its annexes, we highlight certain 
points in the following:  
 
Our main recommendations and concerns are: 
 
1) We support the change from directive to regulation. 
 
2) We do not support re-use requirements for transport packaging and for the special case of household 
appliances without a proper assessment. Before setting up re-use requirements, an impact 
assessment on feasibility and concrete transposition needs to be conducted.   
 
3) The foreseen recycling targets are too ambitious. Recycling targets must be adapted to the available 
material resources. In addition, conflicts of objectives with other goals, e.g., the reduction of material 
used, must be resolved.  
 
4) We support the planned introduction of a harmonised label. The need for harmonisation of labelling 
on European level is high. Member states should not have the possibility to set up individual, national 
labels for their EPR systems or information requirements, since this permission contradicts the 
harmonisation efforts. Additionally, it should be reviewed if well-known, already used systems can be 
used instead of new labels. In any case, producers should be able to convey the necessary 
information independent of the method used like printing, engraving, embossing etc., which is why 
colour-coding is not feasible. Digital methods of information should be preferred. 
 
5) A declaration of conformity for packaging creates an unnecessary bureaucratic burden. Especially 
when focusing on small and medium enterprises, any non-expedient bureaucratic burden must be 
avoided. A storage time of ten years for the technical documentation of packaging material and the 
creation of a DoC itself is not appropriate. 
  
6) Setting up design for recycling criteria, not on material-basis but based on efficient sorting, collection, 
and recycling, is only efficient if the collection and recycling structures in all member states are 
aligned with each other quickly. Otherwise, this requirement will distort the internal market. 
 
7) The aim of packaging minimisation needs further clarification of wording and exceptions. The special 
shape of some of our products makes it barely possible to comply with these specifications. 
 
In general, the environmental impact over the entire lifecycle as such should be the basis for the new 
requirements. Under some conditions neither re-use nor the use of a certain percentage of recycled material 
is beneficial for the environment. This should be properly considered. We highly encourage the European 
legislators to prioritize goals.

Detailed comments 
 
1. Change from Directive to Regulation and Protection of the Free movement of goods 
The European Commission envisages to change the political framework of the packaging issue. This also 
affects the effectiveness of the implemented regulation in the member states. Up to now, the current Packaging 
and Waste Packaging Directive (PPWD) allows member states to adapt the European framework to their 
national situation. Therefore, different systems for packaging collection with various cost systems exist within 
the European Union. The complexity of these systems is not always easy to understand. The collection of 
packaging at households and other sources of waste is also very heterogeneo us in terms of frequency and the 
collection systems (bags, bins, recycling centres) and waste collection vehicles used.  
 
Even more concerning are the information requirements and national labels some member states have 
implemented during the last years. These labels create a huge fragmentation of the internal market, since 
manufacturers are forced to create national versions of packaging. Already being implemented national rules 
have been recognized as being a barrier to trade. Additional national legislat ion from the Member States will 
further imbalance the EU single market. Reducing these national attempts by creating a regulation helps 
industrial actors on the market to be compliant with the requirements for the European market. Additionally, a 
regulation allows to create only one packaging design for the whole European Union, which facilitates economic 
processes for all, smaller and medium enterprises (SMEs) and larger companies. Therefore, it is equally 
important to also clearly state – as is done in art. 4(2)-(4) – that the placing on the market of packaging that 
complies with the rules of the PPWR may not be prohibited, restricted, or impeded. 
 
 
2. Clarification of some definitions (art. 3) 
It is of utmost importance to use legally unambiguous definitions in the legislative text. In the current draft, some 
definitions are not clear. Hence, we will welcome clarification on the following points: 
  
• Art 3(1)-(4): Despite the given definition, we are unsure of what precisely is targeted as “transport 
packaging”. For instance, a drum is obviously meant for transporting cables but is also used on site to 
manipulate cables during their installation. Likewise, the distinction between “grouped” and  “transport 
packaging” may be complicated, e.g., a pallet with coils of wire can be considered both as grouped and 
transport packaging. From our understanding, the new distinction between transport packaging and all 
other packaging creates confusion. According to the current definition, nearly all packaging is 
considered as transport packaging. Concurrently, some main aspects of transport packaging, as pallets 
and wrapping foil, are mentioned separately in the regulation. Furthermore, some components of end-
products can also be considered as packaging according to the current definitions, however, these 
components serve more than a packaging function: they protect the parts during transport and storage 
at the logistic centres and dealers, but also serve as a means to properly store and protect the parts 
during the lifetime of the product with the end-consumer e.g., reusable pouches and cases for 
accessories, boxes to store power tools, tools or heads of screwdrivers, pouches to store razor 
accessories or attachments and accessories of small domestic appliances. We ask the European 
legislators for clarification of packaging definitions here and to properly exclude packaging that serves 
as storage and protection during use at the end-consumer.  
 
• Art 3(31): “recyclability” is too generic. We will welcome specific requirements by material categories. 
In addition, the statement “All packaging should be recyclable by design by 2030” requires a common 
EU-wide definition of recyclability based on the potential of packaging materials to be recycled 
considering existing and emerging waste management infrastructure and technologies. Besides, the 
definition of recyclability and the materials that fall under the definition should be updated regularly, 
since technology develops, and materials used change. All packaging materials should be categorized 
accordingly. 
 
• Art 26 / Art. 27: “large household appliances listed in point 2 of Annex II to Directive 2012/19/EU ” 
creates confusion. Point 2 of Annex II are small appliances. So, is this part referring to large or small 
appliances? For both kinds of appliances, the target of 90% is quite ambitious, not to say unachievable.  
 
• Art 3(5): “e-commerce” packaging is difficult to understand. Sales packaging and transport packaging 
usually have different disposal routes. What is the background to e-commerce packaging being 
classified as transport packaging? The practical implementation could prove to be complex. There are 
also contradictions in art. 11 and art. 21.  
3 
• Art 3(19): “composite packaging”: It is unclear whether there are thresholds for practicality, e.g., < 5%, 
that quantitatively subordinate materials can be assigned to the main fraction.  
 
• Art 3(23): The definition “single-use packaging” is defined by exclusion and in doing so referencing 
“reusable packaging”. However, “reusable packaging” is not defined in the definitions but in art. 10. 
 
• Art 3(37): “innovative packaging”: It is unclear to us how "new in relation to materials, design or 
production processes” is interpreted in this definition. 
 
• On definitions from other EU legislation: While the reference to the Ecodesign framework is laudable 
in terms of consistency, it is questionable to reference terms that are not legal yet. This is es pecially 
true for the debated “substance of concern” definition. 
 
 
3. Re-use of transport and household packaging (art. 26) 
The draft proposal foresees the implementation of re-use systems for several kinds of packaging. The focus is 
set on packaging of large electric household appliances and transport packaging. Both are expected to be in re -
use systems for 90% by 2030 (cf. art. 26 (1)). These goals are quite ambitious and unrealistic from our point of 
view. Right now, no re-use systems for packaging of electric goods exist beyond partial solutions for parts 
supply and internal logistics. In some specific b2b cases, like in the car production industry, special boxes are 
also used to transport components. But this system cannot be easily transferred from one unique industry to 
another. In the case of the car industry, all participants of the b2b-chain are known and stay in contact with each 
other. The participating companies exchange the standardized boxes among each other, but the circle stays 
closed. But the described system will not work automatically with copy-paste-mode: Neither in the case of b2b 
or b2c commerce of electric (household) appliances, re-use systems for packaging are established right now. 
Talking about re-use systems for this sector, we question its effectiveness for several reasons:  
 
The most important aspect regarding the topic of re-use is the carbon footprint. In the European Commission’s 
proposal, re-use packaging is estimated to be the solution to reduce the impact of packaging on the 
environment. We would like to point out here that there is currently no comprehensive scientific evidence to 
support this assumption. We therefore call on the European legislators to first carry out well-founded scientific 
studies on this aspect. Re-use packaging requires more material to be used to make the packaging last longer; 
more logistics to transport the packaging back, to store and to handle it; as well as more water and detergent for 
cleaning the packaging.  
Additionally, the material for re-use packaging may be less recycling friendly than material used for single -use 
packaging. The materials currently used for single use packaging are mainly EPS and cardboard1. Both 
materials can be collected easily for households and businesses. The recycling quota is very high 2 and most of 
the material is re-used for new packaging. Today, electric components, spare parts and appliances  are mainly 
packed in this kind of single use packaging material. This raises the question to what extent it is ecologically 
more efficient to replace these materials by re-use boxes made from plastics and fibers. And even if these re-
use boxes were to have a recycled content, they would compete with other recycled applications; the sufficient 
availability on the market may be doubted as explained in the next chapter. We therefore question to what 
extent re-use packaging for transport packaging and household appliances will reach its aim to be more 
sustainable than single-use packaging.  
 
Second, comprehensive logistic systems must be implemented. In the case of large electric household 
appliances, a huge number of manufacturers exists on the European market . They sell products either directly 
to consumers or to other distributors which sell the products further to the consumer. Here, a manufacturer does 
not know all the concerned actors in the distribution chain and especially not the end -user. Thus, it is difficult to 
monitor where a packaging finally ends up. But even if the packaging’s location can be identified via QR code 
or other digital tools, logistic challenges persist. Either consumers are obliged to bring back the bulky, large 
packaging to a shop or community collection point, or delivery companies are needed to collect the packaging  
(especially if we are talking about online purchases). However, a lack of staff is becoming increasingly apparent 
here. A time lag must be expected between the handover and the return of the reusable packaging. Creating an

Third, implementing a re-use system requires a well-thought-out financial background system. Studies and

These aspects underline the efforts society, industry and politics must do to implement re-use packaging: 
Consumers must learn to adapt to a new system, logistic industries must be set up and tested and one should 
also not forget that packaging must be developed, tested, and certified before being sent out. Consequently, we 
assume 90% of transport and large household appliances packaging to be reusable and be integrated in a 
system in 2030 is too ambitious. The change should start with a reduced scope and give more time to 
manufacturers and developers of packaging to develop and to test new packaging.  
 
Regarding the re-use of pallet wrappings and straps for stabilization and protection of products put on pallets 
during transport as described in art. 26 (9): It is unrealistic to expect to be able to re-use such articles. These 
articles are supplied in roll/reel form and unwound/cut to length by the application equipment. After the transit 
period when they are removed, they are torn and dirty and are now finite lengths  that cannot be re-applied to 
pallets. These articles should only be subject to the recycled content requirements of Article 7.  
 
 
4. Minimum recycled content in plastic packaging (art. 7) 
The draft envisages a minimum recycled content for packaging. We agree on the fact that using recycled 
content is favourable compared to virgin material to protect the environment and to work more resource 
efficient. Already today, many producers of electric products, spare parts and components use recycled content 
in their packaging on a voluntary basis. But the aim is limited by the amount of recycled content recovered from 
post-consumer and post-industry plastic waste available: A study of the German market highlights that 49% of 
virgin packaging material cannot be replaced by recycled content for different reasons: Sometimes the material 
is not available at all, sometimes the use of the material is limited by its colour, its odour, its processing, or its 
efficiency.4 For companies, recycled content may create uncertainties: Plastic made from recycled material may 
retain impurities, and hence, it is not always suitable to be used for packaging. Thus, the use of recycled 
contents often impairs the quality of packaging and may lead to an increase in the volume of the packaging so 
that it can sufficiently perform its protection function. 
 
It is therefore a fallacy to look purely at the figures for packaging material, which is currently pro duced with little 
recycled content. Furthermore, we face shortages of post-consumer recycled material feedstock. Due to the 
pandemic, lockdowns, and global supply chain interruptions the volume of production of recyclable resins, 
plastics and other raw materials has either slowed extremely or come to a complete halt.  
 
Thus, the European Commission’s goal for recycled content in packaging from 2030 on is quite ambitious. For 
our sector, according to art. 7(1)(d) 35% per unit of packaging is supposed to be recycled content recovered 
from post-consumer plastic waste within less than ten years. Since the recycled material recovered from post-
consumer plastic waste is not available in the amount the regulation assumes, we are wondering how producers 
are supposed to fulfil this obligation. Lower targets would be more realistic and give manufacturers time to 
change their production and design of packaging.

5. Labelling of packaging (art 4 & art. 11) 
We expressly welcome the European Commission’s aim to address the flood of national labels currently 
emerging all over Europe. These labels create a disproportionate burden on manufacturers and hamper the free 
circulation of products on the European internal market. It is therefore crucial for the new European packaging 
framework to tackle this issue. However, the current proposal leaves some questions open:  
Art. 4(1) aims to ensure the free movement of goods. Concurrently, art. 4(3) allows member states to have 
national labels if they do not conflict with the European regulation and do not “prohibit, restrict or impede the 
placing on the market of packaging”. From our point of view, it is unclear what “conflicting” in this context 
means. Although the intention of the paragraph to use “confusing” symbols is understandable, the designation 
of symbols that are mandatory by international or non-EU countries must not be classified as “confusing”. It is of 
high importance that the European legislators make clear here that national labels should only be voluntary and 
no condition for manufacturers to sell a product in a certain country. In addition, national labels should no longer 
be a requirement for placing on the market in a state once the new Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation 
is adopted.  
 
In this context, it is also of upmost importance that the European Commission quickly implements a European 
label to reduce uncertainty. The timeline proposed in the draft creates certain challenges for manufacturers in 
this case, since the European Commission plans to set up the criteria only two years before they come into 
force. The European legislators are asked to check if currently available labels which are still known by the 
consumers can be used. It should be avoided that an extensive information campaign becomes necessary to 
achieve a better separation of packaging materials. With so much information coming at consumers, it will be 
difficult to raise consumer awareness for new packaging labelling. 
 
Regarding the more technical aspects of labelling: It should be ensured that symbols are used that are neither 
color-coded nor subtitled in different languages. Therefore, ideally the symbols are without colour specification, 
can be printed/engraved/casted with high contrast and can be used in all 27 member states because no 
differentiation by country or language is required. In exceptions, additional information that is absolutely 
necessary should be accessible via the intended QR code and not on the labelli ng directly. This information, 
which is identical in content in all countries, may be provided in several languages for the recipient countries.  
The compatibility with QR code required in other laws (e.g., Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation 
(ESPR)/ Digital Product Passport) must be guaranteed and a multitude of QR codes on the packaging/product 
prevented to avoid consumer confusion. A single QR code for product and packaging should be able to be used 
but should not be mandatory, referring to all other necessary information. 
 
There should be exemptions for labelling where the material composition is obvious such as a paper bag, 
having to print “PAP” onto a paper bag or cardboard carton adds unnecessary cost and brings no benefit.  
 
Referring to art. 12 the retroactive labelling of waste receptacles for the collection of packaging waste does not 
seem appropriate in terms of additional effort. Other implementations such as awareness campaigns should 
also be possible. If there is to be compulsory labelling of waste receptacles, we ask the legislators to apply it 
only to new ones as imprinting is then possible in the injection molding process. 
 
 
6. Obligations of manufacturers - technical documentation (art. 13) 
Another aspect that is critical from our point of view is the creation of a technical declaration of conformity for 
each packaging. Some of the required details go far beyond necessary information for business partners or 
consumers and should therefore be strictly remain internal. Upon request by market authorities, companies may 
explain why they used a certain technique or material to protect the good. But this kind of information should not 
be communicated externally. Besides, the requested data is quite extensive.  
 
In its current version, art. 13 would create huge administrative burden, which is mainly a problem for small and 
medium enterprises. Also, it is unclear why it is necessary to keep the technical documentation of the packaging 
for ten years. A 10-years period is useful for a longer lasting product, but with the exemption of re-use and refill 
packaging the packaging as such will be recycled in a shorter time.  
 
Additionally, it is questionable whether the requirements of Annex VII are appropriate for a produc t which is 
mainly consisting of a single material.  
 
The requirement to mark packaging with a type, batch or serial number brings an unreasonable burden for little 
value. If the articles are too small to mark, then the link between the article and the docu mentation is impossible 
to trace. It will restrict opportunities for having multiple suppliers of packaging articles necessary to ensure 
6 
disruption free production. For packaging that needs to have an identifier, there should be a voluntary option to 
link it to the product’s identifier. 
 
To ensure the competitiveness of European local companies, any additional bureaucracy that does not directly 
benefit the natural environment has to be avoided. Therefore, the European legislators are asked if a 
Declaration of Conformity for packaging material is really necessary and the adequate tool to achieve the goals. 
The legal requirements are clear and there will be bilateral contracts and specifications between packaging 
producers and users. An additional DoC does not have an added value for consumers or businesses when 
making their purchasing choices. If the legislators insist that a DoC for packaging should be introduced, a single 
DoC for product and packaging should be able to be used where possible but should not be mandatory. Market 
surveillance authorities need to enforce the requirements to ensure a level playing field. We encourage the 
European legislators to rethink about these aspects. 
 
 
7. Design for recycling criteria (art. 6) 
The draft foresees that all packaging in the European Union is recyclable from 2030 on. The European 
Commission is going to set up a delegated act defining the criteria for recyclability. In general, it is important that 
recyclability is assessed in a uniform way to ensure the free movement of goods in the European Union. 
Unfortunately, the current approach is not based on materials, but on the efficiency of existing sorting and 
recycling infrastructure in the Member States. If the sorting and recycling facilities are assessed differently 
between individual Member States, this would significantly distort the internal market.  Up to now, the concrete 
definition of “recyclability” is defined in the context of the recycling process (and in the context of the design 
process) is not clear to us. For example, some paper materials are not recyclable due to degraded fibres and 
amyloidised cellulose contained in them. Such paper materials have a better environmental impact than e.g., 
plastic packaging. The derogation for innovative packaging is not clear in its scope.  
 
We ask the European legislators to clarify whether any material application that can be found for the recycled 
content counts for the recyclability of the material or whether the recycled content must be reused for its original 
purpose. We also have the question of how the new specifications are to be linked to the performance classes 
defined in Annex II. 
 
Furthermore, without timely specific material requirements the packaging industry and manufacturers of goods 
need time to adapt to the new criteria for packaging. The design for recycling criteria shall be defined on the 
base of existing standards on recyclability (e.g., EN45555), to also ensure a level playing field. Therefore, a 
sufficient transition period between the publication of the design for recycling criteria is needed of at least 48 
months.   
 
 
8. Packaging minimisation (art. 9 & annex IV) 
The terminology of “empty space” is of particular importance. A continuous provision of evidence is to be 
rejected, a verification within the framework of market surveillance and/or random sampling within the 
framework of existing expert inspections appear to be appropriate. Additionally, as all packaging is concerned 
by this requirement, the question comes up whether this requirement unnecessarily hampers the adoption of 
reusable transport packaging as might only be available in certain formats and thus – dependent on the size of 
the order – will have more or less “empty space” but might nonetheless be more beneficial to the environment 
than a non-reusable option.  
 
Manufacturers of finished goods invariably purchase packaging articles such as plastic bags and cardboard 
cartons from suppliers that produce a range of standard sizes. This may on occasion require the manufacturer 
of the finished good to choose a bag or carton that is a little larger than is necessary. It is also unreasonable to 
expect manufacturers of finished goods to stock a large variety of shapes and sizes or to have to design 
bespoke cartons. 
 
 
9. Requirements for substances in packaging (art. 5) 
The definition of substances of concern is very broad and makes it burdensome for industry to trac e them. In 
addition, an appropriate grace period when new substances of concern are listed is required. It is also important 
to ensure consistency with existing chemical legislation in the European Union. Generally, substance 
restrictions should be handled in REACH in a harmonized way in the EU, instead of in other legislation (avoid 
double regulation) or on national level.

If the European Commission is adopting Delegated Acts in accordance with art. 5(5) a), a sufficient stakeholder 
consultation needs to be carried out and an appropriate transitional period must be granted.

PACKAGING FOR THE FUTURE  
IKEA view on the proposal for an EU Packaging & Packaging Waste Regulation  
For nearly 80 years, packaging has played a key role in the IKEA business model. Innovative and 
universal approaches to packaging and labelling enable IKEA to optimize production and distribution 
and minimize waste on the more than four billion customer pa ckages passing through IKEA value 
chain each year. On an annual basis, IKEA spends over 1 billion Euros on approximately 920  000 
tonnes of packaging material.  And with these big volumes come big responsibilities. Like our 
approach to our products, we aim to  make product packaging as sustainable, affordable, and 
customer friendly as possible. For example, we are on a journey to completely phase out plastic from 
consumer packaging solutions by 2028 , although some renewable or recycled plastic packaging 
might remain in the  parts of the IKEA food range where it is needed to secure quality and food safety 
standards beyond 2028.  
IKEA supports the efforts of the proposed Regulation for Packaging and Packaging Waste (PPWR), 
published by the European Commission on 30 November 2022 . We applaud the European 
Commission’s decision to change the Directive to a Regulation and anchor the proposal under the 
Internal Market legal base, which will bring increased harmonization, support further strengthening 
of the EU Single Market, and decrease complexity for European businesses. Prevention and reduction 
of waste are fundamental elements for a successful transition from a linear to a circular econom y.  
 
 We recommend to the co-legislators to ensure the following key enablers:  
1. A harmonised approach to packaging markings is needed to secure the free circulation of goods 
and to simplify communication to consumers.  
2. Harmonised rules for Extended Producer responsibility (EPR) and ecomodulation will 
reduce administrative burdens and strengthen incentives aimed at supporting sustainability 
efforts 
3. Incentivising packaging reuse and refill is important. However  waste prevention and recycling 
play a complementary role and no solution should be assessed in isolation. Reuse targets should 
be applied only if based on solid evidence for reduced environmental impact.  
4. Ensure an adequate transition period, taking into account the complexity of the proposal and 
step-wise development of requirements  
5. Industry involvement is key, especially on technical aspects, which are to be discussed in 
secondary legislation. We encourage setting up a Packaging Forum. 
 
To support the transition to the circular economy  and a positive framework for packaging 
sustainability, we encourage the EU to adopt a modern, clear, simple, and harmonised regulatory 
framework for packaging and packaging waste . We take the opportunity to share our knowledge, 
insights, and recommendations with co-legislators through this paper  and we remain at your 
disposal for any further clarification.

1) Harmonisation of packaging markings 
 
Packaging labels play an important role in guiding consumer behaviour. For example , widespread 
acknowledgement about the importance of recycling has led to several Member State proposals  on symbols 
and/or instructions to be included on packaging to support customers in sorting packaging waste correctly . 
While the intent is positive, the lack of EU -harmonised rules has led to a complicated web of misaligned  - and 
sometimes conflicting - actions at National level, preventing the free circulation of goods. Without a common 
approach across the EU, we will encounter  many negative consequences , including logistical disruptions, 
increased use of materials, trade barriers and , ultimately information overload, which can disincentivize 
customer action. For these reasons: 
 
• We welcome Art 12 of the PPWR proposal to harmonise labels that enable the separate collection of 
packaging waste fractions . Our recommendation is  to establish  a set of  customer-friendly symbols 
indicating recyclability and/or sorting. A successful package labelling system needs to be flexible enough to 
allow for adaptation for both local waste collection schemes and global supply capabilities. To enable it: 
o The more generic a symbol-based system is, the easier to adapt to local, regional or national waste 
collection systems.  
o Symbols on packaging should be monochrome, to allow easy printing in various packaging 
materials, and not include text, to avoid translations.  
o The same s ymbols included on -pack should be used at national and local levels  to guide 
consumers in sorting according to local needs. Creating such a common, generic set of symbols 
could offer a more intuitive, consumer-friendly approach to demonstrate how packaging should be 
sorted no matter where the consumer sits in the EU.  
Given the urgency of EU action on this matter, we believe that proposed entry into force in 2028 would 
be too late. Additionally, any national initiatives by Member States to introduce new packaging labelling 
requirements should be paused until an EU wide scheme is agreed. 
 
• We are concerned by the proposal to maintain local differences for symbols connected to Extended 
Producer Responsibility Schemes ( EPR) (recital 49; article 4 par. 5; art. 11 par. 8 ). Such exception would 
undermine the effort embedded in this Proposal to harmonize markings on -pack, resulting potentially in 
the co-existence of dozens of different symbols  yet only locally relevant . We are concerned that it would  
offer a legal base for Member States to retain national markings on separate collection. If the aim is to prove 
the financial contribution to a national EPR scheme, and since such information is not relevant for the 
consumer, we believe that a marking/symbol is not the most appropriate solution . However, should a 
symbol be deemed necessary, it should be harmonized across the EU.   
 
• We welcome the e ffort to ensure harmonization and guarantee the free movement of goods (art 4 
par. 1,2,3), but the current wording of article 4, paragraph 4, has the potential to undermine these 
efforts by allowing for national-specific sustainability or information requirements for packaging.  To 
protect the Single Market and in respect of the legal base for the PPWR, National requirements that would 
introduce changes to packaging requirements would behave as a barrier to trade. 
 
• We equally welcome the proposal to harmonise information on material composition, also allowing for 
new technologies such as digital marking (art. 11 par 1, 6). However, art. 11 paragraph 4 mandates that this 
information should be included in a label printed or engraved visibly. While such a label is one of the possible 
solutions, the legislation should not  exclude less visible ways to indicate the material composition (e.g. 
digital marking), if deemed appropriate for the goal and if aligned with technology development.     
 
• We support the proposal to create a voluntary harmonised label to indicate the recycled content  of 
packaging (recital 46; article 11, par 3) . While the PPWR proposal focuses on  recycled plastic, we believe it 
would be also a great  opportunity to harmonise measurement methods  for other packaging material 
streams. It is also important to ensure consistent principles with other legislations, e.g. the future Ecodesign 
for Sustainable Products Regulation.

2) Extended Producer Responsibility (EPRs) 
Different EPR systems are set at the Member State level, each with different registration rules and reporting 
obligations. Even when there are common EPR rules in the EU, such as within the Packaging and Packaging 
Waste Directive (PPWD), the scope, fees, and reporting requirements vary across  different Member States 
leading to a complex, expensive, and inefficient system. A scattered approach to EPR rules, including on 
modulation of fees, creates uncertainties and inconsistencies, undermining efforts for incentivizing better, 
circular, practices. For these reasons: 
• We support the PPWR proposal to harmonise criteria for the modulation of extended producer 
responsibility fees, which we hope would set precedent for other product categories to follow (recital 
25, article 6 and 7). Harmonised rules on eco -modulation will better steer incentives in packaging 
development and should overrule local approaches. It is important to clearly define rules on how to measure 
recycled content . W orking towards industry certification methods for both downstream use rs and 
suppliers of recycled material would be a step in the right direction. While the PPWR seems to focus on 
recycled content, w e believe the use of renewable material should also be one of the criteria for eco-
modulation.   
• Today, we are f acing scattered and complex rules on registration, with different type s of information 
requested across EU countries and unclear responsibilities among producers and producer responsibility 
organizations (PROs). We welcome that the PPWR proposal identifies  this as an area for improvement 
(Section 3). However, we believe the current proposal could be improved to steer and  simplify, to the 
largest extent possible, the registration and data sharing process , reducing duplications of 
registrations in every Member State.  Clearer responsibilities should be defined between producers and 
PROs.  
• We are concerned by the proposal to allow Member States to introduce market -specific labelling 
requirements to identify EPR Schemes (recital 49 ; article 4 par. 5; art. 11 par. 8). If local EPR markings are 
mandatory to apply on-pack, it could result in the co-existence of dozens of different symbols, only locally 
relevant, in the same packaging, with a confusing message to our costumer.   
 
3) Reuse and refill targets  
 
While waste prevention continues to be the primary goal, the holistic impact of the waste hierarchy should be 
considered. In the case of packaging, reusability and recyclability cannot always be placed in a strict hierarchy  
because too much depends on the concrete situation, including e.g. product-type, packaging level (primary, 
secondary, tertiary), packaging functionality . In addition, “no packaging” should be generally preferred over 
“reusable” solutions every time it lowers the environmental impact. Therefore, we recommend that only in case 
of clear environmental benefits of reusable packaging, concrete quotas should be prescribed, whereby waste 
savings must be weighed against other environmental impacts such as additional transport energy or carbon 
emissions. Beyond accounting for environmental, social, and economic factors, reuse targets should be based 
on a neutral and science-based analysis of the entire value chain.   
Cost and convenience are a factor in the employment and adoption of packaging reuse schemes. Incentives can 
play a significant role in accelerating the investment into the development of practical and scalable solutions for 
producers and motivate consumers to adopt new behaviours . We invite the co-legislators to pay careful 
attention to these issues, always investigating the most environmentally positive impact in practice.   
We offer the following considerations and clarifying questions to the co-legislators:

• Setting reuse targets alone, without clear and specific implementation criteria, will not be sufficient 
to drive truly systemic change.  Successful reuse systems require support and buy -in from not only 
economic operators, but the entire packaging value chain, public authorities, waste management sector, 
and of course consumers . We therefore ask the European Commission to consider additional thorough 
analysis based on feasibility studies, which would take into account this systemic approach including reverse 
logistics requirements  (incl. countries outside the EU) , recyclability requirements, logistic pools, local 
distribution, reusable packaging formats, minimum rotations, standardization and product safety/hygiene 
protocols with enforceable conditions.  
 
• Scope and definitions need to be clarified for the targets on transport, eCommerce, and household 
appliances packaging. We ask the co-legislators to further clarify the scope and definitions for each target, 
making it clear for companies which packaging types are considered reusable and at what stage of the 
transport journey . Concrete examples should be provided to ensure consistent interpretations by all  
economic operators, eliminating misaligned efforts and  discrepancies in what is considered as ‘transport 
packaging’.  
 
• We ask the co -legislators to ensure that  reuse systems for take -away and ready -prepared food 
packaging are, in practice, as economically and environmentally viable as possible. Implementation criteria 
should consider deposit fees, reverse logistics, consumer awareness, minimum rotations , and consumer 
behaviour, including the likelihood of frequency in consumers’ visits. 
 
4) Suitable implementation timelines 
 
We recommend the co-legislators to ensure an adequate transition period, taking into account the complexity 
of the proposal . In addition, since many of the requirements established in the PPWR will need to be further 
detailed in delegated or implementing acts, and the PPWR framework legislation has already set specific dates 
for targets and ambitions to be met, there is a risk that delays in the adoption of secondary legislation can 
let little or no time for implementation of the set requirements . Against this risk and to increase legal 
certainty, the framework legislation should recognize that a reasonable transitional time will apply after the 
adoption of the secondary legislation.   
 
5) Industry involvement in the development of secondary legislation 
 
We ask the Commission to establish a formal channel for dialogue with the wider industry  as well as the 
academic and research communities, in order to together develop scalable and realistic solutions, establish clear 
and harmonized definitions, and set effective implementation criteria. 
 
6) Others 
 
Void space limits for eCommerce packaging 
 
Improving packaging optimization and using as little material as possible while still protecting the product is 
part our business DNA. In addition to  hyper-optimizing the flat pack, we are scaling up a  “box on demand ” 
solution for eCommerce with packaging tailored to individual shipments. In the markets where it is already 
applied, it has substantially reduced the volume of packaging materials used and  reduced both packaging and 
transport costs since more parcels can now be delivered per vehicle. 
 
24 April 2023 
We welcome the Commission’s proposal to minimize excessive eCommerce packaging and support the 40% 
void space limit target to be reached by 2030 . Regarding article 21 on obligations related to excessive 
packaging, we ask the co-legislators to consider the following recommendations: 
• Develop a detailed calculation methodology  to clarify how the void space in packaging is measured, in 
consultation with  the industry. The methodology should take into account situations where multiple 
products are packaged in one order as well as how to calculate the void space for products whose volume 
can be minimized (I.e. plush toys, pillows, mats, etc) to accommodate smaller packaging. At IKEA, when 
packing goods such as baskets and bins for eCommerce deliveries, we take the opportunity to fill the cavity 
space with other small products. These situations where the void space would account to a negative % 
should also be considered.  
• Allow for exemptions from the 40% void space limit for certain packaging applications when appropriate. 
E.g. when primary sales packaging is used as eCommerce packaging or for fragile goods which need 
additional protection.  
• Consider that there are cases where the products’ primary packaging is used for e -commerce purposes as 
well. These efforts should be defined as waste prevention.

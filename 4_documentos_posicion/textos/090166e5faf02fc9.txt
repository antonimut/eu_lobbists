Position paper 
Summary of policy asks  
 
Article 5: requirements for substances in packaging  
• Amend Article 5 to void duplication with existing chemicals and food safety EU legislation by referencing 
“substances of concern that pose an environmental risk” in Article 5(1) and offering clarity of what the 
“minimisation” requirement entails.  
Article 6: packaging recyclability  
• EU design for recycling criteria develope d by the European Commission should cover all technicalities 
referring to the packaging type assessed. In this respect, there is no need for an additional negative list 
or description of design for recycling parameters in the Annexes of the PPWR.  
• Annex II Table 2: Grades A – E should be linked to the compatibility of the packaging type with the design-
for-recycling EU criteria (e.g. A = highest compatibility, B = high – medium compatibility, C = medium 
compatibility, D = medium – low compatibility, E = lowest / no compatibility).    
• EPR fees should be eco-modulated based on recyclability only.  
Article 7: recycled content targets for plastic packaging  
• Focus the scope of Article 7(1) on plastic packaging and as an average on the portfolio placed on the 
market by producers.   
• Remove the mandate of the 2040 recycled content targets for plastic packaging, instead ensuring they 
are either set as aspirational or at a later stage based on robust data and a mature EU market for 
secondary raw materials.  
Article 8: compostable packaging 
• Remove the letter (g) from the scope of Article 8(1) and enable the correct labelling of compostable 
packaging to allow for correct consumer sorting and disposal.   
Article 10 and 26: reuse and refill targets 
• Article 26(12) should be amended to include an obligation that the majority of transport packaging used 
by an economic operator between its sites or between its factories and its customers is reusable.   
• Industrial reuse obligations should exclude pallet wrappings.   
• Refill at home should be recognised as reuse.  
Article 22: restrictions of packaging formats  
• Remove Article 22 and Annex V from the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation.  
Article 38: prevention of packaging waste  
• Remove Article 38 (3) on prevention of packaging waste, which allows Member State to achieve the 
PPWR targets by a variety of measures at their discretion.  Avoid imposing additional waste prevention 
targets without a robust impact assessment. Remove Art 45(2)(b) which allows the charging of single use 
packaging above alternative options.

Requirements that are missing completely from the PPWR  
Lifecycle thinking (otherwise mandated via WFD Article 4)   
Despite attempting to curb packaging waste pollution and increase secondary raw materials on the EU market, 
the PPWR misses out on the opportunity of grounding its sustainability requirements in measures that truly  
deliver the best environmental outcome, based on lifecycle of product environmental footprint assessments.  
Measures based on product environmental footprints or lifecycle assessments are already foreseen in EU 
proposed legislation on product sustainability or green claims, and should be strengthened in the PPWR text.  
Some of the proposed requirements of recycled content, reuse targets, or compostability fail at taking into 
account the results of increased GHG emissions, therefore directly contradicting the EU’s climate goals.   
Policy ask: Article 2 should foresee that the PPWR leads to a better environmental outcome.   
 
Substances in packaging (Article 5)  
We welcome the PPWR’s focus to address packaging from all sustainability points of view. We caution however 
that Article 5 addressing requirements of packaging does not use the correct terminology and may have 
significant repercussions if not re-assessed in the adoption process.  
Only substances of concern that pose environmental risks should be addressed by the PPWR, firstly to ensure 
consistency with the circularity goals of the regulation  (Article 1), and secondly, to avoid regulatory confusion 
with the EU’s food product safety and chemicals legislation which already regulate the presence of substances 
in packaging from a chemical safety and human health point of view. The compliance obligations advanced by 
this article fail to account for the documentati on and information needed to be transmitted throughout the 
supply chain, for example documentation related to raw materials purchased by suppliers. We ask that this is 
also rectified.  
It should be clarified what it means to ‘minimise’ the presence and con centration of substances of concern , 
firstly by clarifying that the substances of concern that pose environmental concerns should be minimised. A 
workable minimisation requirement should refer to an environmental impact “as low as reasonably achievable”.  
Policy ask: Amend Article 5 to void duplication with existing chemicals and food safety EU legislation by 
referencing “substances of concern that pose an environmental risk” in Article 5(1) and offering clarity of what 
the “minimisation” requirement entails. 
 
Recyclable packaging (Article 6)  
a. 2030 recyclability of packaging (by design): concrete deadlines and use of industry design guidelines 
Amcor strongly welcomes the focus of the proposal on design for recycling and the approach to develop design 
for recycling EU criteria which would demonstrate packaging recyclability in 2030 and beyond.  
We feel however that the lack of concrete deadlines for the European Commission to adopt secondary legislation 
in this respect continues the legal uncertainty borne by the EU packaging value chain  industry and would delay 
achieving the recycling targets of the PPWD and PPWR. Therefore, we recommend EU policy makers to introduce 
1 January 2026 as the deadline for the adoption of EU design for recycling criteria under Article 6 paragraph 4. 
Furthermore, we note that industry design -for-recycling guidelines are not represented as a source for the 
European Commission to proceed with the adoption of the EU design criteria for packaging types. This is a missed 
opportunity as industry design for recycling guidelines (1) have been widely developed and consolidated, in the 
case of flexible packaging, by aligning the various stake holders of the EU flexible packaging value chain  (e.g. 
CEFLEX, 4EG design guidelines) and (2) will support EU policy makers in the swift and timely adoption of 
delegated acts setting out EU design criteria by the dead line of 2030. EU design-for-recycling criteria should be 
specific to the packaging category, based on publicly available evidence and testing, and cover specific packaging 
components and constituents.  
 
3 
 
We encourage the European Commission to revert to a hybrid process, whereby industry guidelines or 
harmonised EU standards on packaging design are approved via the delegated acts. Where such industry DfR 
guidelines or CEN/CENELEC standards do not exist, these  should be developed by the European Commission, 
together with the support of industry experts in a transparent manner, for example, a Packaging Forum, ahead 
of 2030.   
Policy ask: Amend Article 6(4) to include a deadline of December 31, 2025, for the adoption of the delegated 
acts by the European Commission establishing design for recycling criteria for the packaging types, categories 
and materials in Annex II. EU design for recycling criteria developed by the European Commission should cover 
all technicalities referring to the packaging type assessed. In this respect, there is no need for an additional 
negative list or description of design for recycling parameters in the Annexes of the PPWR.  
b. Recyclability performance grades  
Considering that the methodology for calculating the recyclability of packaging is to be determined via secondary 
legislation adopted after the entry into force of the PPWR, we advise the EU co -legislators to simplify the 
recyclability performance grades in Annex II Table 2. The gradin g A – E should be linked with the compatibility 
(from highest – lowers) of the packaging with the design for recycling criteria developed by the European 
Commission for the packaging type under review. This would also allow a clearer eco-modulation EPR approach. 
Policy ask: Grades A – E should be linked to the compatibility of the packaging type with the design -for-
recycling EU criteria (e.g. A = highest compatibility, B = high – medium compatibility, C = medium 
compatibility, D = medium – low compatibility, E = lowest / no compatibility).   
c. EPR eco-modulation based on recyclability only 
To enable packaging producers to bear financial responsibility for the sorting, collection, and recycling of the 
packaging they place on the market in accordance with its design, EPR fees should be modulated on recyclability 
only.  Based on a mature EU market for secondary plastic raw materials or bio -based plastics, eco -modulation 
criteria should be revisited to incentivise the uptake of recycled plastics or plastics from alternative sources with 
reduced greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions . To reward already in 2030 recycled plastic content or bio -plastics 
would lead to a race to the bottom where early contractors would be rewarded for securing and using the 
“lower-fee feedstock” in a scarce market.   
Concerning the EPR eco-modulation for newly classified packaging items, such a single-serve beverage capsules 
units (e.g., coffee / tea capsules) we recommend that eco -modulation is based on the weight of the packaging 
empty.  
Policy ask: EPR fees should be eco-modulated based on recyclability only.  
 
Recycled content targets for plastic packaging (Article 7)  
a. Levels of the minimum targets  
We believe the proposal strikes the right balance between the ambition of incentivising plastic packaging to 
include recycled content  and what is  technically feasible in the designated timeframe . Stronger collection 
systems for flexible packaging, additional sorting (e.g. out of the mixed waste stream) , a phase -out of the 
landfilling o f recyclable packaging, and a recognition of the fuel -exempt mass balance method to support a 
technological-neutral target-setting – are all much needed pre-requisites to accompany the targets.  
Contact-sensitive flexible packaging, such as packaging for food, cosmetics, can only include recycled plastic 
content from post -consumer plastic waste through chemical recycling technologies  accounted through fuel -
exempt mass balance , which can offer the material  at the same high -quality required by those types of 
packaging.  
Finally, the 2040 recycled content targets should be set as aspirational, as concluded by the European 
Commission in its official impact assessment report, or at a later stage based on the lev el of maturity of the 
recyclates market.  
 
4 
 
b. Scope of the recycled content targets for plastic packaging  
Recycled content should be imposed only on packaging predominantly made of plastic (over 50% per weight), 
which would enable the appropriate sorting, collection and retention of the plastic material for future use in 
packaging applications. The target should be addressed to producers of packaged goods and set as an average 
of all plastic packaging they place on the market, instead of applied each individual unit of packaging, like PPWR 
proposal suggests. This would allow more flexibility for packaging producers to allocate recycled content based 
on available supply of recycled material and technical feasibility and offer them a realistic chance of being able 
to meet the target.  
An approach of PPWR Article 7 paragraphs 1 and 2, which imposes recycled content targets on the plastic part 
of any type of packaging as of 2030 would create a disproportionate burden for packaging made predominantly 
of other materials and which contain a plastic lining or coating  to strengthen the functionality of the packaging 
(e.g., decontamination/oxidation purposes, transfer protection) . The technical efforts to reach such targets in 
those thin plastic parts would be disproportionate compared to  the benefits for circularity. These very thin 
components are typically not recovered during the recycling process of the main (packaging) material, meaning 
that requiring them to contain recycled mat erial would eventually see that material lost. We recommend that 
plastic recycled content is integrated into plastic packaging as the most efficient way to keep it circular.  
Finally, in situations of market scarcity of recycled plastics needed to comply with the 2030 recycled content 
targets for plastic packaging, the European Commission should reduce the targets much earlier than suggested 
in the PPWR, for example 2027 as the latest, instead of 2028.  
Policy ask: Focus the scope of Article 7(1) on plastic packaging and as an average on the portfolio placed on 
the market by producers.   
Policy ask: Remove the mandate of the 2040 recycled content targets for plastic packaging, instead ensuring 
they are either set as aspirational or at a later stage based on robust data and a mature EU market for 
secondary raw materials.  
 
Compostable packaging (Article 8)  
a. Material neutrality  
As the main EU policy instrument regulating packaging as a product for 2030, the PPWR should remain material-
neutral in its approach and prescription of obligations for packaging types.  
According to Article 8(1) PPWR, coffee capsules must, expected as early as 2026, be compostable in bio -waste 
treatment facilities. The market for coffee capsules  is made from plastic (predominantly PP), aluminium, and 
only very few compostable coffee capsule options. Over 50% of today’s coffee capsules are made of aluminium, 
which are recyclable . Mandating all coffee capsules, including aluminium ones, for industr ial bio -waste 
compostability is a highly unsuitable measure leading to unintended environmental outcomes with a market 
intervention void of environmental, economic, or social impacts.  
Compostability should not be an aim in itself , only one avenue through which packaging circularity can be 
achieved. This is because:  
1. The PPWR should lend itself to respect the EU’s waste hierarchy, allowing the placing on the market of 
recyclable coffee capsules   
2. The EU bio -waste treatment infrastructure landscape does no t accept compostable p ackaging, most 
often because these are seen as contaminants.  
3. Market interventions should be based on a robust impact assessment  - the PPWR impact assessment 
report does not look into  the impacts of this measure for aluminium coffee capsules, only made from 
plastic  
The PPWR cites recycling as a critical enabler of circularity by making it a minimum criterion for placing packaging 
on the market. Beverage capsules which demonstrably lend themselves to recycling are already advanced in this 
journey, only to be cut short by a shift to mandatory composting. This move finds no justification in the waste

hierarchy, undermining the functional approach to packaging with no evidence of better en vironmental 
performance of compostability over recycling as a general rule. 
Producers should be able to choose which material delivers best environmental results while fulfilling consumer 
expectations. Stronger enforcement of updated European harmonised st andards for packaging recoverable 
through composting and anaerobic digestion and clear labelling instructions can help scale up compostable 
solutions while minimizing contamination of the bio-waste. 
a. Insufficient EU composting infrastructure and continued consumer confusion  
There is a lack of biological waste treatment capacity in Europe , as in most EU countries compostable plastic 
items are not accepted in the organic waste stream .1 The capacity of the installed bio -waste treatment 
infrastructure varies greatly,2 further advancing a patchwork of inconsistent policies of Member States towards 
compostable products . Before any products are mandated to be compostable, Member States should be 
encouraged to develop their bio-waste treatment infrastructures to process compostable items.  
Many plastics today are designed to decompose under controlled conditions in industrial bio -waste treatment 
plants but do not decompose through home composting in people’s gardens. Confusion also remains at the level 
of EU citizens as to what compostability actually means, as many perceive it to entail “home composting”.  
A recent consumer study 3 shows that more than 60% of compostable plastics do not fully biodegrade in home 
compost bins , leading to a general lack of consumer confusion around compostable/biodegradable labels or 
appropriate disposal practices. Most consumers understand compostabil ity as “home compostability” and not 
as industrial compostability. EU regulators should first tackle the issue of claims and disposal labels arising from 
compostable packaging, allow sufficient time to EU consumers improve their waste disposal practices, w hile in 
the meantime ensuring the strengthening of the EU’s biowaste treatment infrastructure.  
As long as they have a proper disposal by consumers and end-of-life scenario which does not lead to more GHG 
emissions, compostable packaging options should be allowed to co-exist with conventional ones.  
Until the targets of separate collection of bio -waste at source (as mandated by the WFD) are achieved at the 
end of 2023 by all Member State s, the EU should refrain from a prescriptive market intervention which w ould 
jeopardise the free movement of products across the EU.  
b. Aluminium coffee capsules should not be mandated for compostability, as they are fully recyclable  
Coffee capsules are recyclable in the aluminium stream , while coffee grounds are recovered. Through 
established collection points across the world, aluminium coffee capsules have been collected, recycled and 
revalued for 25 years. The typical recycling process sees the collected aluminium capsules sent to the recycling 
facility, shredded to separate the coffee grounds from the aluminium capsule and transform coffee grounds into 
green energy or nutrient-rich compost. Finally, empty capsules  are processed to recycle the aluminium further  
into new capsules of the same high-quality aluminium needed, or in other products.4 
We welcome that the PPWR proposal qualifies coffee capsules as packaging, therefore allowing their collection 
through municipal waste management systems in a harmonised manner across the EU.  Many Member States 
have already taken steps in this regard, such as Germany and Austria, and as of 1 January 2023, Belgium as well.   
c. Compostability should be one pathway to circularity, not the only pathway  
Compostability in itself, so as to say manufacturing packaging with the sole purpose of seeing its decomposition 
in industrial facilities in conditions of heat, humidity and oxygen, should not prevail over well-designed packaging

d. Impact of mandating capsules to be industrially compostable not assessed by EU regulators  
Mandating coffee capsules for compostability, which is a measure carried out without any data gathering and 
adequate impact, would lead to unintended consequences, for example switching from one material to another 
-  a market that the capsules market is unable to withstand. For example, all the global production of aluminium 
capsules is taking place in Europe.  The European Commission’s impact assessment underpinning the PPWR 
proposal does not consider aluminium capsules, and neither does the additional study carried out by Eunomia 
and COWI for the European Commission6 in addition to the initial impact assessment – the former only addresses 
regular and lightweight plastic carrier bags and their compostability potential.  
Finally, as recent EU studies recommend, policy makers should first develop an assessment methodology to 
understand for which applications compostable materials should be used, based on an environmental, social 
and economic point of view.7 
We therefore call on EU policy makers to avoid any law -making that does not adequately consider all the 
environmental, economic, and social impacts of these measures, in line with the EU’s better regulation principle.  
Policy ask: remove the reference to Article 3(1)(g) from the scope of Article 8(1) and enable the correct 
labelling of compostable packaging to allow for correct consumer sorting and disposal.  
 
Reuse and refill targets (Article 10, Article 26)  
a. Industrial packaging (transport packaging)  
We acknowledge the crucial importance of curbing packaging waste and respecting the EU waste hierarchy set 
out in the Waste Framework Directive, which allows for reuse measures to slow packaging pollution. The case 
of industrial packaging, however, is diff erent. Industrial packaging waste is not collected via the same avenues 
and infrastructure as household waste, while it is already recycled today. Industrial packaging is foremost 
focused on ensuring the safe transportation and preservation of packaging be tween sites and economic 
operators and is largely already focused today on reusable options.  
A 100% mandate of reusable packaging between factories and economic operators will never be achieved, 
logically – as some packaging may still break or become damaged. Furthermore, some flexible packaging options,

b. Packaging refill options  
We notice that packaging refill options made available at distributor or retail level are not recognised as 
contributing to reuse or refill targets, as per the PPWR proposal. We consider this to be a great missed 
opportunity, especially as refill pouches have a lower environmental impact compared to the more conventional 
single-use packaging, and have a tremendo us potential of encouraging a robust reuse system, especially for 
home and personal care products (e.g., hand soap, shampoos, detergents).  
Policy ask: recognise refill options as contributing to the reuse targets of the PPWR.  
 
Restrictions on certain use of packaging (Article 22)  
The proposal offers little justification for proposing bans on single use packaging where it does not fully assess 
alternative solutions. No market bans should be proposed without a thorough impact assessment carried out by 
the EU’s executive for each of the products in scope, and without hygiene and safety considerations.  
In terms of the list of packaging formats and purposes listed in Annex V, the table should be carefully designed 
not to cause any m isunderstanding and clarify that the ‘illustrative examples’ are only restricted if used in the 
restricted use  (e.g. single-use plastic grouped packaging in collation films is mostly used to facilitate distribution, 
while the restricted use should at maximum be those grouped packages that do not have a distribution function, 
and are only used to incentivise consumption). 
Policy ask: Remove Article 22 and Annex V from the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation.  
 
Waste prevention targets (Article 38)  
We fully support the European Commission’s approach in determining the needed prevention targets for 
packaging waste, as well as the levels proposed for 2030, 2035, and 2040. We caution co -legislators not to 
impose additional targets for packaging waste preve ntion (e.g. targeting one material or additional packaging 
products) without a robust impact assessment and data.  
Policy ask: Remove Article 38 (3) on prevention of packaging waste, which allows Member State to achieve 
the PPWR targets by a variety of measures at their discretion.  
Policy ask : Avoid imposing additional waste prevention targets without first carrying out a robust impact 
assessment on the basis of updated market analysis and data.  
 
Compliance requirements (Articles 51, 52, 54) 
We support the intention of the PPWR to empower mem ber states to take action when they determine that 
packaging is not aligned with the requirements of the Regulation. We caution however that vague formulations 
in the text can have shortcomings with significant repercussions for the security of supply of the Internal Market. 
 
8 
 
Unclear compliance obligations are penalised through product recalls (Article 56), which is a disproportionate 
measure when there is no environmental impact associated with the lack of compliance. Instead, administrative 
warnings and fi nes are more sensible . Given that the PPWR has an environmental protection objective, 
compliance should assess environment protection. Health and safety protection are assessed already in specific 
EU legislation, which should not prompt the PPWR to duplicate regulation. 
Finally, compliance obligations should be divided along the actors operating within the supply chain, since often 
many responsibilities of packaging conformity fall onto the packaging manufacturer and not the supplier. 
Packaging that is sensitive (fo od, feed, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices) should be tacked via 
documentation already covered by existing EU legislation for those types of sensitive packaging, and not under 
the PPWR – an outcome proposed by the PPWR, which burdens an already existing and well-functioning system.  
Article 51 (Packaging databases) 
The packaging databases envisaged by the PPWR are to include information on the toxicity or danger of 
packaging materials and components used for their manufacturing. Not only does the PPWR approach a 
dangerous ideological precedent, that of assuming that today’s packaging – especially most sensitive, such as 
that for medicines, medical devices, food or baby packaging – would contain toxic or dangerous materials or 
components, but also that end -user safety is not r egulated in the European Union. However, this is already 
provided for in established EU legislation: REACH, CLP Regulation, Food Contact Materials Regulations and many 
others. We call for the removal of the entry referring to the packaging databases of entry (b) in Article 51 (2).  
Article 52 (Procedure for dealing with packaging presenting a risk at national level) 
Enabling member states to take action regionally or nationally only when they determine whether packaging 
complies with requirements outside this Regulation. Chemical and food safety, as well as human health are all 
aspects protected and regulated under existing legislation. National authorities should only be empowered to 
assess compliance of packaging with the requirements imposed by this Regulation. The proposed will only lead 
to a lack of harmonisation, running counter to the objectives of this measure. Furthermore, Article 52 does not 
foresee the right for companies whose packaging is und er assessment to challenge the conclusions drawn by 
the authorities.  
Article 54 (Compliant packaging which presents a risk) 
The PPWR implies that even if packaging respects today’s EU legislation on product, food, chemical safety, and 
its own requirements , it can never be truly compliant. The assumption sets a concerning precedent with far -
reaching and potentially unintended consequences throughout the Union market. We call that Article 54 enables 
national authorities to carry out their assessments of packaging only on the basis of the PPWR, without infringing 
on the competences established through other EU law.

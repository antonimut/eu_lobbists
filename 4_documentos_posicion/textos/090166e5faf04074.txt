New Packaging Regulation 
Environmental Action Germany (DUH) calls for more 
ambitious waste prevention and higher reuse targets 
About DUH  
Environmental Action Germany (Deutsche Umwelthilfe, DUH), founded in 1975, is a recognised environ-
mental and consumer protection association  that campaigns mainly at national and EU level. DUH is a 
politically independent, non-profit organisation, entitled to take legal action. In the area of circular econ-
omy, DUH has been campaigning for waste prevention, circular product design, responsible consumption 
and a sustainable economy. DUH is a member of the European Environmental Bureau (EEB) as well as the 
Break Free From Plastic movement (BFFP). 
Background 
At the end of November 2022, the European Commission put forward a proposal for a new Regulation on 
packaging and packaging waste (COM (2022) 677), which for the first time contains concrete waste pre-
vention as well as reuse targets for packaging.  
With annually over 177 kg per capita, packaging waste volumes in the EU are higher than ever. They have 
increased by 20 percent in the last decade - faster than economic growth. Plastics production – of which 
packaging accounts for 40 percent – is responsible for 8 and 9 percent of EU-wide oil and gas consumption, 
respectively,1 thus requiring more oil and gas than any other industry in the EU. To date, voluntary indus-
try initiatives have not resulted in savings of  virgin plastics, an increased market share of reus able pack-
aging, or improved product design. 2 Consequently, there is an urgent need for binding an d ambitious 
legislation to reduce packaging waste, strengthen reuse systems and ensure high-quality recycling. Well-
designed reuse systems for packaging offer significant environmental benefits: They save resources and 
energy, release fewer toxins and contribute to a reduction in climate-damaging greenhouse gases.3 
General evaluation of the Commission proposal 
Environmental Action Germany (DUH) welcomes the revision proposal for the Directive on packaging and 
packaging waste (94/62/EC), which has been in force since 1994, and its conversion into a Regulation, as 
an important step towards true and sustainable change in the packaging industry. DUH appreciates that, 
for the first time, binding requirements for waste prevention  and the introduction of e nvironmentally 
friendly reuse systems have been proposed. However, DUH  considers them too weak to solve the enor-
mous packaging waste problem in Europe. It is also regrettable that the proposed requirements for pack-

The conversion of the previous directive into a regulation (Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation - 
PPWR) which is direc tly applicable in all Member States is to be seen as positive. This will ensure rapid 
implementation and comparably binding force for market players (producers, manufacturers, distributors 
and importers). However, from our point of view it is essential that the Member States are empowered 
to also go beyond EU targets. In Germany, a reuse target of 70 percent for beverage packaging has been 
in force since 2019. This must not be weakened under any circumstances by the new EU requirements. It 
is therefore important that the regulation is not only based on Article 114 TFEU, which ensures the func-
tionality of the internal market, but also on Article 191 with the aim of protecting the environment. 
Overall, too many regulatory details – especially in the respective articles on recycling and on reuse targets 
– are outsourced to secondary legislation like  delegated and implementing acts. Firstly, this creates in-
vestment uncertainties for businesses, as further time delays as well as exemptions may occur. Secondly, 
the process of implementing /delegated acts is not sufficiently transparent and involves too few s take-
holders. Therefore, at least the delegated acts on design for recycling and on  the calculation of reuse 
targets should be adopted earlier. 
Waste prevention targets (Art. 38) 
» For years, DUH has been calling for concrete waste prevention targets at the EU level. Therefore, we 
regard the first-time formulation of these as an important regulatory milestone. However, in order to 
reverse the upward trend in packaging waste of the past decade not only in the long term, but as 
soon as possible, the targets must be considerably more ambitious and must apply at a n earlier 
stage. With the Commission's current targets, a reduction of per capita packaging waste to a level 
similar to 2009 (150 kg of packaging waste per capita) will only be possible by 2040. DUH calls on EU 
legislators to take countermeasures earlier  in order to achieve a reduction to 150 kg of packaging 
waste per capita already in 2030. This requires the following per capita reduction targets: 
12 percent until 2027 
15 percent until 2030 
30 percent until 2035 
50 percent until 2040 
» Furthermore, annual per capita packaging waste should be limited to a maximum of  150 kg by 2030 
and 90 kg by 2040. This measure shall ensure that those Member States that generate the most pack-
aging waste also have to make correspondingly greater waste prevention efforts.  
» In order to prevent shifts from heavier to lighter packaging materials, additional material-specific sub-
targets should be set. These should apply to packaging made of plastics, composite materials and 
metal. 
Reuse (Art. 26) & refill (Art.25/26) of packaging 
» The introduction of mandatory reuse targets is progressive and an imperative signal for the packaging 
industry. However, the reuse targets must be increased significantly. In particular,  the reuse targets 
for non -alcoholic and alcoholic beverages (except wine and spirits) are far too low (25 percent by 
2040) and were weakened by 50 percent at the last minute before the publication of the proposal. In 
Germany, reuse targets of over 40 percent have been achieved for decades. To ensure that all Member 
States are on track by 2030 and 2040, interim targets and asse ssments are needed, s tarting with an 
initial phase-in target for 2027, followed by further interim targets in 2033 and 2036.

» It is also a matter of concern that the reuse targets for beverage and takeaway packaging can also 
be achieved via refill, i.e. through refilling consumer-owned containers taken to restaurants or ca-
fés, for example . While 'refill' can contribute to waste prevention, the refilling of e.g. consumer -
owned containers is not the same as establishing managed reuse systems for packaging. Mixing these 
two regulatory measures creates loopholes and encourages greenwashing – not least because refill is 
difficult to measure in comparison to reuse and, for example, the conversion of common electronic 
point of sale  (EPOS) systems in gastronomy  would involve a great  deal of additional bureaucracy. 
Therefore, it is not sensible  to include refill in reuse targets  from a practical point of view alone . In 
order to promote refill across the board, Article 25 of the Regulation should oblige HoReCa businesses 
and food retailers to allow their customers to refill their own containers, as Section 34 (1) of the Ger-
man Packaging Act does. Requirements for this, e.g. in terms of hygiene, can be defined in a delegated 
act or in guidelines. In Germany, there are already hygiene guidelines for refill , e.g. from the Food 
Federation Germany ( Lebensmittelverband). Moreover, it must be prohibited to offer disposable 
packaging for sale at refill stations and fresh food counters.  This is the only way to ensure  that, for 
example, lettuce offered for refill at a  salad bar, is not ultimately packaged in  disposable packaging. 
All in all, it should only be possibl e to achieve the reuse targets specified in the draft regulation by 
means of reu se systems for packaging described in Annex VI Part A, and not by refilling consumer-
owned containers or even disposable containers offered at the point of sale. 
» DUH also calls for the extension of t he proposed r euse targets to further  packaging streams. Food 
packaging in retail as well as cleaning products and cosmetics packaging harbour untapped potential. 
For dairy products, such as yogurt or cream, standardised reusable jars exist in Germany and their 
market share still has a very large growth potential . It is furthermore incomprehensible why milk 
should remain excluded from the targets for non-alcoholic beverages4:

» The exemption for cardboard boxes from the mandate for transport packaging to be reusable (Para. 
12 + Para. 13) for  shipments within a company or Member S tate of the EU must be  deleted/re-
moved, since it completely undermines the mandate. If this exemption remains in the Regulation, a 
switch to reusable solutions will fail to materiali ze. Disposable cardboard boxes are far from being 
ecologically on a par with reusable transport packaging. 
» Overall, an explicit enabling clause in Art. 26 on reuse targets and Art. 44 on deposit and return sys-
tems should enable Member States to adopt measures that go beyond the scope of the Regulation. 
Deposit and return systems (Art. 44) 
» DUH welcomes the obligation to establish deposit and return systems (DRS) for single-use plastic bot-
tles and beverage cans. This will drastically reduce littering. So far, however, according to Art. 44 (6), 
it remains voluntary to enable the return of reusable packaging via reverse vending machines installed 
for the take-back of single-use beverage packaging. In order to leverage synergy effects and save costs, 
it should be mandatory for reverse vending  machines to be designed to accept reusable containers 
from the outset. In addition, DRS should also be mandatory for single-use glass bottles and beverage 
cartons. 
» The option for Member States to bypass DRS by means of exemption regulations, provided that they 
achieve a collection rate of at least 90 percent, should be removed, as this can lead to delays regarding 
the implementation of truly effective systems. 
Restrictions on use of certain packaging formats (Art. 22 & Annex V) 
» DUH is in favour of restricting the use of unnecessary packaging types. However, the current proposal 
still allows for too many exceptions and thus weakens the great potential to effectively reduce these 
packaging types. If it remains possible to p ackage fruits and vegetables provided there is a risk of 
dehydration and turgidity loss, cucumbers will remain shrink-wrapped. If single-use packaging for on-
site consumption of beverages and food is banned in HoReCa, but exemptions for micro-businesses 
are possible, a large part of the industry is likely to be exempt from the restrictions: In Germany,  for 
example a mandate to offer reusable packaging in the gastronomy industry has been in force since 
January 2023. However, businesses with up to five empl oyees and a sales area of 80 square meters 
are exempted from this obligation. This accounts for as much as 60 percent of gastronomy businesses. 
France, on the other hand,  has been effectively demonstrating since January 2023 that disposable 
tableware and packaging for on -site consumption in restaurants and cafés can be replaced by envi-
ronmentally friendly reusable options. It is therefore not comprehensible why the EU should wait 
until 2030 to introduce such a single-use ban. DUH is in favor of an immediate ban with no exemp-
tions. 
Recycling (Art. 6/46) 
» The requirements for the so -called 'design for recycling' and 'recycling at scale' are not sufficiently 
clear from the draft of the PPWR, as the specifics are outsourced to delegated acts. However, the 
Commission envisages that the 'design for recycling' criteria will be structured differently for different 
packaging categories. Minimum criteria for recyclability should, in any case, apply equally to all pack-
aging materials, similar to the ‘minimum standard’ laid down in Section 21 (3) of the German Packag-
ing Act. In addition, the 'design for recycling' criteria should already apply from 2025, so that non -
recyclable or poorly recyclable packaging disappears from the market more quickly. 
» In addition, DUH c alls for the integration of a negative list with criteria that significantly impede the 
recycling of packaging. Such a list was already included in the leak of the draft Regulation. In this way,

the exclusion of packaging that contradicts the recycling concept in itself can have a quick and simple 
effect. A negative list should contain the following elements in particular: 
1. Non-detectability of packaging with the established NIR sorting technology. 
2. Multilayer packaging made of more than one polymer and/or different material types (e.g. plas-

and/or barriers that cannot be separated in established processes) 
3. Full-surface sleeves as well as permanent labels with adhesives that are difficult to dissolve, e.g.

» Furthermore, the option for 'innovative packa ging' (Art. 6[9]) to comply neither with the 'design for 
recycling' nor with the 'recycling at scale' criteria for up to five years after being placed on the market 
offers a gateway to circumventing the recycling targets set. From DUH's point of view, these exemp-
tions for 'innovative' packaging have to be removed. The focus should not lie on the development of 
new supposedly environmentally friendly materials that are not recyclable . Rather, recycling should 
be considered from the beginning. 
» Regarding the recycling targets (Article 46), there should be no exemption for the achievement of the 
material-specific targets set out. The se targets have not been increased compared to the recycling 
targets from the currently effective "Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive". The fact that the ex-
isting targets have not yet been met cannot be reconciled with a further delay of five years in achieving 
the material-specific targets. The focus must now be on enforcing the recycling provisions that have 
been in effect. In addition, the targets for 2025 and 2030 must be supplemented by long -term tar-
gets for 2040. This is necessary to enhance the binding character of the legislation  and increase the 
availability of recycled materials on the market for ambitious long-term recycled content targets. 
Minimum recycled content in plastic packaging (Art. 7) 
» The integration of recycled content targets in Article 7 is an important step towards greater resource 
independence in the EU.  DUH welcomes the fact  that the targets are to be met exclusively through 
the use of post-consumer waste and are to apply per packaging unit. It is important to ensure that the 
targets are achieved through mechanical recycling. The chemical recycling processes of pyrolysis and 
gasification are energy-intensive, involve high material losses and pollutant loads, and are therefore 
not suitable from an ecological point of view for contributing to the attainment of recycled content 
targets. Also, in the context of chemical recycling, the re is often no  doubt-free traceability between 
recyclate and processed waste. Appropriate environmental criteria should therefore apply to recycled 
content targets, which prevent the inclusion of recyclates from the pyrolysis or gasification of packag-
ing. 
» So far, the proposal does not differentiate between single-use and reusable packaging with regards to 
the recycled content targets. However, reusable packaging has a significantly longer product life. A 
plastic beverage crate, for example, is in use for up to 20 years. DUH proposes creating longer transi-
tion periods of at least five years for reusable packaging and to exempt reusable packaging that has 
already been put on the market from the recycled content requirements. Like this, remaining stocks 
can continue to circulate in the system without – in the worst case – having to be recalled prematurely 
in order for recycled content requirements to be met.  
» Furthermore, the recycled content targets proposed by the EU Commission are limited to packaging 
made of plastics. However, recycled content targets should also apply to other material types, at 
least including glass, cardboard and paper packaging.  This is also important to avoid displacement 
effects.

» It is a matter of concern that compostable packaging is excluded from the recycled content targets. 
The composting process itself must not be understood as a form of recycling. Packaging made of bio-
degradable bioplastics usually degrades only insufficiently or not at all in industrial composting plants. 
It furthermore causes impurities in the compost product . Consequently, packaging made of biode-
gradable plastics is not permitted for disposal in the organic waste stream in Germany. The compost-
ing of biodegradable plastics usually has no added value for the compost produced (e.g. through plant 
nutrients or substrate build-up). Like this, the material that has been produced at great environmental 
expense is thus lost unused. Therefore, there should be no incentives whatsoever to dispose of pack-
aging made of bioplastics via the biowaste stream. Furthermore, also for degradable bioplastics, i t 
would be much more ecologically advantageous to recycle them and thus keep the material in the 
loop for as long as possible. In addition, the production of plant-based plastics is associated with sig-
nificant environmental impacts, such as soil contamination, eutrophication and land use. From DUH's 
point of view, all packaging made of bioplastics must achieve 'recycling at sc ale' via mechanical recy-
cling just like other packaging, as well as meeting the recycled content targets. 
Plastic carrier bags (Art. 29) 
» The regulatory scope for plastic bags needs to be expanded urgently. Experience from Germany shows 
that retail chains are circumventing the current ban on lightweight plastic bags with a wall thickness 
of 15 - 50 micrometers by offering plastic bags for sale that are only a few micrometers thicker but 
still of a disposable nature. DUH is therefore calling on EU legislators to widen the scope of the provi-
sion and enable Member States to ban 'very light' plastic bags of a wall thickness of up to 15 microm-
eters and plastic bags with a wall thickness of 50 to 120 micrometers as well.

German Reuse Alliance calls for a strong position on

About the Reuse Alliance & general evaluation of the proposal 
The Reuse Alliance (Mehrweg-Allianz) is a coalition of Deutsche Umwelthilfe e.V. (DUH), the Association 
of German Beverage Wholesalers (BV GFGH), Private Breweries in Germany (Private Brauereien Deutsch-
land), the Association of German Retail of Beverages (VDGE), Foundation for Reuse (SIM) and the Initiative 
for the promotion of reusable packaging ( Pro Mehrweg). The Alliance, together with the Cooperative of 
the German bottled water companies ( Genossenschaft Deutscher Brunnen ), unifies decades of experi-
ence in operating the world wide biggest reuse system for beverages in Germany.  
The Reuse Alliance assesses the draft of the packaging and packaging waste regulation (PPWR)  as an im-
portant step towards reducing unnecessary packaging waste. The Alliance appreciates that binding reuse 
quotas are set at EU level for the first time  which gives a strong signal to the packaging  and beverage 
industry that well -managed reuse systems allow for maximised environmental and economic benefits.  
However, we want to point out that the current draft falls short in terms of level of ambition especially 
for the reuse quotas for beverage packaging . Moreover, we want to raise our concern that existing and 
well-functioning reuse systems must not be disregarded or even jeopa rdised when setting regulations 
that affect these systems. 
Comments on certain provisions of the Draft Regulation 
First and foremost, it is of utmost importance that the German translation of the proposed regulation 
uses the common and therefore correct terminology of re-use as opposed to single-use. The German term 
‘Mehrweg’ is replaced throu ghout the translation by th e paraphrase ‘wiederverwendbare Verpackung’. 
This creates a softening of the t erm at its root, making re-use not a matter fact but a matter of interpre-
tation. Against the ba ckground that the term ‘Mehrweg’ is a long-established commitment of quality in 
Germany, the softening of that term  would represent an unacceptable  step backwards for the reuse  in-
dustry – not least because some of the signatories entail ‘Mehrweg’ in their names. 
Definition of re-use 
We welcome that the EU-Commission has defined ‘systems for re-use’ in Article 3, paragraph 26, meaning 
the organisational, technical /or financial arrangements, which  enable the re-use either in a closed loop 
or open loop system. At the moment, however, we not only see inconsistencies between the English and 
German versions, but also miss the particularly important incentive to return reusable packaging in Art.

3 as we do not consider the reference in Annex VI part A to be sufficient. Incentives for reuse are essential 
to ensure the efficiency of systems and avoid “pseudo” reusable packaging. 
In this way, we strongly support the d efinition set in the German Packaging Act : "Reusable packaging is  
packaging that is intended to be reused several times for the same purpose after use  and whose actual 
return and reuse is made possible by adequate logistics and promoted by suitable incentive systems, usu-
ally by a deposit."  
Additionally, we propose to set an average number of minimum rotations for reusable packaging in Article 
10 b). If an economic operator has to set up systems to guarantee at least 15 rotations, operators want 
to save even more costs by making their packaging as durable and thus economical as possible.  Further-
more, setting a minimum rotation rate prevents the risk of g reenwashing. According to a comparison of 
32 life cycle assessment  studies, 10-15 rotations for all packaging materials already brings the environ-
mental benefit wanted compared to single-use packaging1. 
Moreover, the terminology of open and closed systems is confusing as the terms from recycling are trans-
ferred to reuse systems. The correct terms widely used in the reuse packaging industry are pool systems 
and individual systems. 
Labelling of packaging 
The requirements to tag packaging with a label as well as a QR code or other form of digital data carrier 
set out in Art. 11(4) do not sufficiently take existing reuse systems into account and may therefore cause 
major problems for reusable beverage bottles that are  already circulating in functioning reuse  systems. 
The indelible application of a label  and a QR code/digital data carrier on a beverage bottle cannot be 
transferred into practice as the bottle labels are washed off before refilling . Easily removable labels is 
an important requirement for pool bottles to be reused by several economic operators as only the label 
indicates the filler’s brand. This is why the signatories demand an exemption for reusable bottles that are 
already circulating on the market. Otherwise this requirement can lead to high additional costs that make 
reuse systems less competitive to single -use packaging and , in the worst case, lead to reusable bottles 
being taken off the market prematurely and having to be recycled earlier than necessary. 
Reuse targets 
The introduction of mandatory reuse quotas is progressive and provides investment security for the pack-
aging industry. However, the reuse quotas in Article 26 must be increased significantly. In particular, the 
reuse quotas for beverage bottles for non-alcoholic and alcoholic beverages (except wine and spirits) are 
far too low at 25 percent for 2040. In Germany, the Packaging Act sets a target quota of 70 percent for 
reusable beverage packaging, while the beer sector exceeds the quota with more than 80 percent reuse 
and quotas of over 40 percent have been achieved  in the overall sector  for decades. The fact that the 
draft PPWR is based on Article 114 TFEU on the functioning of the EU's internal  market, in conjunction 
with the currently unambitious formulation of the reuse quotas in Article 26 of the draft, must under no 
circumstances lead to Germany's ambitious quotas having to be lowered. This could be ensured by explic-
itly allowing Member States to set more ambitious quotas through an opening clause in Article 26. 
Furthermore, it must be guaranteed that the general requirements for systems for reuse in Annex VI, 
particularly the requirements regarding a governance structure for reuse systems , do not threaten al-
ready existing reuse systems by allowing derogations if they already exceed the proposed reuse quotas 
and if an exceptional economic burden can be proven. Germany has had well-functioning reuse systems

Recycled content 
Reusable packaging should not suffer any disadvantages with regard to the recycled content targets  in 
Article 7. So far, the proposal does not differentiate between single-use and reusable packaging. However, 
reusable packaging has a significant longer lifespan. Beverage crates, for example, circulate on the market 
for up to 20 yea rs. Crates that have already been put on the market would have to be take n out of the 
system prematurely and sent for recycling, if they do not (yet) meet the corresponding quotas for 2030. 
In order to avoid this, a transition period for reusable packaging of at least 5 years needs to be integrated 
in the proposal. Alternatively, the targets should only apply to reusable packaging that is made available 
on the market for the first time. 
Deposit and return systems 
The reuse alliance welcomes the obligation to establish deposit and return systems (DRS) for single -use 
plastic bottles and beverage cans. This will drastically reduce littering. So far, however, according to Art. 
44 (6), it remains voluntary to enable the return of reusable packaging via reverse vending machines in-
stalled for the take-back of single-use beverage packaging. In order to leverage synergy effects and save 
costs, it should be mandatory for reverse vending machines to be designed to accept reusable contain-
ers from the outset.

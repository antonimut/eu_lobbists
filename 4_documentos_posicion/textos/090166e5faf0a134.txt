Comments on the new proposed regulation for Packaging and Packaging Waste in the EU 
 
Dear Madam or Sir, 
 
We welcome the opportunity to provide input on the EU’s draft regulation for packaging and packaging 
waste (P&PWR). The Food Packaging Forum (FPF) is a charitable, science-based organization at the 
science policy interface. FPF is dedicated to raising awareness for hazardous chemicals in and 
environmental impacts caused by all types of food contact materials and articles (FCMs), including food 
packaging. Our work enables science-based decision making in the interest of protecting public health 
and the environment. As our expertise is mainly on chemicals in food packaging, we focus our remarks on 
this aspect. 
 
1. Context and general remarks 
 
With its Farm to Fork Strategy (F2F), the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability (CSS), and the Circular 
Economy Action Plan (CEAP) the EU has taken a much-needed stand on food packaging in general, and 
on hazardous chemicals that are used to make FCMs and that migrate from finished FCMs in particular. 
 
The proposed P&PWR is an important opportunity for implementing the EU’s ambitious goals as laid out 
in its F2F, CSS and CEAP regarding the reduction of adverse environmental and human health impacts 
from packaging. Food packaging is particularly important as it is highly prevalent (making up around 30% 
of overall household waste in the EU (according to Eurostat), and it strongly contributes to plastic littering 
(Morales-Caselles et al. 2021). The revision of the P&PWR is therefore an important opportunity for 
effective measures on problematic food packaging. Accordingly, the P&PWR proposal sets ambitious 
targets for reuse and recycling. 
 
But food packaging is also of very high concern when it comes to the migration of chemicals.  Indeed, our 
own work through the FCCmigex Database has shown that more than 1800 chemicals migrate from 
FCMs, and at least 388 of the chemicals used to manufacture FCMs and/or migrate from FCMs are 
hazardous according to the CSS (Zimmermann et al. 2022). Therefore, the presence of hazardous

chemicals in food packaging should be prevented, as mandated both by the CSS and the F2F, to enable 
safe and sustainable food packaging for an effective circular economy. Taking this important step is 
especially crucial in the context of the proposed P&PWR, as hazardous chemicals that continue to be 
used in the manufacture of food packaging will be perpetuated in reuse and recycling of said materials 
and products. As such, the presence of hazardous chemicals in food packaging is a barrier to 
enabling the circular economy for food packaging, and they will hinder the successful 
implementation of the P&PWR. 
 
Food packaging is regulated under EU 1935/2004 and delegated acts, and currently DG Sante is in the 
process of revising its FCM regulation. But due to delays in other dossiers (notably, REACH) a new 
proposal is not expected before 2024. What is more, DG Sante has indicated its intention to address 
sustainability requirements for food packaging in a revised FCM regulation as mandated by F2F, CSS and 
CEAP, but as this is a new objective that is out of scope of its current activities, DG Sante has stated that 
it has “no experience” on this specific matter (see slide 8 in the DG SANTE working group meeting 
presentation from December 15, 2022). 
 
For these reasons, it is essential that the issue of hazardous chemicals is adequately addressed in 
the P&PRW, but the wording in the current text of the regulation does not achieve this. Waiting for 
the revision of the FCM regulation will mean that hazardous chemicals would only be removed years after 
the P&PWR enters into force. This situation presents a serious threat for achieving the EU’s ambitious 
and necessary waste reduction targets by increasing safe reuse and recycling. It also implies that the 
EU’s entire population and environment continue to be exposed to hazardous chemicals from FCMs . This 
concern has already been expressed by the European Parliament in its own 2016 initiatives report where 
it is stated that current approaches do not sufficiently protect public health. 
 
What is more, the new regulation on recycled plastic FCMs issued in 2022 by DG Sante does not 
adequately address the issue of hazardous chemicals in recycled plastics, either (EU 2022/1616). We 
have provided extensive comments on this and expressed our concern about the lack of safety 
assessments of recycled plastics prior to their entry onto the market. It is important that the “Design-for-
recycling” criteria mentioned in the P&PWR will sufficiently tackle the issue of chemical safety. 
 
2. Compostable packaging  
 
Providing a clearly defined, short list of mandatorily compostable packaging: We welcome the aim 
to limit compostable packaging to applications where it is of clear benefit to the environment or human 
health and to lay down specific and common rules for the use of such packaging. As laid out in the 
proposal, it is crucial to avoid consumer confusion and facilitate the correct sorting and disposal of 
compostable packaging. However, symbols alone will hardly suffice to achieve that aim given the 
magnitude of information already provided on the packaging and that consumers sparsely read all labels. 
Therefore, besides symbols, further measures need to be taken. A short, clearly defined list of packaging

types (as already outlined in the proposal: filter coffee pods, sticky labels on fruits and vegetables, 
lightweight carrier bags) that are mandatorily compostable is one necessary step to avoid confusion not 
only amongst consumers but also other stakeholders in the value chain.  
 
Sufficiently addressing hazardous chemicals: The proposal states that “all compostable packaging 
constituting a food contact material is to meet the requirements set out in the Regulation (EC) No 
1935/2004.” However, all compostable packaging is by definition designed to enter the organic waste 
stream and become part of the open environment. All compostable packaging may be regarded as a 
material in contact with food. Therefore, to avoid exposure of humans and the environment to hazardous 
chemicals, all compostable plastics need to be inherently safe as defined under the CSS. The draft 
P&PWR text currently lacks a clear statement that compostable packaging needs to be free of hazardous 
chemicals to prevent negative impacts on human and environmental health (e.g., to be added in Annex 
3). 
 
Giving a time frame of decomposition: Chapter II Article 3 (and Annex 3) defines “compostable 
packaging” as packaging that should “ultimately decomposes (sic) into carbon dioxide, mineral salts, 
biomass, and water.” But this definition is insufficient as it does not specify a time limit for the 
decomposition of the packaging. To avoid the presence of packaging waste and/or its fragments in the 
finished compost, the time frame and degradation conditions (e.g., temperature, moisture) need to be 
clearly defined in the P&PWR text with a defined and appropriate time limit.  
 
Demonstrating safety and complete decomposition: Regarding the two previous points, standardized 
methods that allow assessing and demonstrating the safety of compostable packaging and its complete 
decomposition under industrially controlled conditions and in the environment should be defined. It needs 
to be the obligation of the compostable packaging manufacturer to provide information on complete 
decomposition (i.e., full mineralisation) and safety before the product is placed on the market.  
 
3. Defining INERTNESS as a material property that enables safe reuse 
 
Finally, materials that are most suitable for reuse (and recycling) are those that are inert, meaning that 
they almost do not interact chemically with the foods they are brought into contact with.  Inert materials 
have very low, negligible overall migration, and they also do not absorb chemical contaminants. Such 
materials are most suitable for reuse as they will not become contaminated by prior life stage residues 
(like food pigments and flavors, detergent chemicals, or other contaminants).  
 
For the P&PWR’s ambitious reuse targets to be implementable, it is essential that a definition of inertness 
is provided that is applicable to reusable food packaging. Importantly, the current Overall Migration Limit 
of 60 ppm as specified for plastic FCMs in EU 10/2011 cannot be considered as a suitable threshold for 
inertness, as it does not take health aspects into consideration. And while most FCMs on the market have 
Overall Migration well below the current threshold, there is a lack of robust analytical approaches with low

approaches are needed for both defining and measuring FCM inertness that are sufficiently protective of

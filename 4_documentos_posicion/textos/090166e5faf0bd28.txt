 
Eastman feedback to European Commission consultation on PPWR proposal 
April 2023 
 
Eastman welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the European Commission’s proposal for 
the revision of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). We fully support the effort of 
the Commission to harmonise the rules regarding packaging and packaging waste to avoid distortion 
of the Single Market and to accelerate the circular economy for plastic packaging.  
 
Eastman is very pleased with the ambitious ta rgets in the proposal that are in line with the waste 
hierarchy, namely reduce, reuse, and recycle (3R), in particular, the ambition for all packaging to be 
recyclable and the introduction of the recycled content targets. In situations where the 3R concept is 
not applicable, we believe that compostable or biodegradable plastics provide a significantly more 
sustainable solution than landfill or incineration. 
 
In order to achieve those targets and to ensure that materials on the market are used in the economy 
as long as possible (circular economy for materials), certain enabling conditions are necessary. Below 
we outline our proposal towards recycling technologies, recyclability criteria, scope and methodology 
for calculating the recycled content, as well as additionally emphasizing the necessity to implement 
the appropriate sorting technologies in the Member States. 
 
Recycling 
For the definition of recycling, the proposal refers to the Waste Framework Directive, which already 
defines what recycling is, namely waste to products, materials and substances. We fully support this 
definition and welcome the reference to high-quality recycling in the  PPWR proposal , which will 
ensure that valuable materials are kept in a circular loop. 
 
Only by the combined use of all recycling technologies - mechanical, physical, and chemical recycling 
the EU can make sure that:  
- the amount of plastic waste that ends up in landfills, that is incinerated or winds up in our 
environment is kept to the minimum,  
- the amount of waste for reprocessing into new products, materials or substances whether for the 
original purpose or other purposes is maximised, 
- the recycling targets can be met, and 
- that there is sufficient supply of recycled plastics/secondary raw materials. 
 
Recyclability 
Eastman fully supports the goal that all packaging placed on the market is recyclable by 2030. However, 
to achieve this, investments are needed to ensure the right infrastructure is in place  to collect, sort 
and recycle plastic waste. This requires creating appropriate conditions and especially ensuring legal 
certainty, predictability, and openness to innovate followed by timely investments and roll-out of that 
infrastructure. 
 
It is therefore essential that the “state-of-the-art” processes (as mentioned in Articles 3 (31  & 32) 
cover existing as well as developing innovative future technologies  to collect, sort and recycle. It 
should not hinder the scaling up of those technologies that are currently under development, or the 
development of new technologies  that could provide more efficient and sustainable solutions. As 
packaging evolves so will recycling and sorting technologies, as they will become more effective and 
optimized with better yields over time.

Therefore, when developing the design for recyclability criteria in a delegated act, the Commission 
should consider future proof criteria and cover emerging and  innovative technologies that will be 
available by 2030 to help making packaging recyclable. 
Example: 
Currently, new technologies are being developed  to aid sorting – e.g.  digital watermarking will help 
with more granular sorting of waste, something not possible with existing technologies, consequently 
increasing recycling of packaging waste.  The practical result of such new sorting technologies can be 
seen in the example of small cosmetic packaging (nail polish, lipstick, etc.) , which at present often is 
not sorted because the outdated technologies being used are unable to divert these articles to recycling 
streams. Therefore, they end up with mixed waste in landfills or incineration. With new and innovative 
sorting technologies, both hardware and digital identification solutions, which are being developed , 
these small pieces of packaging can be sorted and sent to recycling so a true circularity can be created, 
avoiding that packaging sizes need to be increased or valuable resources incinerated. 
 
Also, new recycling technologies are being developed to increase the quality of the recycled materials 
so true circularity can be created and the EU’s dependency on natural resources can be reduced 
significantly. 
 
Example: 
In 2022 Eastman initiated its investment in a molecular recycling facility ( chemical recycling via 
depolymerization) in France. Our recycling technology will have the capability to recycle almost any 
kind of polyester-rich waste (mixed hard -to-recycle plastic waste including polyester -rich textiles) an 
infinite number of times into high-quality (food grade) secondary raw materials, which will help to 
reduce the amount of primary raw materials used in products and to provide a solution to those 
materials that cannot mechanically be recycled and end up in incineration or landfill. 
 
Recycled content: Scope of plastic waste 
Plastic waste is not only generated by plastic packaging for consumer use. To tackle the plastic waste 
issue in the EU and to cut our dependency on natural resources, it is essential that all plastic waste is 
recycled and that we keep valuable materials in  a circular loop  and ensure a sufficient supply of 
secondary raw materials . There are advanced technologies , like the Eastman molecular recycling 
technology, that can recycle plastic waste coming from different waste streams to produce high-quality 
secondary materials. 
 
Therefore, plastic waste that counts towards recycled content  should include all plastic waste and 
not be limited to only post -consumer plastic waste and be aligned with the already existing 
definitions (e.g. ISO 14021). There is immense value in using post-industrial waste and pre-consumer 
waste as recycling feedstocks. From the 53.6 Mt of total plastic production in the EU on average only 
9.1 Mt enters recycling facilities of which 5,5 Mt is actually recovered and put back into production 1. 
Therefore, it is critical to recycle all plastic waste through advanced methods rather than see plastics 
landfilled or incinerated, or, worse, see them end up in our environment. 
 
Therefore, all plastic waste should be recy cled, and all plastic waste must contribute towards the 
recycled content targets to ensure a sufficient supply of secondary raw materials. 
 
Recycled content: Sorting technologies 
To guarantee a certain output of secondary raw materials it is essential to have continuously sufficient 
volumes of feedstock . Therefore, all plastic packaging should be collected without introducing too 
complex at-home sorting processes  that put an unnecessary burden on consumers and risk losing 
valuable material. Investments in enhanced sorting technologies  will help to sort all the collected

packaging appropriately. Moreover, to ensure that waste within Europe can be recycled in a 
sustainable and economically viable way, and to bring recent and newly developed technologies at 
scale, it is essential that Member States take a cooperative role when dealing with waste

Incorporating broader streams of plastic waste increases the efficiency an d productivity of recycling

Recycled content: Calculation methodology 
The Commission is currently developing a methodology to calculate and verify the recycled content in 
PET beverage bottles under Directive (EU) 2019/904 on reducing the impact of certain plastic products 
on the environment packaging (SUPD).  There are also additional recycled content t argets being 
discussed under different legislative proposals, including PPWR, for different sectors (textiles, 
automotive, construction, etc.) 
 
As the scale of recycling is key to the success of circular economy, the interoperability of the different 
targets and calculation methods  becomes a decisive factor. Because of that, w e encourage the 
Commission to develop a common calculation and verification methodology , which applies to all 
plastics recycled content targets set in for the different product categories. 
 
It is essential to provide legal certainty as soon as possible for investments in new recycling facilities 
or working on scaling up, as well as for the economic providers to ensure compliance with the targets 
set in the legislation. Waiting until the end of 2026 to get clarity on the uptake of recycled content in 
packaging is too late, the detailed rules are needed much sooner in order to bring results by 2030. 
 
Additionally, we call to adoption of  the mass balance chai n of custody for calculating recycled 
content. 
 
The chain of custody mass balance is essential for the transition to a circular economy more quickly, in 
an economically viable way and with less environmental impact. As a system allowing to track the 
recycled feedstocks as they flow through manufacturing processes , it will enable a fast and massive 
diversion of waste materials away from disposal (e.g. landfill and incineration) and into material 
recycling under the management of a chain of custody system that avoids fraud. 
 
This system allows allocating the recycled content to various products and ensures that the amount 
of recycled content allocated to the products is balanced  with the amount of recycled materials fed 
into the manufacturing process. All the virgin and molecularly recycled inputs are accounted for at the 
beginning of the process to ensure the correct percentage of recycled material is designated to each 
batch of products at the completion of the manufacturing process before being distri buted to 
customers.

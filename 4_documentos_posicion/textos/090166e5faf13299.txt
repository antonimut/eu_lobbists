 
 
AIM POSITION  
 
1 
 
European Commission proposal for a Regulation on packaging and packaging waste 
amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and repealing Directive 94/62/EC 
April 2023 
 
Introduction:  
AIM, the European Brands Association, welcomes the European Commission’s proposal on the  Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). The branded consumer goods industry acknowledges the need for 
change and wants to be a driving force in the transition to a clean, circular and climate-neutral economy. As 
outlined in our contribution “Brands for a Clean & Circular Economy”, focusing on the eco-design of packaging 
from a holistic point of view has been a priority for branded consumer goods manufacturers.  
A Regulation, with a full Single Market legal basis, is the right legal instrument:  
We strongly welcome the European Commission’s decision to transition the legal instrument from a Directive 
to a Regulation  based on t he Sin gle Market legal basis . These  are necessary towards ensuring an EU 
harmonised approach to packaging and packaging waste management and prevention, as well as preserving 
and strengthening the integrity of the EU Single Market and allowing for the scale -up of circular economy 
solutions.  
Member States must provide an effective packaging waste prevention & management system:  
Achieving the targets and measures set out for economic operators will only be possible if  an effective 
packaging waste prevention and management  system is in place across Member States.  Investments in 
sorting, collecting, and recycling facilities across the EU are necessary to complement brands’ efforts in 
designing more sustainable products. 
 
To ensure a strong EU policy  framework that allows the transition towar ds more sustainably designed 
packaging, as well as effective and efficient packaging waste prevention and management, we call on the EU 
policymakers to take the following considerations and recommendations into account. 
 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
LABELLING REQUIREMENTS – ARTICLE 11: 
- Provide a certain and sufficient transition period for manufacturers to ensure the compliance of their 
packaging with the new rules by: 
1) requiring the European Commission to adopt within a shorter time  frame of 12 months the 
implementing acts referred to in Article 11, 
2) allowing for  a 36-month transition period  to apply  once the Commission adopts the 
implementing acts and  
3) allow ing packaging manufactured or imported before the entry into application of the new 
measures to be marketed until the stocks of the production are exhausted. 
- Provide a clear and future -proof framework for the communication o f recycled content  in plastic 
packaging through a label.  
- Allow for the use of digital means for labelling requirements  and the provision of additional 
information to the consumer.

- Ensure maximum harmonisa tion of the labelling requirements in the EU, without exceptions for 
national EPR symbols, to preserve the integrity of the EU Single Market. 
- Ensure clarity for all economic operators on the labels, marks, symbols or inscriptions which are likely 
to mislead consumers and ensure that such symbols are phased out in accordance with the timeline 
set out in Article 11(1).  
PACKAGING MINIMISATION – ARTICLE 9: 
- Prevent the risk of standardisation of packaging, which undermines brands’ identity and increases the 
risk of counterfeiting and other intellectual property infringements facilitated by standard and simple 
shapes, by ensuring the protection of intellectual property rights for packaging. 
- Clarify the calculation of the empty space for sales packaging (it should be measured at the filling point) 
and ensure that the assessment takes into account the capabilities of the machine manufacturing the 
product. 
OBLIGATIONS OF DISTRIBUTORS – ARTICLE 17 
- Ensure that the obligations of distributors do not result in an effective oversight role in respect of the 
packaged goods supplied to them by the manufacturers. 
MINIMUM RECYCLED CONTENT REQUIREMENTS FOR PLASTIC PACKAGING – ARTICLE 7 
- Ensure that the responsibility for recycled content targets applies along the material value chain and 
is shared across economic operators. 
- Ensure a clear and future -proof framework for the calculation of recycled content (including when 
content is allocated through mass balance) and support a target calculation for recycled content as an 
average of all plastic packaging placed on the EU market by an economic operator rather than per unit 
of plastic packaging. 
- Provide safeguards in case of low amounts of available recycled plastics and excessive prices. 
RECYCLABLE PACKAGING – ARTICLE 6 
- Define the timeline for the application of the recyclability requirements and ensure the involvement 
of stakeholders in the development of the delegated acts (e.g., for the establishment of the Design for 
Recycling criteria).  
- Allow manufacturers to make claims on the r ecyclability of the packaging until the  essential 
requirements on recyclability become mandatory.  
NON-COMPLIANCE WITH PACKAGING REQUIREMENTS  
- Ensure that unless there is a risk to the health and safety of the user, packaging that is not compliant 
with the essential requirements should not be recalled.  
 REUSE AND REFILL 
- Introduce the concept of “system for refill”  and extend it to those system s for refill that allow 
consumers to refill their containers at home, with products purchased through a final distributor. 
- Exclude B2C transport packaging under the reuse targets set for transport packaging, as well as clarify 
the scope of the targets for grouped packaging.  
- Consider an exemption for reusable e -commerce packaging from the empty space requirements, to 
allow for the development of a harmonised solution for reusable packaging in e -commerce and 
different sizing options to account for the full variety of products provided via e-commerce.

AIM COMMENTS 
 Article 11 - Labelling requirements for packaging  
Ensuring a certain and sufficient transition period for manufacturers to adjust their products to the new 
labelling requirements 
We recommend that the proposal requires the Commission to adopt within 12 months the implementing 
acts referred to in Article 11 , that the  transition period for the industry  starts to apply when the 
Commission adopts th ose implementing acts, and that the packaging manufactured or  imported before 
the entry into application of the new measures can be marketed until the stocks of the products are 
exhausted. 
Manufacturers should be allowed 36 months to adjust their product and manufacturing process to be able 
to comply with the new labelling requirements.  
The complexity of the supply chain impacts the time and the process  needed for the collection of 
information from multiple packaging suppliers, the necessary calculations, documentation  and the process 
to change the artwork. According to the type of packaging, there is a specific lead time of up to 2 years from 
ordering the packaging to the final placing on the market . Additionally, the following elements should be 
taken into consideration: 
• Labels are not necessarily changed every year for all products and using up existing stocks of pre -
ordered packaging can extend the time needed to roll out the new labels. 
• As all economic operators will be implementing the new measures and changing the ir artworks 
within the same time frame, we anticipate technical bottlenecks (e.g., a limited number of printers 
on the market).  
• Having a longer transition period allows manufacturers to mutualise the changes that will be required 
with the additional new la belling measures that will be laid down in other product -specific or 
sectorial EU legislation and initiatives (e.g., Cosmetic Products Regulation, Detergent Regulation, and 
measures announced under the Farm to Fork Strategy). 
To allow the industry to place on the market packaging compliant with the labelling requirements, the period 
of 36 months should be linked to the development and entry into force of the European Commission’s 
implementing acts. If the manufacturers’ deadline for compliance is linked to the adoption of this Regulation, 
any potential delay from the Commission on the adoption of the implementing acts mentioned in Article 11 
(5) and (6) would impact the industry’s ability to bring production lines in compliance with the new 
requirements within a short time  frame. As shown by a similar experience with the late adoption of the 
marking requirements under the Single -Use Plastics Directive, a delayed publication of the implementing 
rules impacts both companies and the environment, forcing businesses to resort to other options, such as 
stickering or even the destruction of stocks of pre-ordered packaging. This would have consequences on the 
creation of packaging and product waste and the sustainability of the packaging p laced on the market (in 
particular its recyclability), being ultimately contradictory to the objectives of the PPWR proposal.  
   
Ensuring a flexible and future-proof framework for the communication of recycled content through a label  
AIM members are committed to investing to innovate the eco -design of their packaging, as well as moving 
towards higher recycled content than currentl y set by legislation. The communication that packaging 
contains additional recycled content is crucial to empower consumers to make a more sustainable choice. 
The Regulation should provide a framework for the communication on recycled content, including when 
the recycled content is only traceable through a mass balance method, as is the case for recycled content 
coming from chemical recycling. In this case, existing claims or communication guidelines from recognised 
third-party certifiers (e.g. ISCC) could be used for that purpose.

Allowing for the use of digital means for labelling requirements and providing additional information to 
the consumer 
We welcome the measures which permit information on packaging reusability to be provided  via digital 
means and we are supportive of the further upscaling of digital solutions. It is furthermore important that 
the proposal remains technology-neutral, to allow for the necessary flexibility for innovative solutions to be 
deployed.  
Digital labelling enables manufacturers to update or adapt information (e.g., to new regulat ory or 
technological changes), without having to modify the artwork of their packaging. It also allows manufacturers 
to provide more information to the consumer, while minimising packaging. Thus, we advocate for the 
extension of the possibility to use digi tal means for other information and labelling requirements , in 
particular those related to the material composition and the share of recycled content in plastic packaging 
or biobased plastic content. This is particularly relevant for very small packaging, where there is limited 
space and where it is not possible to affix the label or information to the grouped packaging.  To this end, 
we call for the inclusion of the definition of “small packaging” in alignment with existing legislation to bring 
legal certainty to economic operators. 
 
Ensuring maximum harmonisation of labelling requirements in the EU, without exceptions for national EPR 
symbols: 
We welcome the Commission’s intention to harmonise existin g sorting labelling requirements which is key 
to addressing the current fragmentation of the Single Market and preserving the free movement of goods.  
The implementing acts that will establish the specifications for the labelling requirements and formats for 
the labelling of packaging and waste receptacles should focus on symbols (pictograms) rather than language 
instructions or colours. Specifically, labels should not include harmonised language instructions which would 
require translation for packs that are used in multiple EU Member States , as these would need more space 
on the packaging, ultimately contradicting the objective of packaging minimisation set under this proposal. 
It is also best to avoid a colour approach for the labels, especially due to the different colour schemes already 
in place for waste management in the different Member States  and the need to ensure that the chosen 
colour does not decrease the ability of the packaging to be recycled. Moreover, colour requirements would 
impact the packaging design and brand differentiation and would imply additional costs for companies when 
printing on packaging. 
However, the current proposal still allows national authorities to require packaging included in an extended 
producer responsibility (EPR) scheme to be identified with a corresponding symbol throughout the territory 
in which that scheme or system applies. This will allow national labels to continue to proliferate, contributing 
to further consumer confusion. Hence, we strongly recommend adopting a harmonised approach and 
preventing any fragmentation in terms of labelling requirements, without allowing for the continued use 
of national symbols for EPR schemes.  
 
Ensuring clarity for all economic operators on the prohibition of provision or display of labels, marks, 
symbols or inscriptions which would likely mislead the consumers 
It is important to clarify that all stakeholders, including economic operators, and Producer Responsibility 
Organisations (PROs) shall not provide or display labels, marks, symbols or inscriptions which would likely 
mislead the consumer.  Likewise, Member States shall not require economic operators to display labels 
which would potentially be misleading.  To ensure legal certainty for the application of the prohibition to 
provide or display labels, marks, symbols or inscriptions which would likely mislead the consumer, we 
recommend:  
- Clarifying that such labels, marks, symbols or inscriptions shall be phased out in accordance with 
the deadline set out in Article 11 (1). While Article 11(1) sets out a specific time frame  for the 
application of the labelling requirements, Article 11(7) does not provide such an indication. Thus, 
application of the prohibition set out in Article 11(7) would apply from the entry into force of the

Regulation. This could lead to double the workload for the packaging manufacturer, which would be 
obliged first to have to remove the labels considered to be potentially misleading, and at a second 
stage, undertake the necessary changes for the artwork to comply with the  other labelling 
requirements set out under Article 11.   
- The European Commission should also provide guidance on the aspects subject to the harmonised 
labelling requirements which are likely to mislead or confuse the consumer.  
 Article 9 – Packaging minimisation  
Rules on packaging minimisation should not lead to standardisation and should not minimise brands’ 
identity 
Branded consumer goods manufacturers are committed to better eco -design of their packaging, including 
through the reduction of weight and volume. We agree that, when designing their own packaging, consumer 
goods manufacturers should assess how to minimise the packaging, according to the performance criteria  
laid down in Annex IV of the Regulation.  
However, w e are concerned that the removal of “mark eting and consumer acceptance” from the 
performance criteria listed in Annex IV without any possibility for recognition of distinctive design and 
differentiated presentation represents a risk of standardisation and limitation of the brands’ creative 
freedom to design packaging. We agree that marketing and consumer acceptance alone should not be the 
only criteria determining or justifying an increase in the weight or volume of the packaging . However, as 
acknowledged by the proposal, the presentation of the good is a key functionality of the packaging. Packaging 
should be designed to minimise its volume and weight while maintaining its ability to perform the packaging 
functions including presentation and allowing reasonable and proportionate design differentiation. Brands’ 
packaging contributes to communicating the brands’ identity to the consumer, building trust and informing 
the consumer. Through marketing communication on the pack, brands can also nudge consumers towards 
more sustainable choices and practices or choose reusable packaging.  
Packaging design is thus an integral aspect of product design and presentation. Differentiated volume and 
shape of the packaging are often inherent to the product brand identity and equity, reflect know-how, or 
tradition and cultural heritage (e.g., advent calendars, perfume bottles, wines and spirits), that can highlight 
brand equity, product type and/or geography, and support premium isation and brand elevation . As such 
they are often protected under intellectual property in the EU – including, but not limited to, trade marks, 
registered designs and protected Geographical Indications. Intellectual property rights, which can also 
protect packaging, play a vital role for sectors whose businesses rely directly on the added value of their 
innovations and the tangible and intangible values of their products. Branded consumer goods 
manufacturers are part of the EU’s IPR-intensive industries1, which represent 75% of trade within the EU, 
generates almost 4 in 10 jobs in the Member States, and had a contribution of EU GDP of 47.1% in 2022, 
worth 6.4 trillion euro s. This includes design of artwor k and packaging, which are an important aspects of 
product design and presentation. 
Unqualified packaging minimisation may also stifle packaging and product innovation, with a range of 
adverse consequences , from preventing market entry by SMEs to advances in consumer safety. As an 
example of its role in consumer safety, packaging differentiation is often key to the prevention of 
counterfeiting, which is  facilitated by standard or simple shapes.  Counterfeiters wishing to replace safe, 
legitimate products with illicit, dangerous products have an easier task the more standardised packaging 
becomes.2

Additionally, we are concerned that th e objective of packaging minimisation and the criteria detailed in 
Annex IV do not distinguish between the material used and its environmental impact. As branded consumer 
goods manufacturers have invested towards less impactful packaging materials, the PPWR should ensure 
consistency and prevent any impact on ongoing investments towards more sustainable solutions. 
 
Clarifying the calculation of the empty space for sales packaging 
In addition, it is necessary to ensure that manufacturers can adopt an integrated approach across the layers 
of packaging  and concerning the calculation of the empty space , to ensure that packaging is effectively 
minimised while guaranteeing the safety of the packed product. It should be noted that the only way to 
accurately measure how much product is inserted into sales packaging for certain products is as a pack-fill 
level at the point of filling because some products settle during transport and appear less filled at the point 
of sale, which economic operators cannot control. In contrast, an empty space ratio could be misinterpreted 
as a measure of the volume of product to the volume of air in the pack, which is only an indication of how a 
product is shaped and fills out the pack. For instance, even if both packs are filled to the same level within a 
bag, a bag of ring-shaped products, which has an empty space in the hole in the middle, will have more total 
empty space in the bag than a bag of products that are ball-shaped due to the shape of the food.   
Moreover, the assessment should be made taking into account  the specific capabilities of the machine 
manufacturing the product: economic operators invest in their machines by cycles and the same product 
can be manufactured on two different lines which might not have the same capabilities.  
 Article 17 – Obligations of distributors 
The obligation of distributors currently foreseen under Article 17 risks creating an effective oversight role of 
the distributors in respect of packaged goods supplied to them by the manufacturers. As manufacturers and 
distributors are often competitors between innovative brands of manufacturers and own-brand/private label 
brands of distributors (e.g. supermarkets), this  has the effect of potential misuse or abuse of commercial 
advantage. Distributors should be obliged to ensure that conformity data provided by the manufacturer is 
confidential and shared with the sole purpose of complying with the obligation laid out in A rticle 17 . 
Conformity data should not be used for any other reasons, including inter alia to assist their own packaging 
design in respect of the supporting documentation for the self -certification of their own branded goods. 
Hence, the role of distributors should never be abused within the context of commercial dealings. Any abuse 
of the duty of care should be penalised within the scope of Article 62. 
 Article 7 - Minimum recycled content requirements for plastic packaging 
Ensuring that the responsibility for recycled content targets applies along the material value chain 
We welcome the establishment of mandatory targets for recycled content in plastic packaging which will 
contribute to the uptake of secondary raw materials. Under the current technical and regulatory limitations, 
the recycled content targets must still guarantee quality, hygiene standards, stability and fitness for use. 
However, there  are relatively few feedstock suppliers for consumer goods manufacturers , who have no 
control over the quantity and price of the recycled plastic available on the secondary raw materials market. 
Therefore, the responsibility for the recycled content obligations should apply along the material value 
chain and be shared across economic operators . The enforcement would  thus be better focused on the 
supply of recyclates rather than their subsequent use.  
Ensuring a clear and future-proof framework for the calculation of the recycled content (including when 
allocated through mass balance) and supporting a target calculation as an average of all plastic packaging 
placed on the EU market by an economic operator 
Additionally, the new legislative framework should be flexible and future-proof, to allow economic operators 
to achieve, and potentially go beyond, the mandatory targets set under the proposal through both existing 
             
 
 
    7 
 
and future recycling technologies, including chemical recycling . Chemical recycling technologies provide 
today with a solution  which, if at scale, would enable  the achievement of recycled content targets for 
packaging materials, including types of contact-sensitive packaging, which cannot be otherwise  processed 
through mechanical recycling.  This is possible through the  use of a m ass balance allocation which allows 
tracing the flow of materials through complex value chain s, where it is not possible to segregate raw 
materials. While the Commission will adopt implementing acts to define the methodology for the calculation 
and verification of the percentage of recycled content, the current legislative framework should not preclude 
the economic operators to rely on mechanical or chemical recycling technologies.  
We recommend that mandatory recycled content targets are not calculated per unit of plastic packaging 
but rather as an average of the overall portfolio of plastic packaging placed by the manufacturer on the EU 
market. In the same vein, companies that are investing to move towards higher recycled feedstock should 
be able to communicate to consumers the information related to the share of recycled content. Moreover, 
given that a significant amount of innovation is taking place in the field of recycling technologies, the label 
laid down in Article 11(3) should be technology-neutral.  
 
Providing safeguards in case of low amounts of recycled plastics and excessive prices 
We welcome that, when justified by a lack of availability or excessive prices of specific recycled plastics, the 
Commission can adopt delegated acts to review the minimum targets  requirements. However, we believe 
that it is necessary to provide a safeguard mechanism, to ensure that, below a specific amount of recycled 
content, the Commission is obliged to take the necessary actions  through an urgency procedure  and the 
targets set out in Articles 7(1) and 7(2) shall be suspended. 
 Article 6 - Recyclable packaging 
Establishing a clear timeline for the application of the recyclability requirements, while ensuring the 
involvement of stakeholders in the development of the secondary legislation 
We believe that timelines for the entry into force of recyclability requirements should be clear and provide 
adequate transition periods to the industry. The current lack of a timeline for specific criteria that packaging 
shall comply with to be considered recyclable wou ld imply that requirements are to be applied from the 
entry into force of the Regulation, which would be too restrictive. 
In addition, the delegated acts to be adopted by the European Commission to establish the Design for 
Recycling criteria and the recycling performance grades must b e developed in close cooperation with 
stakeholders. To this end, e xisting standards  and D4R guidelines  for packaging materials should be 
considered. 
Moreover, it should be clarified by when the European Commission shall adopt the several delegated acts 
mentioned in Article 6, which include the establishment of the Design for Recycling criteria (applying from 1 
January 2030), recycling performance grades, rules on the eco -modulation of extended producer 
responsibility fees, as well as the methodology to assess if the packaging is recyclable at scale (applying from 
1 January 2035). 
 
Allowing manufacturers to make claims on the recyclability of packaging  
The proposal should clarify how brands can continue making claims on the recyclability of the packaging until 
the essential requirements on recyclability will become mandatory. In alignment with the proposal on 
Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition, it should be possible for companies to substantiate claims 
on the recyclability of packaging when they draw up the required technical documentation, by having it 
verified by a third party.

 Non-compliance with packaging requirements 
It is necessary to ensure that measures of non-compliance with packaging requirements are proportionate. 
The proposal currently provides that , in case of non -compliance, the Member States can ensure that the 
packaging is recalled or withdrawn from the market. Wh ile it is necessary to have a clear enforcement 
framework in place, including penalties, we are concerned about the potential unintended consequences 
that the recall of packaging that is not compliant with sustainability requirements but is safe, would have 
on the environment (including potential destruction of the good). Unless there is a risk to the health and 
safety of the user, there should not be a recall. Moreover, penalties and corrective measures should be 
proportionate according to the responsibility in the supply chain. 
 Reuse and refill  
Reuse measures should be forward-looking and support brands that want to offer products through reusable 
systems that can reduce single-use packaging and packaging waste. The concept of refill should be extended 
to those systems for refill that allow consumers to refill their containers at home, with products purchased 
through a final distributor. These systems are convenient for consumers and fulfil the same function of reuse, 
by reducing the amount of single use packaging put on the market. By extending the definition of refill, the 
EU will encourage brands to expand on these models, both for the sectors that will be covered by the targets, 
but also for those companies that want to offer a range of sustai nable packaging solutions to their 
consumers.  
We recommend the introduction of the concept of “system for refill”, to clarify that refilling a container (e.g., 
a glass) with any beverage should not be considered a refill in the context of this proposal.  
 
Moreover, we support the set measures to increase the use of reusable packaging in e-commerce. However, 
the target set in the proposal remain s challenging to achieve due to a lack of precise indication of the type 
of packaging covered, as well as the current EU market set-up. In particular: 
- The targets set for “transport packaging” should exclude B2C transport packaging because this type 
of packaging is made of cardboard, is not reusable and is shipped by independent transport 
companies, so reverse logistics would be extremely complicated to set up. 
- The scope of the targets for grouped packaging used to create a stock -keeping unit is not clear 
enough. We recommend including examples and clarifying whether only B2C packaging is excluded, 
or also B2B. 
- E-commerce shipments are variable in size, product count and variety of products per shipment 
order. Establishing harmonized solutions for reusable packaging in e-commerce will enhance the take 
up of reusable options, but will need time to develop a multitude of sizing options to account for the 
full variety of products provided in the EU via E -Commerce solutions. We recommend exempting 
reusable e-commerce packaging from the empty space requirements – at least for a transition time.

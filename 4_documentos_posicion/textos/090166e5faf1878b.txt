CEC comments on the Proposal for a  
Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) 
 
The European Footwear Confederation (CEC), the voice of the European Footwear industry, welcomes 
the efforts of the European Union to reach the sustainability goals set in the EU Green Deal and 
continues committed to contribute to the green and digital transitions. On this occasion, we thank the 
Commission for the opportunity to provide feedback to the Proposal for a Packaging and Packaging 
Waste Regulation (PPWR), and would like to raise attention to some relevant aspects for the footwear 
industry, as well as requests. 
 
 Harmonisation of requirements and choice of the legal instrument 
The CEC fully supports the harmonisation of sustainability, labelling and information requirements 
at EU level envisaged by the proposal and the choice to turn the Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Directive, currently into force, into a Regulation. However, the openness foreseen in certain provisions 
could hamper the achievement of this goal. In this regard, we wish to underline the still risk of arts. 
4.4 and 4.5: national administrations should be discouraged from adopting additional requirements 
and restrictions to those agreed at EU level, in order to maximise the free movement of goods and 
avoid trade barriers within the Single Market falling in detriment of the competitiveness of companies 
and generating legal uncertainty. The incentives for the application of the waste hierarchy addressed 
in art. 38.3 should also be defined, or at least monitored, at EU level, to guarantee the level playing 
field among economic operators. 
 
 Packaging as product protection  
In the footwear industry, packaging fully falls under the scope of “product protection” set in the Annex 
IV of the proposal. A shoe box is part of the consumers wardrobes, and it is very often used for the 
domestic protection of footwear. Furthermore, it is fundamental to exclude separate packaging used 
to fill the inside of the shoe from the empty space volume, unlike what indicated in art. 9.3. 
 
 Packaging and the creativity of European companies 
A shoe box does not only have a transportation and protection function, but it is also part of the 
creativity and marketing of a company when engaging with consumers. Hence, we do not agree with 
the removal of the “marketing and consumer acceptance” in the performance criteria list of packaging, 
and would like to have it re-included.  
Moreover, footwear is one of the product categories with a highest number of counterfeiting cases. 
A personalised shoe box can therefore be critical for identifying counterfeited shoes, and 
standardisation and/or simplification should be avoided. In this regard, the creativity of European 
artisans should also be protected, and we should not put at risk their jobs. Consumers appreciate 
product differentiation and design. When it comes to fashion products and their packaging, 
standardisation does not correspond with creativity and savoir-faire. Therefore, we would like art. 9

to be amended to clarify that the reduction to the minimum necessary for ensuring its functionality

 More accurate and effective empty space ratio 
Art. 21.1 establishes that economic operators who supply products to a final distributor or an end user 
in grouped packaging, transport packaging or e-commerce packaging, shall ensure that the empty 
space ratio is maximum 40%. This requirement seems not realistic for grouped packaging with 
products of very different sizes on e-commerce, because in some cases there will certainly be empty 
space over the 40% limit. As currently proposed, the risk is that economic operators will eventually 
opt for separate packaging for each item and inject more future waste in the market. A solution could 
be to define the empty space ratio in accordance with the following criteria: the packaging box is 
designed to fit the size of the biggest item of the grouped packaging, and all elements of buffer 
packaging are recycled. 
 
 Attention to smaller companies 
The proposal is meant to apply to all companies, regardless their size. While recognising the need of 
a wide scope, the implementation of the PPWR should ensure that all action required is relevant and 
proportionate, unnecessary burden is avoided and no company is left behind. In the same 
streamline, the CEC calls for the assessment of the current timeline and, if necessary, to consider a 
longer adaptation period where needed.  
A special attention should be given to the derogation for micro-companies defined in art. 22.3 and 
subject to the implementation by Member States. Not only does the proposal not provide legal 
certainty that such exceptions will be implemented, but the harmonisation efforts at the core of the 
proposal would be also at risk. To avoid different interpretations among Member States, art. 22.3 
should already establish the derogation for micro-companies.  
 
 Information to consumers and labelling 
We welcome the harmonisation of labelling as settled in the art. 11 of the proposal, as we have lately 
seen a few EU national initiatives in this respect causing big disruptions on the free movement of 
goods, and a big administrative burdensome to our companies. We believe that a digital label would 
be the most appropriate solution in accordance with packaging minimisation principle. 
However, the PPWR proposal is only one of the current or upcoming pieces of legislation defining 
labelling and information requirements. With the Digital Product Passport that will enter into force 
within the ESPR, labelling and information requirements will be extended to other aspects of the 
product and/or packaging (ex: geographic origin, materials, sustainability scores, maintenance, etc.), 
and there is a high probability of overlapping information.  
Therefore, the CEC invites the legislators to coordinate the labelling and information requirements 
of the PPWR with the other relevant legislative proposals and find common solutions to avoid useless

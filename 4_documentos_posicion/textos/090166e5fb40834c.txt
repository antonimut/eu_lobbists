 
Cobalt Institute response to Critical Raw Materials Act consultation 
2nd May 2023 
 
1. Recommendations and summary 
 
  
We support the intentions of the Act, which represent an important first step towards 
supporting resilient raw materials sourcing and production, globally and in the EU. 
There remain opportunities to go further, which we outline these in this paper. 
 
General policy comments: 
• Provide incentives – the Commission and Member States should provide greater 
clarity as to how State Aid rules, existing and new funding programmes, would be used, 
and introduce grant, loan and tax credit mechanisms to support the cr eation of new 
facilities in the EU and third countries.  These should be focused on both OpEx and 
CapEx. 
• Create a supportive investment climate – mechanisms should be added to all 
legislation where restrictions are being placed, or investment conditions being changed, 
for the use of Strategic Raw Materials.  For example, the setting of occupational 
exposure limit values should include an assessment of the impact on the use of 
strategic raw materials in Europe. 
• Ensure r egulatory consistency  – all uses of strategic raw materials serving 
applications and technologies deemed strategic for the EU should be considered to 
meet the essential use concept. 
• Supporting international investment  – the Commission should provide de -risking 
mechanisms to support cobalt investments of strategic importance for the EU  but 
realised in third countries, in the same way as projects realised within the EU (fast track 
and easier access to financial mechanisms such as loans, loan guarantees, grants, tax 
credits). 
• Geopolitical ties with sourcing nations – we recommend the Commission establish 
strategic partnerships with major cobalt producing nations, with a focus on support for 
de-risking outside investments (corruption risks, state capture etc.). 
 
Specific comments: 
• Benchmarks – we support the inclusion of non-binding, horizontal benchmarks, but a 
weighting mechanism should be used to ensure high-tonnage materials don’t dominate. 
• Regulatory burden – requirements like stress tests and supply chain audits, as well 
as for extractive waste, must be implemented in a light touch way. They should be 
designed with businesses and the investment climate in mind, whilst also delivering 
information the Commission needs. 
• Stockpiling – stockpiling should be a Commission or Member State responsibility. 
• Joint purchasing – the EU needs to ensure that joint purchasing approaches are in 
line with the smooth operation of a global free market, and that European companies 
have incentives to invest in mining in third countries. 
• Industry support for the Board – industry’s technical expertise should be brought in 
to support the decision making  the Board, for example in the sub -groups on 
coordinating financing for Strategic Projects, geological surveys, and national supply 
and information.

2. Introduction to cobalt 
 
Cobalt, listed as a critical and Strategic Raw Material in the European Union (EU), plays a central role 
in the EU’s security, future prosperity and transition to a low carbon economy.  
 
• As of 2021, 65% of all cobalt used globally goes into lithium -ion batteries1. Over half of this 
number (34% of all cobalt) goes into batteries for electric vehicles, with the remainder going 
principally into batteries for consumer electronics (31%)2.  
• In 2021, cobalt-containing batteries accounted for three quarters of the global electric vehicle 
battery market and EVs are expected to drive 70% of medium-term cobalt market growth3.  As 
a result of this, demand for cobalt expected to increase up to 403% by 2050 globally4. 
• Cobalt is also critical for a range of other sectors, including superalloys as used in aerospace 
and defence applications (11% of cobalt use). It is also used for cemented carbide tools, alloys 
and magnets, which represented about 14% of cobalt usage in 20215. 
• Cobalt is also essential for human health as a key part of vitamin B12. 
 
Cobalt is a valuable metal and has unique chemical properties. These intrinsic properties mean it is 
often used to provide strength, resilience  and durability to products where it is used.  In batteries, it 
stabilises the cell and makes it more durable; it makes superalloys capable functioning normally despite 
extreme heat; in magnets it makes them retain their magnetism despite exposure to extreme cold and 
heat, and in prosthetics it allows the steel to endure despite years of grinding motions. Given the cost 
of cobalt, it is typically used only where it is essential, and substitution is not possible. 
 
The European cobalt market has the potential to grow to 30-50 kt in 2030  and 80-100 kt in 2050 . 
Europe’s success rate in developing battery cathode manufacturing capacity will determine the growth 
in cobalt demand6. Employment in the Europe’s cobalt value chain reached 48,000 jobs per annum on 
average over the 2010-2021 period. The end-use sector was by far the largest contributor, generating 
approximately 18,000 jobs. Direct applications followed (15,000) and smelting & refining accounted for 
8,000 jobs. Of total global employment, Europe was the third largest region7.  
 
Europe does not have abundant reserves of cobalt within its borders, and so will be reliant to some 
degree on important primary material in the near future. By 2050, secondary supply from end -of-life 
batteries has the theoretical potential to provide 65% of Europe’s total cobalt demand. This requires 
that Europe is successful in building up sufficient battery recycling capacity to match its electric vehicle 
market. But until 2040, volumes of secondary cobalt will remain relatively low as sufficient quantities will 
not yet be available from in -use batteries, and Europe’s increased demand will need to be met by 
primary supply sources 8. While cobalt containing batteries are typically economically attr active to 
recycle, not all battery types are. Lithium-ion phosphate batteries (being used today in low-cost electric 
vehicles), for example, are not considered economically recyclable9.

3. General response to the Critical Raw Materials Act 
 
Given the EU’s stated policy ambitions around circularity, decarbonisation and open strategic autonomy, 
European workers will need to work with cobalt more in the future. It is estimated that worldwide capital 
expenditure of between USD$2.5 trillion and USD$3 trillion will be required to meet the mining, refining 
and chemicals needs of the energy transition11. For Europe to secure its place at the lead of the green 
transition, its public and private sectors need to make major investments in the coming decade. 
 
To achieve this, Europe must ensure that its investment climate is the most compelling in the 
world, so it attracts the lion’s share of this investment and does so before other jurisdictions. 
This means business es need confidence, predictability, and stability . This confidence wi ll facilitate 
access to finance, which can be further support by proactive EU policies. 
 
This is not the reality in Europe today . Europe has more barriers than other jurisdictions but fewer 
incentives. The Critical Raw Materials Act goes some way towards addressing this but leaves important 
gaps. Europe can lead the way, and it can do this by: 
 
1. Creating a supportive investment climate that creates confidence for business with 
predictable and stable regulation. 
2. Providing incentives for investment that are WTO compliant and compatible with a global 
free market. 
3. Supporting the de-risking of international investments through strong geopolitical ties 
and agreements with important producer countries. 
 
We will break these down in more detail one-by-one. 
 
3.1. Providing incentives for investment that are WTO compliant , compatible with a 
global free market and competitive. 
 
The United States’ Inflation Reduction Act has had a major impact on the investment climate for 
materials the US considers “critical minerals”. The Act offers number of grants, long-term, low-cost loans 
and loan guarantees as well as tax credits to critical raw materials manufacturers. Among these include 
a tax credit equal to 30% of the cost of capital expenditure and 10% of the cost of produc tion without 
any sunset clause. 
 
The CRM Act fails to provide comparable support to the Inflation Reduction Act. The Act should either 
include, or should be followed, by clear incentives in the form of grants and tax credits for  facilities in 
the cobalt value chain, including processing, refining and/or recycling.  
 
It is crucial to get fast tracked and easier access to existing financing programmes and loan guarantees, 
as described in the Commission’s comment to the CRM Act. The conditions of these existing financing 
programmes and loan guarantees will have to be reconsidered, reshaped and if required supplemented 
with new instruments to close the gap to foreign acts such as the Inflation Reduction Act, otherwise the 
goal of the Critical Raw Materials Act m ight not be achieved and important investment project in the 
Critical Raw Material supply chain might be realised outside of the EU. 
 
It is also important that the CRM Act be implemented quickly, given the speed at which the Inflation 
Reduction Act and its related programmes are being brought in (as this is already affecting capital flows 
and investment decisions).

The Commission and Member States should provide greater clarity as to how State Aid 
rules would be used and introduce grant, loan and tax credit mechanisms to support the 
creation of new facilities in the EU and third countries. These incentives should be 
competitive with mechanisms provided in other jurisdictions, like the US Inflation 
Reduction Act. 
 
3.2. Creating a supportive investment climate that creates confidence for business with 
predictable and stable regulation. 
 
Investment needs long term certainty, predictability, and stability. When a company makes major capital 
expenditures – like those required for Strategic Raw Material refining, processing and recycling facilities 
– it will carefully assess the likely revenues, demand growth and risks associated with a project. This 
includes assessments of potential regulatory risks. Getting a return on investment for a facility like this 
typically takes about 20-30 years, and so risks are assessed over those periods. Another way of framing 
it is the importance of considering both “OpEx” (operational expenditure) as well as upfront “CapEx” 
(capital expenditure) costs. Both must be considered to make investment attractive  and regulator y 
barriers can increase both OpEx and CapEx costs. 
 
In Europe, many strategic and critical raw materials also have “hazard” classifications. Currently any 
substance classified as “hazardous” can have significant barriers placed on their use, under a whole 
range of legislation from Ecodesign, to REACH and OSH.  
 
Proposed control s on the use of a critical or Strategic Raw Material (for example authorisations, 
restrictions, “essentiality” classifications, disproportionate occupational exposure limit values, etc.) have 
a chilling effect on investment. These are fast-moving sectors and businesses can only make investment 
decisions based on the information they have today  – for example the low proposed value for an 
Occupational Exposure Limit for cobalt  will prevent important investments in strategic critical raw 
material supply chains to be made in the EU. Realistic occupational exposure limits would not jeopardise 
safety or environmental protections, but deliver long term certainty, predictability, and  stability to the 
investors. Likewise, the Essential Use Concept is unclear, creating doubts about whether cobalt -
containing batteries and other products would qualify. This doubt is felt by investors who fear the 
economic viability of their investments could be undermined by definitions that are beyond their control. 
 
We welcome moves to expedite the permitting system – this will support investment in Europe – but it 
will only support investments where a compelling business case has already been made.  
 
3.3. Supporting the de-risking of international investments through stro ng geopolitical 
ties and agreements with important producer countries. 
 
The Act’s benchmarks are focused on domestic supply, but the other half of the picture is resilient 
international supply. We recognise this is  referenced in the “no more than 65% relia nce” target, but it 
remains important that the EU also support European companies to invest in these markets. 
 
Primary cobalt will need to be sourced on the global market, as Europe does not have sufficient 
reserves, but it can be reliably sourced from diverse locations internationally, and European companies 
can play an important role in delivering that diverse, global supply , whilst also bringing their high 
standards of responsible sourcing and transparency. 
 
Supply risks can also be mitigated through good relationships with sourcing countries , strategic 
partnerships, and investment in critical infrastructure. The EU should play a more proactive role on the 
Mechanisms or “triggers” for Strategic Raw Materials should be added to all legislation 
where restrictions are being placed on their use. This should require an additional “check” 
on the impact measures would have on the investment climate for these materials,  
implementing the better regulation principles among others, at an early stage, and before 
measures are publicly announced.  This includes, for example disproportionate 
occupational exposure limit values or authorisations and restrictions under REACH. 
 
All uses of critical raw materials serving applications and technologies deemed strategic 
for the EU shall be considered to meet the essential use concept laid down in the 
Taxonomy Regulation and in any other Regulation or commission guidance in future.

The EU should use existing fund like the Global Gateway to invest in critical infrastructure, in a way that

We recommend the Commission establish strategic partnerships with major cobalt

risks, state capture etc.)  while concretely contributing to expressed prioritie s of partner

4. Specific recommendations on the text 
 
4.1. Benchmarks 
 
We support the inclusion of non-binding, horizontal benchmarks in the Act, as the specificities of each 
raw material (like cobalt) would require a significantly more detailed analysis to ascertain a meaningful 
benchmark for each.  
 
It is currently unclear whether the percentage benchmark refers to tonnage, or another metric like unit 
value. Depending on how this question in answered, it could greatly affect what the target means in 
practice. For example, if by tonnage, then certain applications like copper will dominate whether 
benchmarks are achieved, which could have unintended consequences for investment. If done this way, 
a weighting mechanism should be applied (for example, divide European production by European usage 
for each material, times by 100 to a make a percentage, and then averaging the percentages for each 
metal). This would weight each metal the same under the benchmark – however it would be important 
to note that the individual percentages per raw material are not suitable as benchmarks given the 
horizontal targets not being meaningful at a substance-specific level. 
 
It is also worth noting that we do not believe the contents of the Act alone will be sufficient to achieving 
the benchmarks, so a roadmap should be developed to underpin the benchmarks. 
 
4.2. Regulatory barriers and burdens 
 
As mentioned earlier in this paper, removing unnecessary barriers and creating predictability  are 
important for facilitating investment. 
 
We have already discussed the major barriers creating by chemicals management legislation, but it is 
not the only area.  The Act introduces a number of new requirements for companies , especially on 
environmental footprint declarations and stress tests and supply chain audits, as well as for extractive 
waste. While this are often good to have, they should not ap Justification: Current State Aid rules are unclear 
and lack mechanisms to support the creation of new critical raw materials facilities in the EU and abroad. 
ply undue burdens. 
 
Implemented in a burdensome way (and in conjunction with wider requirements on businesses in other 
legislation) it can add to the cost of doing business in Europe and make EU companies less competitive, 
especially when applied to smaller companies, or start-ups. 
 
A comparison of international requirements is available in an Annex to this document. 
 
4.3. Stockpiling 
 
The Act refers to “maintaining incentives” for private companies to stockp ile strategic raw materials. 
Private sector companies should not be expected to stockpile minerals, without it being paid for  from 
public financing. Requiring companies to stockpile without support would significantly harm balance 
sheets and undermine financial viability.  
We support the inclusion of non-binding, horizontal benchmarks, but a weighting 
mechanism should be used to ensure high-tonnage materials don’t dominate. 
These requirements (stress tests and supply chain audits, as well as for extractive waste) 
must be implemented in a light touch way. They should be designed with businesses and 
the investment climate in mind, whilst also delivering information the Commission needs.  
Stockpiling should be a Commission or Member State responsibility. 
 
4.4. Financial coordination mechanisms 
 
We support mechanisms to support off-take agreements and coordinate with private and public financial 
institutions; however, more can be done. 
 
 
4.5. Critical Raw Materials Board 
 
Where relevant, there should be mechanisms for industry to have a role on the Critical Raw Materials 
Board.

engagement we strive to achieve safe, responsible and sustainable cobalt.

 
Position of the Austrian Federal Economic Chamber (WKÖ) on the European Commission's 
Proposal on a Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) (COM(2023) 168) of March 16th 2023 
 
 
I. General remarks 
 
On March 16, the European Commission (EC) published the so-called Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) 
as a proposal for a regulation (COM(2023) 168).  The WKÖ welcomes this initiative. From the 
proposal, a clear paradigm shift of the Commission can be seen - away from a primarily regulation-
driven policy of green change towards a green industrial policy that carries a business-friendly 
note in terms of l ocation and competition policy . The EC is thus taking a long overdue step 
towards a coherent, effective, and targeted European industrial policy. The NZIA makes an 
important contribution to decarbonization, to reducing energy dependence on EU third countries, 
to reducing the vulnerability of the European energy system, and provides growth policy impetus 
through increased local value creation. Finally, it also shows that the Commission has recognized 
that in a new geopolitical environment  in which open marke t economies and free trade are 
coming under increasing pressure, an expanded set of economic policy instruments is needed to 
ensure or restore fair competitive conditions for European companies. In addition to ensuring fair 
internal market conditions, the competitiveness of the European internal market vis-à-vis other 
economic regions is now also being given greater attention. 
 
 
II. Details 
 
The Austrian Federal Economic Chamber formulates the following statement on the individual 
components of the NZIA: 
 
Chapter I: Subject matter, scope and definitions 
 
We strongly welcome the planned expansion of production capacities for Net-Zero technologies as 
well as their free circulation on the domestic market. From the point of view of the WKÖ , the 
following aspects are central: 
 
 The production of key components  that serve as direct inputs for the manufacture of Net-Zero 
technologies needs to be covered by the NZIA as well.  This also applies to the upstream value chain, 
including processes, chemicals, and materials, which has also to be considered  as strategic to the 
transition to a Net-Zero economy and a circular economy. 
 There’s a need for a clear definition of those "essential components" over which the scope of the NZIA 
extends to enable a stringent mapping of complete value chains. For the NZIA to remain coherent, it is 
necessary to align the technologies listed under the definitions of " Net-Zero Technologies" with those 
technologies listed in the Annex as "Strategic Net-Zero Technologies". 
 Recognition of recycling technologies  (both mechanical and chemical recycling), hydropower, and 
critical infrastructure applications  (including control elements, convert ers, and related digitization 
technologies) as Net-Zero technologies. 
 Close adjustment to other acts, such as the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), a yet to be defined 
"Semi-Finished Products Act" and the European Chips Act (ECA) due to strong interdepend encies.

Chapter II: Enabling conditions for Net-Zero technology manufacturing 
 
We support the core objective of the proposed regulation to streamline administrative and 
approval procedures with suitable instruments and forms of financing in such a way that Net-Zero 
project implementation procedures are significantly accelerated.  There is still a huge need for 
action when it comes to the financial dimension of this project. 
 
Streamlining administrative and permit-granting processes 
 
 The streamlining of administrative and approval procedures must be accompanied by administrative 
simplifications for companies. Shortened deadlines written down on paper alone will not achieve the 
desired results. This is especially the case for the obligation in the NZIA for applicants to improve their 
documentation within 14 days of being requested to do so. Especially  in the case of large projects, 
compliance with such a deadline cannot be ensured. 
 We call not only for an improvement in the enforcement of the licensing procedures per se, but also for 
a move away from increasingly far -reaching and sometimes contradictor y substantive regulations. 
We do not see them as sufficiently addressed in the NZIA and the related CRMA. 
 Beyond the NZIA, the Commission should, in general, work to accelerate procedures in other 
technology areas within its capabilities. 
One Stop Shops 
 
 When the new One Stop Shop s are to be set up, it must be s pecified in advance which national 
authority, or which domestic ministry will be the central point of contact. This is of central importance 
not only for a faster procedure, but also for the central information control concerning legal and 
financial questions of future projects.  The appointment of the corresponding national au thority or the 
domestic ministry should then also take place at the time of the entry into force of the regulation and 
not, as proposed in the draft, three months later. 
 The EC must also ensure that the planned One Stop Shops themselves always have uncomplicated and 
adequate access to all relevant information, which they are then to process as service providers. 
 The labor shortage problem must also be considered for One Stop Shop s. It also brings with it a 
shortage of labor in government agencies. Therefore, the possibilities of digitization must be utilized in 
the processes as far as possible. 
 The procedural relief provided by One Stop Shops should also be accompanied by a reduction in costs 
for companies (e.g., for environmental impact assessments). 
Net-Zero strategic projects 
 
 There needs to be a broad speeding-up of application and approval process es. In the short term , we 
support the so-called priority status  for strategic Net-Zero projects. However, t his may have as a 
consequence that prioritizing certain projects through expedited procedures may create a resource 
bottleneck for other projects not within the scope of the NZIA. In the medium term , therefore, 
application and approval processes outside th e Net-Zero ecosystem must also be guided by the new 
expedited processes. 
 We strongly support the business-friendly solution  that applications are deemed approved if the 
relevant administrative bodies do not respond within the deadlines specified in the NZIA.

Financial dimension of the NZIA 
 In our view, the NZIA as part of the Green Deal Industrial Plan (GDIP) is characterized by a highly 
uncertain financing situation with a confusing collection of measures and subsidies with different 
terms and requirements to be met . This leads to a significant industrial location policy disadvantage 
for Europe compared to other economic regions. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) in the USA, for 
example, is impressive for its simplicity by focusing only on one central instrument - tax credits for 
operating and capital expenditures. 
 A Net-Zero industrial strategy must include a clearly identifia ble approach to support significant 
expansion of EU-wide green value chains for all sizes of businesses (including SMEs) (e.g., possibility 
of EU-wide OPEX and CAPEX support; simplification of IPCEI) - both for retrofitting and upgrading existing 
plants and for new plants. In this context, financial support should not be limited to strategic or 
technology-specific projects. 
 Instead of a confusing arrangement of funding pots and instruments as in the past, we call for the 
establishment of a long -term, clear, lean  and efficient European fund by standardizing and also 
reallocating existing EU funds.  A stronger concentration of instruments at the EU level would 
additionally promote the efficiency gains envisaged in the NZIA and contribute to maintaining a level 
playing field between EU member states in the internal market.  Moreover, fewer and more 
pragmatically designed incentive instruments at the EU level as a framework condition promote reliable 
and stable expectation formation among all economic actors involved. 
 It is evident that an appropriate subsidy structure for the period of upscaling of Net-Zero 
technologies must be guaranteed to ensure the competitiveness of the Austrian and European industry. 
 
Chapter III: CO2 injection capacity 
 For cost and resource efficiency reasons, CO2 cycles in industry should be promoted and cross-sector 
projects facilitated. Therefore, a start must be made quickly on setting up a needs -based connection 
to a European CO2 infrastructure. 
 The obligation on oil and gas producers to fulfill a certain quota of CO2 storage in the future also raises 
the questions of whether sufficient storage capacity will be available across Europe by 2030 and whether 
the infrastructure will be in place by 2030 to transport the captured CO 2 to the storage sites.  We 
therefore call fo r clarification of what consequences can be expected if this obligation cannot be 
met. 
 Corporate data needs to be protected . Companies that are active in the field of prospecting, 
exploration or production of hydrocarbons should be obliged, among other thi ngs, to hand over all 
geological data from those production sites that have been shut down or whose shutdown has been 
announced to the relevant authorities. The existing geological data are privately owned by companies 
that have acquired them at their own expense and risk. The transfer and/or publication of such data 
may only take place after agreement of the companies concerned. 
 The extent to which countries with existing prohibition laws (currently also still the case in Germany) 
and landlocked countries without corresponding storage options can make their contribution to the 
Europe-wide target of annual storage of 50 million tons from 2030 onwards should be regulated in detail. 
­ In the case of Austria as a landlocked country, as well as the 2011 law banning geological 
storage of CO2 in force on its territory, there is a massive distortion of competition due

to the unique situation created by the proposed CCS obligation. Accordingly, there must 
be an exemption for those companies covered by the obligation  that have no 
possibility to comply with the obligation in this member country due to the applicable 
national legal situation. 
­ In Austria, only geological storage facilities that do not exceed a volume of 100,000 tons 
and serve research purposes are exempt from this ban. Consequently, no geological CO2 
storage facilities exist in this country on a relevant scale. Storage potentials of the 
necessary size are also not to be expected. Of greater relevance for Austria is therefore 
the development of a Europe -wide infrastructure  to ensure the establishment of 
sustainable carbon cycles. 
 
Chapter IV: Access to markets 
 
 In the NZIA, public authorities are encouraged to consider sustainability and resilience criteria for Net-
Zero technologies in public procurement or auctions. Austrian companies are increasingly active in 
"sustainability and resilience markets" and thus definitely have competitive advantages. However, 
corresponding criteria must always be developed with a sense of  proportion. It is evident that 
additional quality criteria also mean additional costs for the public sector and also require additional 
know-how and competencies. 
 The aspect of Europe's resilience as a quality criterion must be given special importance in  security 
policy terms. Above all, IT for controlling the energy supply, which to date is often not produced  in 
Europe, poses a cyber security threat with macroeconomic implications. Affected technologies include 
inverters or control systems for the energy  system, which must therefore also benefit from the 
simplifications and funding opportunities of the NZIA. Possible "Buy European" concepts envisaged in the 
NZIA should therefore urgently take the security policy dimension into account. 
 
Chapter V: Enhancing skills for quality job creation 
Given the fact of the shortage of manpower in many European countries - especially in Austria - 
in professions and qualifications that are necessary for the ecological change and the achievement 
of the climate goals, we support the initiative of the EC to provide additional financial resources 
so that the Member States can offer education and training and the acquisition of complementary 
qualifications in the mentioned Net-Zero technologies to the extent needed. 
 We criticize that, despite the fact that the European Commission is not responsible in this policy field, 
a regulation with direct legal force proposes "European" degrees that do not take into account the 
diversity of national (vocational) education systems. We reject  the establishment of a para llel 
structure of European "Net-Zero Industry Academies" besides the existing forms of vocational training 
and continuing vocational training in the Member States. 
 The development of stand-alone, exclusively technology-related European Occupation Profiles does not 
seem appropriate and must in any case take into account the national vocational education and 
training systems (e.g. the corresponding apprenticeship occupations, the range of higher vocational 
education and vocational schools). 
 The proposed regulation gives rise to the assumption that a European Net-Zero Industry Academy is to 
be created for each Net-Zero technology. We request clarification as to whether this is actually what

is meant. We do not consider this approach to be useful and support the creation of Industry Academies 
according to expertise and not strictly separated according to technology. 
 We do not consider the establishment of sector -specific European Skills Academies for each Net-
Zero technology to be appropriate, since the demand for labor in the designated Net-Zero technology 
sectors does not only relate to skills that can be directly derived from technology, but also to various 
qualifications. 
 It is not clear which institutions can act as European Net-Zero Industry Academies. We caution against 
leaving the development of learning content for Net-Zero technologies purely to universities. Regarding 
labor market relevance and the professional usability of the acquired competencies, the social partners 
and vocational training institutions must be equal partners of the universities . If possible, learning 
outcomes should also be able to be acquired by way of dual training. 
 The vocational training system in many member states is based on dual vocational training with its 
training companies. It is therefore recommended to further develop the relevant occupational profiles 
and the training curricula in secondary and vocational schools, the range of courses offered by 
universities of applied sciences and universities on a broad front and to make this further development, 
for which there is a lot of experience in industry and in the vocational training sector, the starting point 
for European support. The involvement of industry and the social partners is indispensable for this 
to ensure labor market orientation. 
 Furthermore, it should also be possible to use the additional funds for in -house training  (higher 
qualification of employees) since the companies themselves know best the type and scope of the 
qualifications required. These funds could also be used to additionally finance company-based training 
and further education of unemployed people  in green skills, since company -based training achieves 
the highest integration successes on the primary labor market. 
 The training and continuing education programs in green skills must be attractive and suitable for the 
entire workforce, such as women and older people, to obtain sufficient participants for these training 
programs. It will be particularly important to acquire the skills relevant to the energy transition through 
vocational training and continuing education for employees, to increase the proportion of women in 
these training programs, and to provide in-company training for unemployed people. 
 The Net-Zero industry sectors  are highly concentrated and in part new industries that have little 
experience in the qualification of their employees. For industries to be able to compete, young and new 
industries must also build on a European value chain. It must therefore be ensured that the qualification 
needs of supplier companies along the value chain  and those of the relevant innovation drivers are 
considered. 
 The creation of pan -European academies may be useful in the higher education sector on a selective 
basis. In this context, the academies proposed under this regulation should also consider the already 
existing European higher education offer , e.g. the EIT Deep Tech Initiative and the European 
Universities. 
 Microcredentials relevant to Net-Zero technologies must also be able to be awarded by vocational 
training institutions and by no means be reserved exclusively for universities. This additionally ensures 
their market value and is also in line with the Council Recommendation on a European approach to 
micro-credentials for lifelong learning and employability, which also explicitly provides for vocational 
training providers as providers of microcredentials.

 In our opinion, the inclusion of microcredentials for Net-Zero technologies in the Austrian National 
Qualifications Framework (NQF)  is only justified if the learning outcomes encompassed by the MC 
address at least the majority of the NQF descriptors of the l evel sought. Where, on the other hand, an 
MC covers highly specialized skills in a very small way, an assignment to the NQF seems to us neither 
possible nor meaningful. 
 Member states are obliged to determine every two years, for the first time by December 31, 2024, 
whether the learning programs developed by the European Net-Zero Academies correspond to the 
specific qualifications required by the respective member state  for access to regulated activities 
within the scope of occupations of special interest to  the Net-Zero industry. In our opinion, such a 
matching should be carried out for Austria by the Austrian Economic Chamber wherever occupations 
relevant to the Net-Zero industry are affected by the trade regulations. 
 Requiring member states to recognize a microcredential as sufficient evidence of a formal qualification 
wherever that MC has been found to match the qualifications required for entry into the profession 
risks, in our view, over-liberalizing access to regulated professions essential to Net-Zero industries. 
 We warn that, with the understandable intention of addressing the pressing shortage of skilled workers 
in these technologies as quickly as possible, the req uirements for the qualifications of these skilled 
workers are being leveled down too far. 
 
Chapter VI: Innovation 
 
Like the EC, we believe it is essential that technologies that are not (yet) ready for the market 
must also be eligible for funding under the NZIA. It is envisaged that these will be promoted 
through so-called regulatory sandboxes. 
 
 Research and innovation should be given at least as much priority in the NZIA as market -ready 
technologies. This must also be reflected in the funding of the NZIA, which still needs to be clarified.  
 We welcome the establishment of regulatory sandboxes as an instrument of innovation policy for the 
testing of innovative new technologies, products and services that is limited in terms of content, time 
and space. 
 In the view of the WKÖ, the NZIA must take a bottom-up approach to the establishment of regulatory 
sandboxes: the need on the part of the business community is the decisive criterion for the sensible use 
of sandboxes. 
 When setting up regulatory sandboxes, particular attention must be paid to the following : 
1. Transparent and uniform selection process. 
2. Clear and uniform procedural regulations. 
3. Testing limited in terms of content, time, and, if necessary, space - no "eternal" exception, no 
"regulatory vacations ", but the possibility of extension according to narrow, predefined 
criteria. 
4. Ensuring competitive neutrality and thus a leve l playing field. Innovative new technologies, 
products and services are to be promoted, not certain characteristics of companies such as age 
or size. 
5. Competent authority or intermediaries such as funding agencies act as a companion, not as a 
supervisory body.  
6. Ongoing evaluation and transparent presentation  of the results and regulatory findings of 
regulatory sandboxes. 
- 7 - 
7. Clear perspectives and rules for the "transition" to normal operation.  
8. The guidance announced by the EU Commission for the first half of 2023 must be considered.  
9. Regulatory sandboxes are innovation policy instruments , not instruments for deregulation or 
the dismantling of safety, protection or qualification standards. 
10. If existing programs (e.g. funding programs) offer corresponding points o f contact, synergies 
should be used for implementation.  
11. When designing a regulatory sandbox, the limits of European and constitutional law must be 
observed, in particular the principle of legality and the principle of equality. 
 
Chapter VII: Governance 
A Net-Zero Europe Platform will help the Commission and MS to coordinate actions and exchange 
information, including in the context of Net-Zero industry partnerships. Among the topics 
addressed will be funding from private investors and public finance. 
 For the WKÖ, in general, the following principle applies: Should the proposed economic policy objective 
lead to a centralization of competencies and administration at the European level, the principles of 
subsidiarity and proportionality must be taken into account . Any kind of expert participation must 
ensure the involvement of the industries concerned. 
 In our view, it is crucial that the Net-Zero Europe Platform really relieves companies of administrative 
and bureaucratic burdens. It must have a coordinating and informing function and thus provide added 
value for the educational and research institutions and industries concerned by promoting cooperation 
and exploiting synergy effects. Under no circumstances must this result in excessive additional reporting 
obligations and additional administrative work for the companies. 
 We do welcome targeted Net-Zero partnerships. But this goal must only be seen as one part of an overall 
ambitious EU trade agenda . The latter must improve access to low -carbon and economically viable 
energy and raw materials at globally competitive costs. This goal should be supported by targeted 
policies, trade and strategic partnership agreements, and a global supply of raw materials.

ePURE’s feedback to the Commission’s review proposal 
for heavy-duty vehicles CO2 emissions standards 
 
• ePURE, the European renewable ethanol producers association, supports the Commission’s aim to revise the CO2 
emissions standards for heavy -duty vehicles, and to strengthen CO 2 emissions reduction targets in line with the 
EU’s 2030 overall GHG emission target of at least -55% and with the climate neutrality objective for 2050.  
• Particularly, the EU should aim to curb emissions from new heavy-duty vehicles in a cost-effective way that would 
reduce the EU consumption of imported fossil fuels to make the best use of domestic renewable solutions , while 
maintaining the EU ’s technical and industrial leadership in powertrain technology. Under the principle of 
technology openness, all technological solutions (such as ED95 1 trucks which reduce emissions up to 90% 
compared to diesel and cost as much as a diesel vehicle) to support the decarbonisation of heavy -duty vehicles 
should be recognised and incentivised based on their GHG emissions reduction potential, their cost-effectiveness, 
and their availability.  
• However, this does not appear to be the objective of the Commission s ince the adopted proposal keeps the 
skewed tailpipe approach for the accounting of CO2 emissions from new vehicles. By focusing only on the tank-to-
wheel part of the vehicles ’ lifecycle, the methodology fails to account for the full environmental footprint and 
ultimately perpetuates the bias for the electrification of road transport  regardless of the societal and 
environmental cost. 
o The issue does not lie in the stringency of targets but in the methodology. Since current targets only consider 
tailpipe emissions, the approach is biased/fails to account for the full environmental footprint of the vehicle. 
EV are misleadingly labelled as ‘zero -emission’, distorting competition between technologies: it is the worst 
possible approach for op erators who will be faced with soaring costs. Forcing all new HDV to be supposedly 
ZEV by a certain date would exclude ICE and with -it renewable fuels. Under the principle of technology 
neutrality all solutions, incl. renewable fuel -powered ICE should be a ble to support HDV decarbonisation 
based on their GHG-reduction potential, cost-effectiveness, and availability. Targets should be formulated to 
represent the full life-cycle emissions of putting a new vehicle on the road i.e., account for the WTW emissions 
of the energy carrier, distinguish between fossil and biogenic CO 2, and consider the production/end of life of 
the vehicle. 
o The best approach to incentivise the best technologies is to properly assess them on their emission 
performance and build trust fr om consumers/operators. Decarbonizing HDV is a challenge and requires all 
available and suitable options to better adapt to the variety of consumers/operators across the EU. 
Benchmarks, mandates, or any form of incentives pre -empting market shares to certa in powertrain 
technologies penalize the deployment of other efficient solutions and in fine hinder the decarbonisation of 
transport. The CO 2 standards should be designed to incentivise all genuinely efficient alternatives including 
powertrains running on low Well-To-Tank emissions/high biogenic content energy carriers. If grounded on a 
solid and non-discriminatory methodology, the CO2 standards could provide the critical push to encourage all 
alternative fuels to improve their environmental performance. 
o Trust from consumers and operators is paramount. Decarbonising HDV is a challenge and requires all available 
and suitable options to adapt to the variety of consumers/operators. Any incentives pre -empting market 
shares to certain technologies penalize the dep loyment of other solutions/hinder the decarbonisation of 
transport. With a solid/non -discriminatory methodology, the standards could provide the critical push to 
powertrains running on low WTT emissions/high biogenic content fuels.

• Before a WTW methodology can be implemented, an incentive for the uptake of all sustainable and available 
renewable fuels such as renewable ethanol should be introduced. A dedicated mechanism to incentivise OEMs to 
sell a wider range of cheaper a nd efficient technologies to better match operator’s needs and finances such as 
ICEs running on low carbon fuels. 
• Such mechanism should reflect the carbon intensity and share of the fuels used and distinguish fossil from biogenic 
CO2. Consistency between EU legislations is paramount, and all sustainable fuels certified under the RED II should 
be incentivised. 
• In the initial public consultation, more than two -thirds of the respondents agreed that such a mechanism, either 
a carbon correction factor (CCF) or a crediting scheme for fuels, should be introduced. Many stakeholders stressed 
the positive impact of such a mechanism to make more renewable and low -carbon fuels available for road 
transport, without compromising on the carbonisation of other transport sec tors such as maritime and aviation. 
However, the legislative proposal does not include any provisions regarding renewable fuels in heavy -duty while 
a superficial Impact Assessment of these questions was presented by the Commission.  
Debunking of Impact Assessment 
• In a joint statement with European industries representing automotive suppliers, vehicle manufacturers, fuel 
manufacturers, retailers, dealers and repairers, we shared our doubts and concerns about the quality of the 
Commission’s Impact Assessment u nderpinning the legislative proposal. Many key elements in support of the 
proposal were excluded from the assessment or have not been sufficiently explained, while fundamental data 
were inconsistently reported. Thus, ePURE has elected to take a constructiv e approach to ensure an informed 
debate take place between the EU institutions when discussing the proposal.  
• Among the questions and remarks that were raised in the industry’s joint request for clarification, ePURE wishes 
to highlight the following: 
o Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) is presented as the main argument in favour of hydrogen fuel cell and battery 
electric trucks over renewable fuel ICE vehicles. A thorough analysis of the Impact Assessment showed that 
there are critical oversights on the economic i mpact of switching to electric trucks and many assumptions 
were taken without transparent supporting evidence. Indeed, the price assumptions for batteries, fuel cells 
and renewable fuels are missing, and price sensitivity analysis reflect only electricity costs. It is not clear how 
the infrastructure costs are reflected in the comparison between renewable fuels and electric options. The 
replacement rate of components such as the battery is assumed to be a very optimistic one over the lifetime 
of an electric truck, leading to great impact in favour of the TCO of an electric truck.  
o How exactly did the Commission conclude that the CCF and recognition of CO 2 reduction coming from 
renewable fuels will not bring any economic benefits to transport operators and the society as a whole?  
Many critical aspects of the analysis of the CCF mechanism are missing, including the data of the TCO and 
vehicle costs, or the underlying assumptions that led to the conclusion  of the Commission that the 
introduction of a CCF mechanism would be equivalent to less ambitious CO 2 reduction levels. 
o Why did the assessment of renewable fuels options only include advanced biofuels and RFNBOs, instead of 
all the renewable fuels as defin ed in the RED II? More than 50% of the renewable energy consumed in 
transport today in the EU is crop -based biofuels. The decision to not include them in the Impact Assessment 
undermines the assessment of the overall decarbonisation potential of all renewable fuels for heavy -duty 
road transport, as well as their cost-effectiveness and immediate availability.

We, Gas Distributors for Sustainability, firmly commit to the decarbonization of transport sector  
notably in an energy mix where each alternative fuel has a role to play  including biomethane 
(bioNGV). To achieve the greenhouse gas emission reduction target s, set by the Green Deal, the 
European Commission must promote all technologies that can effectively decarbonise road 
transport. 
Relying on one type of solution is not a good strategy to sustainably reach our climate 
goals 
While we fully recognise the benefits that these electric-based solutions can offer - provided that 
the primary energy used is renewable or low carbon - many ‘zero tailpipe emission’ technologies 
rely on critical natural resources such as rare earth materials, largely imported from outside of the 
EU. Other solutions, such as internal combustion engine vehicles running on biomethane, are 
much less exposed to technology-related supply chain constraints or vulnerability. Switching from 
a dependence on fossil energy to a dependence on impo rted rare earths and critical metal and 
components will not increase the competitiveness of the EU economy and may even undermine it.  
Considering this point, the proposal's Impact Assessment (IA) lacks clarity on key assumptions 
related to delivering its ambition. It appears that the total amount of electricity needed for zero 
tailpipe emissions trucks proposed has been significantly underestimated. There is no clear 
indication that the IA even accounted for the electricity demand for the assumed – and hopefully 
renewable – hydrogen consumption. 
Zero tailpipe emission technologies alone will not be sufficient to decarbonise a hard-to-
abate sector 
Moreover, relying on ‘zero tailpipe emission’ technologies will not be sufficient to decarbonise 
heavy-duty tran sport. This sector is both technically and logistically difficult to electrify, as it 
requires powerful engines capable of covering long distances while carrying a large payload. 
Different solutions will be needed and implemented in parallel to cover the different needs of the 
market and diversity of consumer needs : while electric trucks will probably be used in most 
applications up to 500 km, other alternatives may be relevant for other use cases, like long distance 
transport needs.

Thus, limiting our decarbonisation strategies to technologies that are not yet sufficiently mature 
and affordable to meet the needs of transport operators (especially trucks and commercial 
vehicles) would mean del aying a much-needed transition for a sector where GHG emissions are 
particularly hard to abate. Indeed, as the purchasing costs of EVs will remain very high for the next 
decades, promoting this solution only would require massive support of public finances, which are 
financial resources that all Member States have at a critical time for the European economy.  
Going beyond tailpipe approach is necessary to reach our climate objectives 
The proposal does not demonstrate the climate benefits of its tailpipe-only approach. The Impact 
Assessment fails to fully detail how the proposed tailpipe CO2 reduction targets actually translate 
into CO2 reductions on a more complete well-to-wheel (WTW) or lifecycle approaches. In addition, 
the Impact Assessment would benefit to detail the CO 2 reductions resulting from a policy option 
acknowledging the fuels contribution to offer a fair basis of comparison. 
A tailpipe -only approach is also not consistent with other EU legislation supporting 
decarbonisation of transport, namely the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) and the Energy 
Taxation Directive (ETD). Recognised by the RED Directive, biomethane can be us ed as a 
renewable fuel, contributing to CO2 emission levels in Life Cycle Analysis comparable to emissions 
from electric and hydrogen vehicles  using renewable energy. Already mature for use in Europe, 
biomethane supports the development of local circular economies as it is generated from organic 
residues and locally produced waste streams. It thus makes a concrete contribution to the energy 
independence of Europe. 
It is therefore necessary to keep some room for manoeuvre with complementary yet effective 
solutions, especially as BioNGV is a proven reliable and competitive solution to deliver as of today 
immediate benefits to market players.

In France, as it has the biggest road network in Europe, the development of gas vehicles has 
become a major issue for the country as it strives to meet the objectives set by the Energy Transition 
Law. At national level, European goals favouring gas are broken down into specific guidelines and 
manifested in concrete terms in sustainable mobility. Today, 28,000 vehicles are running on 
bioCNG in France, half of them are heavy-duty vehicles, and the market is still growing. 
Such potential has been recognised by the European Commission in its RePowerEU 
communication, which set a production target of 35 bcm for 2030. It is therefore time to create the 
proper regulatory framework to boost the uptake of this renewable gas in all sectors, including 
road transport. Indeed, in recent years, the use of biometh ane in road transport has gained 
momentum in Europe, with many countries incentivizing its production and use: enough 
sustainable feedstocks are thus available in the EU-27 to meet the 35 bcm target.

How to improve the proposal? 
A tailpipe-only approach does not reflect the actual GHG performance of powertrain technologies 
when running on fuels with a lower GHG footprint than their unabated fossil -based counterparts. 
To address this, a dedicated mechanism acknowledging the contrib ution of lower carbon fuels 
must be included in the proposal.  
We thus fully support the introduction of a robust and transparent carbon correction factor for 
renewable and low -carbon fuels . Such a factor would help to bring additional volumes of 
renewable and low carbon fuels to the market, which in turn would decrease the CO2 emissions of 
existing and new vehicles and contribute to ambitions on climate, REPowerEU and NZIA. The core 
principle of such a carbon correction factor should be to consider that a portion of the CO 2 
emissions of a heavy-duty vehicle should be offset to reflect the current level of renewable and low-
carbon fuels in the fuels mix. 
A CCF would be easy to introduce in the Regulation, with minimal overhead, based on reliable 
official data, and would ensure no double counting/false claims. 
Therefore, we, Gas Distributors for Sustainability, call on the European institutions and policy 
makers to support the following recommendations: 
 
 
 
 
 
Investing in BioNGV means relying on a solution available today and in the long term to 
decarbonise, preserve air quality and meet the needs of transporters. 
Going beyond a pure ‘tailpipe’ approach 
A tailpipe approach only is insufficient to capture the positive externalities of 
biomethane as a fuel. It is crucial to create a truly holistic approach, which is required 
to achieve carbon neutrality of road transport by drawing on the benefits of all available 
sustainable decarbonisation solutions. 
 
Focus on phasing out fossil fuels by introducing a carbon correction 
factor 
Progressively replace fossil fuels in mobility with renewable and low-carbon gases and 
sustainably produced biofuels, as assessed in the Renewable Energy Directive. A 
carbon correction factor  should be provided for renewable fuels to customers and 
users, who demand solutions that meet all their operational constraints and 
competitiveness criteria, no matter the technology.

SEV’s Comments on the European Commission’s proposal for the reform of 
the electricity market design 
 
The upcoming reform of the EU’s electricity market design must build on 
strengthening the competitiveness of Europe while pursuing a green transition and 
ensuring that this transition is fair. 
As a starting point, it is imperative that this reform is preceded by a comprehensive 
impact assessment to ensure that any change is fit for purpose, acknowledges 
member states’ different situations with regards to their energy mix while maintaining 
the benefits of the internal market and boosting further integration. To this end, the 
reform should seek an evolution, not a dismantling, of the market, maintaining 
regulatory certainty and mature market fundamentals, including marginal pricing for 
spot price setting; the market design isn’t behind the price crisis and replacing it in a 
hasty manner may be disastrous, even more so, if done in a fragmented way.  
Against this background, the following remarks on the reform of the European 
electricity market design must be considered: 
1. Long-term instruments: It is positive that marginal pricing for short-term 
markets remains and that there is focus on promoting long-term contracts 
(and thus reducing the role of short-term markets in price setting).  
In particular, PPAs is an important tool for an industrial user because it allows 
to take a long position in the market. This way, the end user can diversify a 
part of its electricity cost. In Greece, the market caps are an obstacle for both 
RES producers and off-takers to strike a PPA agreement. Among other 
things, an exception mechanism should be adopted for financial PPAs with 
industrial users as off-takers. Furthermore, allowing well-designed de-risking 
mechanisms for PPAs (beyond guarantee schemes addressing off-taker 
payment default), is critical to boost this market. 
Regarding two-way CFDs, in Greece, those for inframarginal units especially 
for RES units have been already in place for several years. However, the 
industrial end users have not benefited from the mechanism. A proposal

could be to implement part (ex. 50%-50%) of the new projects over CFDs and 
another part as PPAs and this way combine the benefits of two worlds.  
2. Forward markets: There are several tools already in place, however, the 
needed liquidity is missing. In any case, those tools are for more 
sophisticated market participants than the industry sector. Given that forward 
markets represent a hedging mechanism, regulatory certainty as regards the 
rules in the spot markets is essential, to allow forward markets to mature and 
acquire depth.  
3. Temporary market interventions: It is necessary to phase out the 
emergency measures so that the market normality will be reestablished -in 
order to achieve the ambitious EU climate and energy targets. The 
emergency measures should be phased out without delay especially in 
member states like Greece where such measures do not bring any additional 
tangible benefit regarding the consumers’ support (due to the existing very 
strong dominance of the two-way CfDs). 
4. Flexibility: The draft EU Regulation should be adjusted in a way that 
considers all the different flexibility needs and available options to TSOs. 
Specifically, the large penetration of RES in the Greek energy system (also 
the case for several other MS) during the current decade will render 
necessary the provision of different types of flexibility services to the system, 
which (a) cannot be adequately addressed exclusively through specific 
flexibility providers, and (b) may require the implementation of more targeted 
and fitting flexibility mechanisms. To this end, for certain MS, mechanisms 
targeting flexibility provided by e.g. hydroelectric capacity and fast ramping 
gas fired plants may be needed to cover particular needs, while separate 
flexibility schemes aimed at tapping the full potential of DR and storage may 
be needed to cover different flexibility needs of the grid.  
Against this background, in order to facilitate the integration of an increasing 
share of renewable generation into the electricity system, flexible back up 
generation is required and should be able to provide flexibility services 
through targeted flexibility mechanisms designed for low CO2 fossil fuel 
plants. 
5. Fixed price contracts: The suggestion to include a requirement that 
suppliers offer fixed price contracts restricts suppliers’ autonomy in the 
market and their ability to have their offer portfolios reflecting customers’ 
needs.

6. Access to affordable energy during an electricity price crisis: Targeted 
public interventions in price setting for the supply of electricity (article 66a) 
must be applied to all vulnerable consumers and not only to small and 
medium sized enterprises. There should be an explicit reference to the 
possibility/obligation of the Member States, in the event of a price crisis, to 
apply measures for all particularly affected customers, including electro-
intensive enterprises. 
Overall, it seems that the proposed revision of the rules for electricity market design 
will provide a framework, but a number of changes and implementation strategies will 
be left to Member States, such as direct price support schemes and flexibility support 
schemes.

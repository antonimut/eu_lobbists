A LONG-TERM ELECTRICITY MARKET DESIGN FOR 
SECURE, SUSTAINABLE AND AFFORDABLE ELECTRICITY 
 
The reform of the Electricity Market Design (EMD) is a n opportunity to improve 
consumers’ ability to benefit from the roll-out of renewables and at the same time to 
provide confidence to investors in meeting the European decarbonisation goals.  
In principle, many features of the current EMD have proven themselves and  are 
worth preserving. But targeted amendments to the market design are necessary to 
provide sufficient investment signals for renewable as well as flexible, CO 2-free 
generation.  
Therefore, we  welcome the balanced proposal of the European Commission . In 
particular, it is good that the Commission  
▪ preserves the fundamentals of the market design,  
▪ aims to facilitate new investments via two -way Contracts for Difference  
(CfDs) and Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs). 
▪ Does not foresee to systematically limit inframarginal revenues for electricity 
generators and  
Creating a stable and predictable investment framework  is key for renewable  
generation and CO2-free firm capacity . The quick and massive build -out of clean 
electricity is the only wa y to reduce scarcity and thus ensure affordable prices for 
households and industry.  
 
Key Recommendations 
What should be done: 
1. Expansion of Renewable Energies  and Ensuring Affordability via Two-way 
Contracts for Difference (CfDs) and PPAs 
Bilateral CfDs for new built of renewable energies can avoid economically unnecessary 
investment risks and ensure that  political targets are met . We thus welcome the clear 
focus on CfDs for direct  price support measures for new assets. Applying mandatory 
CfDs for existing assets would mean an intervention in commercial contracts between 
market participants which can b y no means be justified as it would make the principle of 
an electricity market obsolete and would severely harm investments.  
Regarding new assets, we question the concept to allow the participation of projects which 
reserve parts of their electricity for PPAs also in CfD auctions . This may lead to market 
distortions and potential winner’s curse : As a result, not the bidder with the lowest cost 
would win a CfD auction, but the bidder with the highest PPA price expectation. Therefore, 
the CfD route and the merchant route via PPA – which are both equally important for the 
future electricity market – should be kept separately from each other. The market share

2. Incentives for Firm Capacity with Capacity Remuneration Mechanisms 
(CRMs) as Permanent Feature of the EMD 
The necessity of firm capacity for the energy transition is widely recognised – given that 
the electricity demand is rising, the expansion of renewable energies must be accelerated 
and the phase-out of coal shall be expedited. However, this will only be possible if H2-fired 
or H2-ready power plants , with their flexible power generation, supplement renewable 
energies in order to maintain the security of supply.  
 
Only CRMs ensure sufficient and timely investments in firm capacity 1 and this must 
also be taken into account in the EMD: At the moment, the European electricity market 
design foresees CRMs only temporary and as a measure of last resort. However, they are 
an important pillar of market design for the decades to come and should be treated as 
such. The Electricity Market Regulation should be amended accordingly. By doing so, 
flexibility and demand response could also be strengthened as part of a CRM. This would 
make it unnecessary to establish a new flexibility support scheme (see point 6).  
 
In order to achieve the EU’s climate targets, a clear CO2 emission limit for CRMs is 
indispensible. In the future, the existing threshold might even be lowered so that it requires 
almost CO2 -free secure power plants, e.g. by being H2-ready or CCS-capable. 
 
3. Securing the Offshore Wind Development with Transmission Access 
Guarantees (TAGs) 
We welcome the introduction of TAGs as they will de-risk investments in offshore hybrid 
projects which is necessary to make these cross-border projects commercially viable. This 
is an important step for offshore wind farms with grid connections to several countries – 
they have the potential to save space and societal costs by pooling generation and 
transmission assets.  
The concrete design of TAGs should be developed carefully, in order to have a targeted 
and reliable compensation . Being grid related the compensation should not be financed 
via the direct support scheme (CfD ) of the wind installation but should be reimbursed via 
the Transmission System Operators  (like the redispatch).  In this context the additional 
congestion income which is  earned on the interconnector due to the  restriction of the 
transmission asset should be  used to co -finance the compensation. To enable such 
commensurate use of congestion income a minor change to the proposed amendment 
Art. 19 EMD is necessary. The compensation mechanism itself and any residual funding 
itself needs specification in subsequent regulation. It should be kept in mind, that while the 
TAG de-risking is a critical perquisite to enable investments it cannot guarantee 
investments in any case by itself. In cases where the revenue level in an offshore bidding 
zone is fundamentally too low, further measures need to be considered by cooperating 
member states.

What should be avoided: 
4. No Prolongation or Perpetuation of the Inframarginal Revenue Cap 
The revenue cap for so -called inframarginal technologies (mainly renewables) was 
designed and implemented in an extraordinary emergency situation. It must remain a 
temporary mechanism. Prolonging this measure, or even worse: making it a permanent 
feature of the market design, would cause severe problems for the electricity market 
and especially the investors’ confidence.  
The revenue cap is a strong intervention in the electricity market and the rights of the 
electricity producers. If this measure would be transposed into a permanent feature of the 
regulatory framework, investments into these inframa rginal technologies and especially 
into renewables will become very unattractive and thus unavoidably slow down. This would 
severely harm the green transition  which requires massive and rapid investments in 
renewable and low-carbon energy, grids and storage. In addition, negative consequences 
of revenue limitations for an efficient dispatch and thus the proper functioning of short -
term markets cannot be ruled out. Thus, unintended effects, including on ma rket 
liquidity and ultimately security of supply, will very likely keep occurring. 
 
5. Peak Shaving Products Risk Distorting the Market and Price Formation 
The introduction of peak shaving products by the TSOs risks distorting the price building 
mechanism in the electricity market and would lead to additional costs for consumers. As 
such a solution is not technolo gy-neutral and discriminatory against electricity 
producers, the results will always be less efficient than those of measures like CRMs , 
balancing activation or intraday purchase s. In addition, the discrimination against 
electricity producers will further weaken the business case for plants which only run in peak 
hours. Harming investments in these plants is ultimately an unnecessary threat to security 
of supply. 
 
6. Flexibility Support Mechanisms  Risk the further deployment of storage and 
demand response 
The possibility for member states to introduce flexi bility support schemes  might harm 
investments in demand response and storage until the final implementation in member 
states, as it shall only be eligible for new -built installations. In the expe ctation of an 
implementation in the future  and the corresponding payments, investors will postpone 
their investments. This will jeopardize  especially the much needed further deployment of  
storage. In addition, a flexibility support scheme is not technology -neutral and 
discriminatory against elelctricity generation which can provide flexibility as well. Thus, 
capacity mechanisms are the much better option (see point 3).

7. Regional Virtual Hubs Risk Draining Liquidity from well-established Market 
Places 
 The mandatory introduction of virtual hubs and ENTSO -E’s and ACER’s role in designing 
them is problematic as it does not appear to address the needs of market participants. 
Where market participants have seen value in virtual hubs, their development has already 
been initiated together with power exchanges and TSOs. Mandating such hubs across 
Europe will at best have no effect and at worst cause a reduction in liquidity on the existing 
hubs. This would be counterproductive as it would actually reduce hedging opportunities.

Edison’s response to the consultation on the proposal to reform the Union’s Electricity Market Design 
 
As a leading player in the electricity sector, Edison shares a strong interest in guaranteeing electricity system 
stability through a solid  market design. Edison is furthermore  committed to strengthen its pathway to 
decarbonization through an investment plan on energy transition amounting to 10 billion euros by 2030, of 
which 5 billion euros to achieve a three -fold increase in installed renew able capacity and another 5 billion 
euros on energy efficiency, storage and other decarbonization solutions. Edison recognizes the central role 
of the electricity market design in facilitating the deployment of renewable energy, including by means of 
backup and integration solutions. 
 
The European Commission’s proposal to reform the electricity market design appears balanced and suited 
to simultaneously achieve the objectives of softening price volatility and providing long- term signals for 
investments on decarbonization. Edison supports the decision not to overhaul the functioning of short-term 
markets, which have been recognized as having a key role in providi ng effective dispatching and balancing, 
as well as favoring cross-border flows.  
 
Edison recognizes that there are areas of improvement in the proposal to facilitate new investments in 
renewables, storage and flexibility technologies more effectively.  
 
The reform aptly recognizes PPAs as the main market tool to reduce consumer exposure at times of price 
volatility and to facilitate new investments . Another positive step for the development of renewables are  
the provisions to increase network information sharing by TSOs . The proposal could be strengthened  
through a further specification of the type of information that the TSO must make public to RES  project 
developers (e.g. planned network reinforcements, saturation of stations and substations  and expiration 
dates of connection requests). 
 
Edison supports the promotion of two-way contracts for difference (CfDs) for new capacity. This will allow 
stable remuneration to RES plants with redistribution obligations if prices rise, without retroactively 
changing the remuneration systems of existing plants . Edison believes that CfDs should remain a 
complementary tool to market-based instruments. Excessive reliance on CfDs risks reducing the importance 
of the spot price, with potential distortions on price signals and liquidity. 
 
Edison welcomes the attention given to flexibility needs through the i ntroduction of national flexibility 
assessments and the setting of indicative targets. In this regard, we believe it is necessary for the proposal 
to offer more space to sector coupling solutions between the electricity, gas and hydrogen sectors . In 
addition, considering the references to po ssible evolutions of capacity mechanisms , we reiterate the 
importance of continuing to consider these mechanisms first and foremost as system adequacy tools . 
Capacity mechanisms play a key role in preserving the security of systems that are increasingly su bject to 
deployment of variable renewables. We therefore believe it is necessary for these mechanisms to be made 
a permanent part of the electricity market design. 
 
The desire to offer access to fixed -price contracts and to require adequate levels of hedgi ng is 
understandable, although on the definition of offers it would be preferable to leave room for the market. We 
point out that the  proposed measure does not foresee instruments aimed at strengthening consumers' 
contractual commitment to fixed contracts . In this context, it is important that there is an appropriate 
balance of contractual commitments  with the aim of preventing early termination  and ensuring the 
enforcement of clauses if early termination occurs. In this context, the publication of non-binding

guidelines on how operators should calculate termination fees should be considered. Indeed, according to 
European regulations, it is the supplier's duty to quantify and demonstrate the cost incurred from early 
termination of the contract, which in tu rn determines the penalty that the customer will have to pay. 
However, this quantification exercise is particularly challenging, especially when it comes to estimating the 
costs of long-term hedging. 
 
On hedging obligations, however, we encourage further assessments on the introduction of  obligations to 
hedge a portion of risk exposure through PPA contracts . The article on supplier risk management should 
avoid references to specific prescriptive obligations about hedging strategies because European providers' 
portfolios vary significantly. Precise obligations could in fact be inadequate  for the risk profile of many 
providers, compromising their hedging strategy. 
 
The possible impact on the internal market of the article on the introduction of regulated price s, including 
regulated prices  below cost , should be examined with caution. We positively assess  the definition of 
prerequisites for declaring a price crisis, and of principles to protect the internal market (e.g., compensation, 
non-discrimination access, eligibility for all operators and limited duration of the measure itself) . However, 
we believe further clarifications are needed on the requirements to  trigger this measure in order to avoid 
divergent and fragmented interpretations across the EU. 
 
Finally, we point out the potential risks of applying a disconnection ban without carefully defining the scope 
of application. Indeed, this provision will have to be adjusted to the definition of vulnerability in the 
different Member States, in order to ensure support to the most impacted consumers (those in energy 
poverty) avoiding that the measure leads to disproportionate costs for the system.

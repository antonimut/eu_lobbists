Enel views on the proposal to reform the European electricity market 
 
On 14 March, the European Commission published the proposal to revise the electricity market design. 
The Enel Group welcomes many aspects of the proposal and would like to point out the following observations: 
▪ The reform is correctly focused on the promotion and development of long -term contractual 
instruments, as an element for achieving the so-called "decoupling", that is limiting the contagion of the 
price volatility of fossil commodities on the price of electricity and directly benefit final consumers from 
the energy transition. Following the same rationale and aiming at reducing retail market risks, the proposal 
also foresees the possibility to introduce long-term hedging obligations on electricity suppliers, which 
provide an interesting way to kickstart liquidity in long-term markets in the most coherent way with market 
logics. 
The development of liquidity for long -term products, and in particular Contracts for Differences - CfDs, 
Power Purchase Agreements - PPAs and forward markets, is indeed necessary to reduce the exposure of 
electricity consumers to fluctuations of natural ga s prices (i.e., to decouple). In fact, long -term 
contractualization favors the decarbonization of the electricity system through the development of new 
renewables and reduces the exposure of consumers to short-term prices, which are influenced by changes 
in gas prices. 
 
However, it is essential to underline that the necessary evolution of the European electricity market design 
must avoid the introduction of administered , mandatory and controlled mechanisms.  Hence, any 
solution proposed for the modification of the Electricity Regulation or Directive must be based on a 
competitive rationale and ensure adequate cost-reflectivity. 
 
Therefore, the development of long-term contracting must also be based on competitive mechanisms 
that allow the correct definition of prices. 
We welcome in the Commission proposal both the promotion of such instruments and the reference to 
long-term hedging obligations on electricity suppliers. The latter, if adequately aligned with appropriate 
suppliers' risk management t ools (e.g., cost -reflective contract termination fees), represent a favorable 
addition to better secure electricity retailers and consumers from possible unexpected market shocks and 
can act as a further incentive for the promotion of long-term contractual instruments and new investments 
in clean and efficient capacity.  
 
▪ Regarding short-term market reform proposals, we positively assess that the attention has been placed on 
the development of flexibility, without affecting the pillars of the functioning of these markets, which 
are essential for the correct operation of the  electricity system and to achieve the objectives of 
decarbonization, security and competitiveness. 
We welcome the safeguard of the essential characteristics of the electricity market (system marginal price  
for short-term market functioning; voluntariness and non-retroactivity of long-term contractual measures 
such as CfDs, as suggested by some proposals answering to the more dirigiste logic of intervention in the 
market) which guarantee the correct use of generation sources, adequate investment signals in the cleanest 
and most efficient forms of generation as well as guaranteeing the correct coordination of national 
electricity markets and cross-border electricity trading. 
We equally welcome the rejection of the extension of emergency measures such as a cap on revenues 
from inframarginal generators, which has been applied in recent months in a very uneven way 
across Member States. The latter is a measure that not only penalizes the much -needed investments in 
new generation capacity with low carbon emissions  but also investments already made, with possible 
heavy repercussions on the financial stability of operators of the sector.  
 
Lastly, the attention posed by the reform on the flexibility of electricity systems is surely positive, 
especially as the participation of flexibility assets in the markets has been limited so far, but it should also 
receive a boost through specific long -term instruments, currently missing in the proposal , not only for

Enel views on the proposal to reform the Regulation on Wholesale Energy 
Market Integrity and Transparency 
 
On 14 March, the European Commission also published the proposal to revise the Regulation on Wholesale 
Energy Market Integrity and Transparency.  
Enel Group welcomes this initiative, as a fruitful occasion to strengthen the REMIT regime and foster the 
supervision of wholesale energy markets, while taking account of market developments and lessons learnt in 
the last ten years. Considering that the current REMIT already provides a clear and robust framework to 
prevent market abuse and ensure confidence for co nsumers and market participants, a review should chiefly 
aim at addressing some issues and lessons learnt from such a decade of implementation, at clarifying some 
aspects of the regime and, overall, at streamlining its implementation. At the same time, Enel is advocating for 
a decoupling of this legislative process from the market design proposal, so that due time is available for 
thoroughly considering amendments needed. This proposal is due to several reasons: (1) this review proposal 
doesn’t have any element of urgency at this stage; (2) the review proposed by the commission is very extended 
and (3) some important points/needs raised by market participants in the first consultation were not considered 
at all in the original proposal. 
When it comes to prov isions for the prevention and prohibition of market abuse, i.e. the essential scope of 
REMIT, a considerable attention should be paid at avoiding a blind copy -paste from the equivalent financial 
regime (i.e., from MAR). Quite the opposite, a tailor -made ap proach should be adopted whenever this is 
necessary to take due account of the specificities of energy markets and their participants. For instance, as 
mentioned below, thresholds for identification of inside information are vital for energy market partici pants, 
even though such thresholds make no sense in the case of issuers, which are the core of MAR scope on inside 
information (and this is due to the fundamental difference in underlying markets dynamics and in the 
functioning of energy physical markets). 
With a view to ensuring the above, Enel Group would like to raise the following observations and key points. 
▪ We welcome the introduction of an obligation for market participants to publish their inside information 
on “Inside Information Platforms” (IIP). Against this background, we propose some amendments in order 
to prevent a critical situation in which a market participant would find itself if the relevant IIP is 
temporarily unable to ensure timely publication; namely:  
o clarifying that MPs are explicitly discharged from any liabilities when information has been 
correctly and timely submitted to IIP for publication, and may hence straightforwardly use the 
information in such circumstances, and/or  
o allowing additional publication on corporate websites as a backup solution. 
▪ ACER or the EU Commission, or NRAs should be able to set thresholds for the identification of inside 
information at either EU or national level. Energy market participants daily manage large amount of 
information, and level one legislation should recognize that in this context thresholds are the only, reliable, 
way to quickly identify inside information, and so timely and properly treat and publish it.  
▪ With reference to the REMIT scope, some proposals are put forward in order to reduce elements of legal 
uncertainty. Namely: avoiding any reference to “potential delivery in EU” and definitely excluding large 
retail customers from the definition of “market participants” and, hence, from the scope of REMIT 
provisions. 
▪ Finally, Enel is suggesting that the EC proposal of extending surveillance obligations to market 
participants is left aside and not endorsed in the final text. As a matter of fact, this obligation, if somehow 
fitting with financial markets, is excessively hard and expensive to implement for physical and spot energy 
markets (for a matter of number and type of market participants, and for the relevant incremental costs 
associated with this extended scope).

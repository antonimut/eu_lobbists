ACCIONA’s response to the European Commission´s proposal for Electricity 
Market Design reform 
The Electricity Market Design reform must contribute to put in place a future-proof regulatory 
framework that fosters investments in renewable energy, as well as other technologies, needed to 
reach carbon-neutrality. ACCIONA welcomes that the European Commission has opted for an 
evolution of the power market legislation instead of fundamental changes because market volatility 
(which can be hedged) is preferable to regulatory volatility (which cannot be hedged) and 
uncoordinated national market interventions. 
ACCIONA appreciates the opportunity to submit feedback on the European Commission´s proposal 
for a revised EU electricity market design. We agree with the thrust of the proposal, and welcome 
that its core elements are maintained in the EP rapporteur’s draft report and the documents 
circulated by Swedish Presidency after the Energy Working parties, with one significant exception, 
“Cap on market revenues” (see below). 
In general, measures adopted by Member States to address price spikes should be limited to extreme 
situations and primarily focused on consumer support, such as direct transfers, tax 
reduction/exemption, and similar measures, rather than intervening in market prices, such as 
implementing revenue caps. If market price interventions are considered for this purpose, they 
should be temporary, uniform across EU countries and designed efficiently to avoid some negative 
effects of their application without high power prices.  
Therefore, we consider that caps on market revenues must be considered temporary measures 
and limited to crisis scenarios defined in the proposal . Additionally, we consider that other 
instruments, such as the gas Market Correction Mechanism (MCM), provide better protection for 
citizens and the economy against excessively high gas prices. 
Embedding the revenue cap as a permanent feature of the market design would also entail the risk 
of harming Power Purchase Agreements (hereafter PPAs) -that have been identified as a key 
component to ensure price stability for the consumers-, and undermining investment attractiveness 
of the technologies affected by creating an additional layer of regulatory uncertainty. As envisaged 
in the Commission proposal , revenue caps will only be effective if there is a uniform 
implementation across Member States . However, the European Parliament rapporteur’s draft 
report would allow Member States to maintain or introduce measures that further reduce market 
revenues for operators.  This is completely counterproductive : it makes little sense to give 
operators full certainty at EU level of what will happen when there is a “crisis”, and then give national 
governments leeway to apply their own measures, resulting in complete ultimate uncertainty. This 
clearly undermines the integrity of the market and gives negative signals for new investments.

Furthermore, it renders fixed prices for corporate customers through PPAs impossible, as the impact 
of any such measures would usually be passed on (in part or in full) to the end customer. 
Market revenues should, caeteris paribus, be exactly the same regardless of where the operator is 
located in Europe. Any revenue reductions (at time of liquidation) or clawbacks (applied at end of 
month or week) should be in response to emergency situations, limited in time and scope, and 
European in nature (as proposed by the Commission). We should be very conscious of the complete 
impossibility of foreseeing or hedging the risk of such potentially open-ended national measures 
being applied. 
In sum, the European market for electricity needs to remain ‘European’; we must learn from the 
distortions ensuing from measures adopted at the national level in the wake of the Ukrainian crisis. 
At the time these measures were applied, their unintended consequences could be understood 
given that we found ourselves in uncharted territory and needed to act fast; it would be harder to 
excuse if we repeat the same mistake with the benefit of hindsight. 
In addition, revenues obtained from the sale of electricity in the balancing energy market must 
always be excluded if a revenue cap is adopted, because flexibility must be encouraged as much as 
possible. It makes no sense that the decision depends on Member States (as the available draft 
report of the European Parliament states), when betting on cross-border balancing services. 
Otherwise, building on our response on the initial proposal from the European Commission, we 
consider that the following key aspects should be retained after the legislative development: 
 Renewable generators must keep freedom of action in the market, deciding the alternative 
they prefer (full access to merchant prices, entering into PPA/CfD, hedging in forward 
markets) to manage in a voluntary and efficient way market and regulatory risks.  
 The market reform must not have retroactive effects on contracts  in force or impact 
existing projects in such a way that could endanger their profitability. The co-legislators must 
ensure the grandfathering of support to existing projects as mandated by the EU Renewable 
Energy legislation. Member States may also establish voluntary two-way contracts for 
differences for existing renewable power-generating facilities. 
 Additional measures promoting PPAs are needed. To further encourage the growth of the 
market for PPAs, Member States should promote instruments to reduce financial risks 
associated to off-taker payment default. Furthermore, energy project developers 
participating in a public tender should be allowed to sale a share of the generation through 
a PPA.

 CfD schemes should be designed in a form which minimizes market distortion , while 
leaving proper price signals for the development of demand response, storage and certain 
types of relevant consumers, such as renewable hydrogen plants. The two-way contracts for 
differences should take into account in their design locational criteria to ensure that new 
investments in generation take place in optimal locations that do not create or worsen 
congestion in the grid. 
 Facilitate and incentivize low carbon flexibility and storage . The proposal opens the 
possibility for Member States to design/redesign capacity mechanisms or even introduce 
new support schemes in order to promote low-carbon flexibility and it should be a priority 
now, given the challenges associated with growing solar generation. Higher rates of solar 
penetration results in more frequently renewable curtailments during the daytime and then 
conversely increasing the need for flexibility as the sun sets. This phenomenon is projected 
to become a real problem and price arbitrage on an energy-only market as well as ancillary 
services revenues will not be sufficient to attract the investment needed in storage or 
demand response solutions. 
 Conditions for declaring a regional or Union-wide electricity price crisis : simultaneous 
compliance with the three conditions included in the proposal seems to be a balanced and 
sensible indicator of electricity price crisis, although it requires further clarification on when 
each condition is considered to be met. Any relaxation of the conditions must be avoided, 
otherwise there is a danger of intervening in the market too frequently.

 
 
The main misconceptions of the regional virtual trading hub proposal 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
On 14 March 2023 the European Commission made a proposal to reform the EU’s electricity market design. 
As European Energy Exchange (EEX), global leader in operating forward markets for electricity, we support 
the policy objective to have well-functioning forward markets. The recent energy crisis once again showed 
the importance of protecting oneself against price fluctuations and forward markets being instrumental in 
enabling companies to do so . However, we are concerned that some of the proposed measures are 
counterproductive to this policy objective. Particularly, establishing regional virtual trading hubs combined 
with zone -to-hub transmission rights  would damage the EU’s energy markets  and is built upon wrong 
assumptions. Therefore, in this paper, we outline the six main misconceptions of this measure. 
1. Regional virtual trading hubs do not require regulatory intervention. The development of hedging 
products or forward contracts  is a competitive business. Market operators compete to offer products 
that allow companies to better hedge their supply or demand. This competitive environment results in 
innovative solutions. If desired by the market, these may well be regional virtual trading hubs. However, 
the responses to ACER’s public consultation on forward markets last year revealed that there is very 
little interest in a generalised roll-out of regional virtual trading hubs. 
2. For companies across bidding zones to agree on a forward contract there is no need to secure 
physical transmission capacity. Companies use forward markets to manage a financial risk i.e. the 
risk that future spot prices will move in an adverse direction. The contracts are typically financially 
settled. To mitigate the risk that the spot prices of the respective bidding zones diverge, companies 
could buy long term transmission rights from TSOs . They are then paid  the price spread between the 
two respective bidding zones, by the TSO. However, there are more efficient hedging tools offered by 
the market.  
3. There are sufficient hedging possibilities for cross -border hedging in the Core region . The 
German forward market is the most important trading hub in Europe, effectively pooling liquidity from 
neighbouring bidding zones  up to and  even including Nordic countries. Spread products allow 
companies to mitigate the remaining price risk that German spot prices do not perfectly match the spot 
prices of the bidding zone they are active in . Such spread products have grown organically by the 
market and have seen a constant growth in volumes  because of the efficiency this trading method 
brings. They do not only exist for the German trading hub, but also for other hubs such as Hungary and 
France. 
4. The Nordic model does not serve as a reference model for the rest of Europe. The price correlation 
between the bidding zone prices and the Nordic system area price has significantly deteriorated . This 
makes the Nordic system area price less useful as a proxy hedge  for Nordic market participants and 
has resulted in continuously decreasing liquidity in the forward markets.  
5. The introduction of a regional virtual trading hub does not facilitate bidding zone 
reconfigurations. The vulnerability to a possible reconfiguration is not removed, but only shifted. A 
split between bidding zones within a hub  will change the weightings of the individual bidding zones 
within the hub index calculation , bringing very similar repercussions on the forward market, as when 
there would not be a hub. The same problem occurs every time a bidding zone is added or removed 
from the hub.

 6. While in spot markets economic efficiencies can be gained by coupling markets, this would 
make no sense in forward markets. Optimising production and consumption across bidding zones 
by optimising grid capacity only makes sense in a timeframe close to the physical delivery of electricity 
when forecasts can be made more accurately. Allocating cross-border capacity sooner than necessary 
only leads to difficulties in forecasting transmission capacity, and subsequent financial risk for TSOs, 
the costs of which will be borne by the end consumer. 
In sum, whilst in theory virtual hubs may be an interesting tool to combine several markets into a larger and 
more liquid market, such a decision should not be imposed upon the market. Doing so could be particularly 
damaging if well-functioning forward hedging possibilities are already present. It would fragment rather than 
increase liquidity, making these markets less efficient in their risk managing purposes . We therefore 
propose to remove the virtual hubs from the electricity market design proposal and rather focus on removing 
barriers to forward market liquidity, such as non -market-based support schemes and unstable regulatory 
frameworks. 
 
On 14 March 2023, the European Commission proposed to establish regional virtual trading hubs  as part 
of its revision of the European electricity marke t design . In this memo, E EX outlines the main 
misconceptions of the proposal based on decades of experience in developing forward markets for 
electricity. 
First, what has been proposed? 
The proposal foresees creating regional reference prices via a hub and obliging transmission system 
operators (TSOs) to issue long term transmission rights (LTTRs) between the hub and the bidding zones it 
is comprised of. 
Practically, ENTSO-E would be mandated to come up with a proposal to e stablish regional virtual trading 
hubs for the forward market s. ENTSO-E is tasked to 1) define their geographical scope  (including the 
bidding zones constituting these hubs) ; 2) define  the calculation methodology for the reference prices of 
the hubs; and 3) define the financial long-term transmission rights from bidding zones to the virtual hubs  
(zone-to-hub LTTRs) to be issued by the single allocation platform, the Joint Allocation Office (JAO).  
Second, what is the rationale behind this? 
According to the recitals of the legislative proposal, the objective is to improve the functioning of the 
electricity forward market s. The underlying assumption is that  there is insufficient liquidity  particularly in 
forward markets based on smaller bidding zones . This would prevent market participants from efficiently 
hedging their price exposure. 
What are the most important misconceptions behind the proposal? 
• MISCONCEPTION #1: Regional virtual trading hubs require regulatory intervention.  
First, if there is a demand  from energy companies  for new hedging products, market operators have a 
natural interest in reacting to it. The development of and building liquidity in such products is a competitive 
business, regulated under EU financial services legislation . Private companies, such as market operators 
and price reporting agencies, compete to offer indices and hedging products that meet energy companies’ 
demand to hedge price risk. This competitive environment results in innovative solutions . If desired by the 
 
market, these may well be regional virtual trading hubs,  as demonstrated recently in Italy . The responses 
to ACER’s public consultation on forward markets last year  revealed that there is very little interest in  a 
generalised roll-out of regional virtual trading hub combined with zone-to-hub LTTRs.  
• MISCONCEPTION #2: For companies across bidding zones to agree on a forward contract 
they need to secure physical transmission capacity. 
The proposal assumes physical transmission capacity must be available in order for two parties across 
regions to agree on a forward contract. This assumption misjudges the very purpose of forward markets. 
Market participants such as energy companies use contracts on the forward markets to manage a financial 
risk, i.e. the risk that future spot prices will move in an adverse direction . These contracts are typically 
financially settled where the participants either pay or receive the difference between the previously agreed 
forward price and the day-ahead spot price. Managing price risk in this way is indispensable for contracting 
conventional power production and is being increasingly used by renewables developers to make their 
projects bankable.  
The fact that it is financial risk market participants are hedging , also means that to  agree on a forward 
contract, market participants do not have to be part of the same bidding zone. If the spot price of the bidding 
zone of one participant closely correlates with the price of the more liquid bidding zone of a counterparty, 
they can enter a forward contract of which the underlying is the spot price of the more liquid bidding zone. 
This practice of proxy-hedging is particularly useful when one bidding zone does not have as many active 
participants as a neighbouring bidding zone. Because from a market participant’s perspective there is a 
trade-off between liquidity, i.e. the ability to find a counterparty, and optimal price correlation.  The latter 
counterparty will have a perfect hedge  because it has entered a contract in its own zone. However,  the 
former counterparty may have a remaining risk: a potential gap between the spot price in its bidding zone 
and the spot price of the bidding zone it is 'proxy-hedged’ in. Several options exist to close out this potential 
remaining risk.  
One option is to buy a zone-to-zone LTTR from JAO. As these contracts are typically options, it means that 
the TSO will pay the holder of a LTTR the day-ahead price spread between  the two respective bidding 
zones if that spread is positive. The TSOs only issue LTTRs up to the extent there is transmission capacity 
available for trading and hence  – assuming LTTR pay-outs never amount to more than the congestion 
income earned – they can pay the holder with the congestion income it earns on the respective transmission 
lines. 
These LTTRs are generally financial contracts, which makes sense as the market participants face a 
financial risk. The physical transmission capacity is made available in the spot market as part of the well -
developed market coupling in Europe – without market participants being actively involved. Issuing LTTRs 
does not increase available physical capacity  but is only a way of offering financial risk management to 
market participants. 
Another more efficient option for market participants to manage their remaining financial risk  are market-
based solutions such as the E PADs offered by Nasdaq in the Nordics and spread futures offered by EEX 
in the Core region as well as other regions. Both provide for the exact same financial hedge as they equally 
pay out the difference between the respective bidding zone spot prices. Howeve r, contrary to the LTTRs, 
they have the benefit that they  are available on a continuous basis, are available man y years ahead and 
are not limited to the amount of available transmission capacity.  Please find an overview of its benefits

• MISCONCEPTION #3: There are not sufficient hedging possibilities for cross-border 
hedging in the Core region 
As indicated above, in the Core region a model with more benefits than a regional virtual trading hub already 
exists. Grown organically, German power is now considered as the most important trading hub in Europe, 
effectively pooling liquidity from neighbouring bidding zones , even including Nordic countries . While 
German producers and consumers benefit from a direct hedge  as they buy German power forward 
contracts to hedge the risk of German power spot prices, for a 
Czech power producer for example , German power futures 
function as a proxy hedge.  If Czech power spot prices and 
German power spot prices are well correlated , this proxy 
hedge works well.  In this case t he power produce r would 
simply accept the small remaining  risk. However, if prices 
show a certain degree of divergence, the same producer may 
wish to hedge the remaining price risk with a locational spread 
future. In this example, the risk that German spot prices 
diverge from Czech spot prices could be mitigated by buying 
a German-Czech spread future.  
Besides Germany, the forward markets in Hungary, Spain and 
France also act as trading hubs for their surrounding bidding 
zones. Spread futures exist for all these hubs, with at least 20 
being actively traded . Since their launch in 2014, there has 
been a constant growth in volumes.  
Finally, EEX’ spread futures have one important benefit compared to EPADs and LTTRs. As EEX’ spread 
futures are not an outright future contract, but a combination of two “normal” bidding zone futures delivering 
the price difference between the two target bidding zones, they increase the volumes in the standard future 
contracts in the respective bidding zones.  This means that they do not only allow for a perfect hedge, but 
they also ensure that liquidity in less liquid bidding zones increases.  

 
If in the Core region, a regional virtual trading hu b combined with zone-to-hub LTTRs were introduced by 
regulatory intervention, it would compete and significantly interfere with the existing hedging tools  as 
described above, at the cost of a well-functioning and organically grown hub that already meets the policy 
objectives of the proposal.  
• MISCONCEPTION #4: Nordic system price serves as a reference model for the rest of Europe 
The Nordic hub model may have been successful in providing more liquidity than single zone futures may 
have done, however, the Nordic market model should not be used as a reference model for the rest of 
Europe.1 Since Sweden has been split into smaller bidding zones, the price correlation between the bidding 
zone prices and the Nordic system area price has significantly deteriorated. This has made the Nordic 
system area price less useful as a proxy hedge, resulting in continuously decreasing liquidity in the forward 
market. At the same time , downsizing the respective bidding zones has not generated the expected 
investments into the congested areas , as those are typically depending on competition which in turn 
requires larger bidding zones. The problem has become so large that many Nordic market participants have 
started trading German power instead of the Nordic system price area , as the benefit of more  liquidity 
outweighs possible  spot price divergence. Notwithstanding, at EEX, we are exploring the possibility to 
introduce new contracts in the Nordics that better meet the hedging needs of these market participants. 
• MISCONCEPTION #5: The introduction of a regional virtual trading hub facilitates bidding 
zone reconfigurations. 
In its policy paper on forward markets , ACER states that the liquidity of regional hubs would not be 
endangered in case of any changes of bidding zone configuration. 2 
First, this statement only makes sense in the case of bidding zone reconfigurations with in one respective 
hub. However, even then the vulnerability to a possible reconfiguration is not removed, but only shifted. 
Should there for example be a split between bidding zones within a hub, it will change the weightings of the 
individual bidding zones within the hub index calculation . This would bring very similar  repercussions on 
the forward market, as the underlying of the futures cannot be changed  that easily. The same problem 
occurs every time a bidding zone is added to or removed from the hub. 
Another aspect which is not sufficiently considered is the effect of bidding zone reconfigurations on LTTRs, 
EPADs and spread futures. These products depend on the stability of the underlying reference prices, i.e. 
the price of the hub/liquid bidding zone and the price of the target bidding zone. Thus, even if a bidding 
zone configuration were to cause less price insecurity within hub futures than bidding zone futures for the 
first reference price – it causes problems if the bidding zone of the second reference price was affected. 
This problem was not as pressing until now as LTTRs are not yet tradable with sufficient lead time. However, 
as soon as LTTRs become tradable three or more years ahead of delivery, they will face this problem, as 
EPADs and spreads do already today.

• MISCONCEPTION #6: Extending the timeframe in which LTTRs are issued will help 
European market integration. 
While the current Forward Capacity Allocation Network Code requires TS Os to issue their transmission 
capacity up to one year ahead, the current proposal extends it to at least three years in advance. The idea 
seems to be based on the underlying misconception that European markets can be physically integrated, 
across all timeframes. However, as explained before, forward markets are fundamentally different from spot 
markets. Whereas the former is used to hedge market participants against a financial risk, the latter are 
used for short -term optimization of production and consumption . Optimisation of production and 
consumption needs across bidding zones and hence optimisation of grid capacity only makes sense closer 
to delivery when forecasts can be made more accurately . Allocating cross -border capacity sooner than 
necessary only leads to difficulties in forecasting transmission capacity, and subsequent financial risk for 
TSOs the costs  of which  will be borne by the  end consumer.  This means that while in spot markets 
economic efficiencies can be gained by coupling markets, this would make no sense in forward markets.  
CONCLUSION 
While virtual hubs may in theory be an interesting instrument to combine several markets into a larger, 
more liquid market, such a decision should n ot be imposed on the market. As evidenced in Italy, private 
entities operating in competitive environments will come forward with innovative solutions when there is 
such demand from the market. Regulation should not force market operators to develop hedging products 
for which there is no demand. It could be particularly damaging if well-functioning hedging possibilities are 
already present in the market as is the case in the Core region, where German power futures combined 
with spread futures are becoming increasingly popular . The potentially forc ed establishment of regional 
virtual trading hubs combined with zone-to-hub LTTRs will compete and significantly interfere with present 
solutions, fragmenting rather than improving liquidity. It should be recalled that only 12% of the respondents 
to the ACER public consultation last year voiced support for the zone-to-hub idea.  
Finally, the idea of TSOs providing cross-border hedging products should only be a fallback option. LTTRs 
issued by TSOs/JAO are fundamentally limited by physical capacities and the expected congestion income 
of TSOs. However, to be properly hedged , there is no need to ensure transmission capacity and market 
operators have produced various cross -border forward hedging solutions, independent of transmission 
capacity. In contrast, transmission capacity is crucial for physical market integration, and TSOs and NEMOs 
have a record of accomplishment of successful cooperation in organising spot market coupling. Allocating 
cross-border capacity sooner than necessary (e.g., tree or more years in advance) only leads to difficulties 
in forecasting transmission capacity, and subsequent financial risk for TSOs to be borne by end consumers.  
In conclusion, this regulatory intervention  would work contrary to its own objective. It risks fragmenting 
forward market liquidity and will incur higher costs for end consumers. We therefore urge to remove the 
proposal and focus on removing barriers to forward market liquidity, such as non -market-based support 
schemes and unstable regulatory frameworks.

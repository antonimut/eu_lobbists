   
 
 
  
 
Feedback on the Commission’s Proposal on the Right to Repair  
         Date: May 24th 2023 
 
Refurbed, the fastest-growing marketplace for refurbished products in the DACH region and leading 
marketplace in Ireland, Sweden and Denmark, welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the 
long-awaited Right to Repair proposal. We aim to make the refurbed idea known throughout Europe 
and contribute to a more sustainable world. We, therefore, appreciate the EU's efforts to promote a 
circular economy and fully support the introduction of legislation on a right to repair. 
As a leading provider of refurbished electronics in Europe, we have seen first-hand the benefits of 
repairing and extending the lifespan of electronics. We believe that a true right to repair will not only 
reduce electronic waste and save emissions but also create substantial economic opportunities. 
The current linear model of production, consumption and waste processing is not sustainable - many 
of the resources we depend on are finite. Their exploitation is pushing us to our planet’s boundaries 
and destroying our livelihoods. To counteract this, a transition to a circular economy is necessary. The 
right to repair is an essential milestone for this transition. Repairing and reusing appliances should not 
only be an option but become the new norm in Europe. 
 
In our view, European legislation should include a universal consumer right to repair regardless of 
warranty limits, whereby every consumer should have the choice to seek repair of any of their devices 
by the professional of their choice, at a fair and affordable price. The current proposal is not ambitious 
enough in this regard. 
 
This is why we would like to provide the following feedback on the proposed legislation: 
1. Design for repairability: In order to establish a right to repair that is implemented in 
practice and has a true impact, devices need to be designed and manufactured with repair in 
mind. The right to repair must therefore be understood as complementing legislation on 
ecodesign. While we acknowledge the progress on the Ecodesign Regulation, we are still a 
long way from where we need to be. Designs have become more intricate and complex, and 
manufacturers are making it more difficult for independent service providers to operate. 
As long as manufacturers can replace a damaged product within the legal warranty period if 
this is cheaper than repairing it, they will not adapt the design of their products. As a result, 
for most products, replacement will always be cheaper than repair, and the most common, and

environmentally damaging, option during warranty. If repair is not possible, replacement 
should be done with a refurbished product wherever possible. 
2. Ban practices that hinder repair: Any contractual, hardware or software technique 
preventing or limiting independent repair outside of the manufacturer or distributor’s certified 
networks (such as part pairing through serialisation) should be prohibited. 
The obligation to repair should also apply indistinctly to all products (new or second-hand) 
regardless of where they have been bought or previously repaired. It is unfortunate to see an 
increase in cases where OEMs refuse to repair their own devices solely because they have 
been refurbished or repaired by an independent service provider, outside of their control. 
3. Access to spare parts and repair information: Manufacturers should not be allowed to 
restrict repair to their own network or spare parts, as this would give them a quasi-monopoly 
on repair and discriminate against independent repairers. The law should ensure that repair 
can be done using original, compatible or reused/second-hand parts, which comply with EU 
standards, safety and environmental regulations. Manufacturers should also make available 
necessary repair information to independent repairers at an affordable price. This will increase 
competition and choice in the repair market. 
4. Fair and proportionate pricing: Spare parts and repair information should be available at a 
fair and reasonable price. Manufacturers should not use pricing as a barrier to repair and 
should not artificially inflate prices to discourage repair. The provision should refer to a 
“reasonable and proportionate price” for repair outside of warranty limits. 
5. Obligation to repair: We welcome the obligation to repair for producers as a powerful tool 
to force producers into offering repair solutions, granting access to spare parts for independent 
repairers and designing repairable products. That said, we recommend mentioning that 
refurbishers are excluded from Article 5.3 when they place products on the market. Currently, 
when a refurbisher imports second-hand products to refurbish them in the EU, they are 
considered a producer according to this draft. However, they cannot offer access to spare 
parts as they are not the initial manufacturer and simply do not have this access themselves. 
 
In conclusion, we welcome the Commission’s proposal to introduce a European right to repair. We are 
convinced that the right to repair is essential to promote a circular economy and reduce electronic 
waste by giving consumers more sustainable consumption choices - by choosing repair over 
replacement. While we support the proposed legislation, we see the need for improvement in certain  
   
 
 
 
key areas as listed above. It cannot be stressed enough that a transition to a more circular economy 
cannot solely rely on a consumer's right to repair - to make the “right to repair” a reality, devices need 
to be repairable in the first place. 
 
We thank the Commission for the opportunity to provide our feedback on this proposal and for taking 
our concerns and comments into consideration. Refurbed stands ready to engage further on this very 
important topic.

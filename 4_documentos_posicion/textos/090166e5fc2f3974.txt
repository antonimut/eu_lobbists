 
Lenovo’s view on the European Commission Proposal for a Directive on Common Rules Promoting the 
Repair of Goods 
Lenovo is a leading global technology company that designs and manufactures innovative personal 
computers, mobile devices, and data center solutions. As a company deeply committed to sustainability, 
Lenovo is fully supportive of the objectives and goals of the proposal for a Directive on common rules 
promoting the repair of goods.  
Several aspects of the proposal will support the transition to a truly circular economy. The introduction 
of new consumer rights supporting easy and effective repairs will help foster environmentally 
responsible practices while benefiting consumers. Lenovo already offers a comprehensive range of 
customer support services, including repair, replacement parts and detailed repair guides for many of its 
products. This approach facilitates a convenient repair experience for both consumers and repair 
professionals, contributing to the overall ease of maintaining and extending the lifespan of Lenovo 
devices, beyond the warranty period of products. We also welcome the European Commission’s 
intention to develop a voluntary European quality standard for repair services. The standard would 
enable EU consumers to easily identify and engage with repairers who commit to a higher quality. In this 
regard, we encourage the European Commission to set a clear timeframe for the development of this 
standard, with reliability as the primary consideration. 
However, we also recognize that the European Commission's proposal might give rise to unforeseen 
repercussions for both consumers and companies like Lenovo, including heightened financial burdens 
and new overlapping or contradictory requirements. It is essential to evaluate the potential trade-offs 
and examine the long-term implications of the proposal to ensure that the overall objective of 
promoting sustainability and consumer benefits is not compromised by the increased costs and 
compliance hurdles encountered by businesses.  
To develop standards that are beneficial, achievable, and effective, we suggest considering the following 
recommendations: 
1. Exclude Internet of Things (IoT) and business-to-business (B2B) products from the scope of the 
proposal. 
2. Include refurbishment options to prevent overproduction and promote increased re-use.  
3. Clarify that manufacturers and sellers can comply with information requirements by making 
compulsory information available online. 
 
Exclude IoT and B2B products from scope 
We commend the European Commission's recognition that policies extending product life beyond the 
legal guarantee period may not be universally beneficial to all products. We appreciate the 
Commission's targeted approach confined to specific categories of repairable products, including 
electronic displays, servers, mobile devices, and tablets. 
However, we also note that the Commission's proposal includes the possibility to add new reparable 
product categories (goods) in response to legislative developments. We draw attention to the definition

of 'goods' in the proposal1, which also encompasses any tangible movable items, integrated with, or 
linked to digital content or a digital service. This indicates that the European Commission could 
potentially introduce reparability requirements for any 'tangible' goods containing digital elements, 
including consumer Internet of Things (IoT) devices, which are indeed classified as tangible goods.  
However, although IoT devices are physical objects equipped with sensors, software, and connectivity 
features, their value proposition extends beyond their hardware. The services, data analysis, and 
insights that IoT devices provide, enabled by their connectivity and software components, can be 
considered intangible goods. Consequently, IoT devices often consist of intricate hardware, software, 
and connectivity components that are tightly integrated, and which might not be preserved or restored 
through repair alone. They can contain integrated proprietary components, firmware, or software, the 
repair of which might not be possible without impacting other interconnected components, 
compromising system performance, or inadvertently creating new vulnerabilities that may affect the 
device's security and reliability. This means that if one part fails, the whole unit may need to be 
replaced. Instead, a more targeted approach that addresses the unique challenges associated with IoT 
devices and promotes sustainability, security, and product longevity may be more suitable. We suggest 
including a more nuanced classification of 'goods' to avoid inadvertently including IoT devices within the 
proposal scope. 
Furthermore, we believe this proposal may not be easily applicable or relevant to highly customized B2B 
products, such as data storage products and electronic displays utilized in the context of business 
operations, which fall under the proposal as per points 5 and 8 of ANNEX II. B2B transactions typically 
involve tailored solutions and services that cater to the specific requirements of the purchasing 
organization. 
These transactions often necessitate customized repair arrangements that are crucial for maintaining 
seamless operations. Such arrangements may encompass the timely provision of new or refurbished 
products or components and the subsequent return of defective items for repair. This practice is 
essential for mitigating operational disruptions and promoting the efficient functioning of businesses. 
Considering these elements, we suggest adding language, possibly within the recitals, to clarify that the 
small data storage products and electronic displays sold outside of B2B transactions are excluded from 
the scope of this proposal. 
 
Include refurbishment options to prevent overproduction of Spare Parts 
We appreciate the European Commission's decision to uphold the current legal warranty period, and the 
position on mandating repair as the primary remedy within the legal guarantee remains valid, though 
we believe a more flexible approach could be beneficial. Mandating repairing for extended period would 
potentially result in excess purchase of spare parts, by way of ‘last time buy’, when a product model

In this situation, promoting repair as the default option, especially in the case of smaller consumer 
appliances, may not always be economically feasible or particularly beneficial to the environment.  
From an environmental standpoint, mandating long-term spare part production could inadvertently 
exacerbate waste generation rather than mitigate it. The maintenance of spare part inventories can lead 
to eventual surpluses and subsequent disposal, particularly when consumers choose to purchase newer, 
technologically more advanced, or aesthetically appealing devices rather than repairing older ones. In 
essence, this outcome contradicts the primary objective of the Commission’s proposal.  
We can observe this phenomenon in the smartphone market. For instance, a smartphone that's five 
years old often lacks the specifications required for customers to fully leverage today's cutting-edge 
technological solutions in areas like imaging, gaming, social media, security, 5G networking, and Wi-Fi. 
Most consumers find themselves in a position where they need to upgrade their devices, not because 
their current device is irreparable, but because the specs of a five-year-old smartphone are becoming 
obsolete.  
Mandating the availability of components for extended periods may also lead to a shift in the production 
paradigm, encompassing aspects ranging from semiconductor chips to other vital components. Such 
requirements could inadvertently hinder technological progress and result in stagnating or significantly 
decelerating innovation.  
As a viable remedy, we recommend considering refurbishment as an alternative to repair within or 
outside the legal warranty period. This could be at the discretion of the sellers or manufacturers. The 
refurbishment process typically involves updating or modernizing the equipment, which can result in 
enhanced performance and functionality and extend its usable life without the need to manufacture an 
entirely new device. Refurbishing existing equipment is also generally more cost-effective than 
purchasing brand-new equipment, particularly for high-cost technology items.  
Essentially, including the option to offer refurbished products would mitigate overproduction and lessen 
the environmental impact tied to the upkeep of large spare part inventories. It also promotes 
sustainable practices and achieves an optimal balance that promotes repairability without inhibiting the 
progression of technology. 
 
Clarify that manufacturers and sellers can comply with information requirements by making 
compulsory information available online.  
We suggest a clarification specifying that manufacturers and sellers can comply with information 
requirements by making compulsory information available online, as far as it is readily available for 
consumers at the point of purchase during offline shopping. This can be achieved, for instance, by 
incorporating a QR code on the product packaging or providing a link to the EU's Digital Product 
Passport, where consumers can access the necessary information using their smartphone.  
 
Online information disclosure offers a more cost-effective and environmentally sustainable approach, 
reducing expenses for manufacturers and waste associated with printing and distributing paper 
 
documents. It also allows for more efficient updating and revision of product information, ensuring 
consumers have access to the most accurate and up-to-date details without the need for reprinting and 
redistributing physical materials. In summary, by promoting online information disclosure, the Directive 
would foster a culture of environmental awareness, encouraging organizations and individuals to adopt 
sustainable practices, while aligning with the overarching objective of the Commission's proposal.

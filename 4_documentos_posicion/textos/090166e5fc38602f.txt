European Commission Proposed Critical Raw Materials Act 
Nickel Institute Position 
 
Introduction 
The Nickel Institute 1 welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for a Critical Raw Materials 
Act, published on 16 th March 2023. Nickel producers are at the beginning and at the end of 
important value chains for the EU´s future . Nickel plays an important role in the manufacturing 
processes of many value chains which are essential in decarbonisation technologies, such as  low 
carbon power generation, sustainable mobility and green hydrogen production as shown in various 
studies 2,3. They also play an essential role in defence applications and the digital transition. Our 
member companies supply raw materials to those value chains and recycle products containing 
nickel once they reach the end of life.  
 
About 69% of global nickel production is used to manufacture stainless steel. Another 12% is used 
in the production of o ther alloys, often employed in highly specialised and demanding  industrial 
applications. Other uses of nickel, in the form of nickel compounds, include the production of 
battery technologies (11%), plating (7%) and other applications including catalysts. 
 
Overall, we support the aim of the initiative and we welcome many provisions of the EC proposal. 
However, we consider there are some aspects that could be improved, to ensure that the 
objectives of the Critical Raw Materials Act are achieved. Our comments cover the following issues: 
 
- Need for more coherence in the EU regulatory framework 
- Interconnectivity of metals production and recycling 
- Benchmark for strategic raw materials 
- “Nickel - battery grade” as strategic raw material 
- “Nickel - battery grade” as critical raw material 
- Permitting 
- Sustainability aspects 
- Strategic partnerships 
- Critical Raw Materials Board. 
 
General remarks 
 
Need for more coherence in the EU regulatory framework 
The Commission proposal touches upon several important issues and proposes various measures 
to boost domestic production, to foster recycling and to improve access to raw materials from 
third countries – which we support. However, an important aspect that is not addressed in the 
Commission proposal is the need for a coherent EU regulatory framework for both metal producers

Acknowledging the interconnectivity of metals production and recycling  
The Commission proposal focuses its measures on critical and strategic  raw materials , 
acknowledging current and future economic im portance and supply risks associated with those 
raw materials . While we acknowledge the approach taken  in the Commission proposal , we 
consider that it does not fully take into account the interconnectivity of the nonferrous metals 
industry, both in primary production as well as on the recycling side . Various metallic and non -
metallic by-products occur from the extraction or from the recycling of the primary metal. Some 
of these by-products may be critical and / or strategic, while others are not. Limiting the actions to 
the critical and strategic raw materials only does not acknowledge these links in metals production 
and recycling.  
 
Recommendation: 
We suggest that the actions in the Commission proposal be applicable to the production of all 
metals and raw materials in general, and not be limited to critical and strategic raw materials only. 
 
Specific comments 
 
Setting of benchmarks for strategic raw materials (Article 1)  
In general, we support the principle of setting non-binding benchmarks by 2030 for the extraction 
and processing of critical and strategic raw materials from domestic sources as well as a target for 
materials coming from recycling. However, we consider that, to be more effective, the Commission 
proposal would need to be more specific and set individual targets for each critical and strategic 
raw material in the scope. Moreover, it should be clarified if the targets are bas ed on volume or 
other parameters. 
 
There are significant differences for the critical and strategic raw materials in the scope when 
looking at domestic mine production, processing and refining as well as recycling. For some metals 
such as e.g., nickel or copper these targets might not be very ambitious whereas for others it will 
be challenging to achieve such targets by 2030. On the other hand, applying the 10% mining, 40% 
processing and 15% recycling targets over all critical and strategic raw materials does not lead to 
a meaningful result, given the significant variation in production tonnages. Some of the critical high 
tonnage materials could already exceed the targets for domestic mine production, processing and 
refining.

The 2023 EU Criticality Assessment provides detailed information on all critical and strategic raw 
materials and forms a solid basis to identify meaningful targets for each material individually. We 
believe that the Raw Materials Board ( in cooperation with stakeholders, notably the relevant 
industry sectors) should play a role in defining meaningful and ambitious, while achievable, targets 
for each critical and strategic raw material. 
 
Recommendation: 
Article 1: We suggest defining mining, processing and recycling targets for each critical and 
strategic raw material individually , to be set  by the Raw Materials Board in dialogue with the 
affected industry sector. 
 
“Nickel – battery grade” as strategic raw material (Article 3, Annex I) 
The Commission proposal identifies “Nickel - battery grade” as a strategic raw material . It 
acknowledges the growing importance that nickel will play in the near future in EV batteries .  
However, in our view t he limitation to “ Nickel - battery grade” does not acknowledge the 
importance that nickel (in the form of nickel metal or nickel compounds) already plays and will play 
in the near future in the manufacturing processes of other  key technologies to achieve the green 
and digital transition  – be it as an alloying element in stainless steel used in low carbon power 
generation, in green hydrogen production or in high nickel alloys in defence applications and other 
products containing nickel in appliances relevant for the digital transition.  
 
Moreover, it has to be acknowledged that a distinction between those nickel products serving EV 
batteries and those used in other value chains is becoming increasingly challenging. Refined nickel 
metal (trade name: class 1 nickel (>99,8% nickel by weight)) is used in several value chains including 
EV batteries. Other nickel products (trade name: class 2 nickel (<99,8% nickel by weight), including 
e.g., ferronickel and nickel pig iron) were in the past used exclusively in stainless steel production. 
In the meantime,  an increasing amount of class 2 products is also converted into materials serving 
the EV batteries market. Given the dynamics in the nickel value chains as well as the relevance of 
nickel in other technologies, we believe that the strategic raw materials assessment cannot be 
limited to battery grade nickel only. 
 
In addition, the limitation to “ Nickel - battery grade” could also create uncertainty and confusion 
given that the draft CRM Regulation does not include any definition of “battery grade” and there 
are some Articles that actually refer to “nickel”  (e.g. Art. 28 on recycled content of permanent 
magnets” and not to “Nickel - battery grade”. 
 
Recommendation: 
Article 3: We suggest considering “nickel” in general as a strategic raw material, without limitations 
to “battery grade”. 
 
“Nickel - Battery grade” as critical raw material (Article 3, Annex II) 
The 2023 Criticality Assessments were published in parallel to the Commission proposal for a 
Critical Raw Materials Act . The EC assessment concludes that nickel does not qualify as a critical 
raw material. While the analysis identified nickel to be of high economic importance , the supply 
risks were considered to be low. These results are consistent with the findings of earlier criticality 
assessments conducted by the European Commission. The Commission proposal states that all

strategic raw material s are added to the list of c ritical raw materials, including “ Nickel - battery 
grade”. We do not agree with the Commission's proposal to automatically identify as critical all 
strategic raw materials listed in Annex I. 
 
We believe that the methodology to identify critical raw materials should be applied consistently 
and without exceptions . The criticality assessment should remain factual  and based on the 
methodology which was applied consistently over the past 15 years. Rather than modifying the 
methodology to identify nickel as a critical raw material, we believe that measures and actions to 
promote the production and recycling of metals should not be limited to critical raw materials only. 
 
Recommendation: 
Article 3: We suggest removing nickel (“ Nickel – battery grade”) from the list of critical raw 
materials. 
 
Permitting (Articles 8-13) 
In the case of nickel mining projects, it takes up to 15 years before the first ton of nickel is 
produced. Permitting is a major bottleneck. Considering the fast-growing demand for strategic raw 
materials such as nickel  in the coming 8 -10 years , it will be essential to ensure that the time 
between the first exploration stages and the production of the first ton of metal is significa ntly 
shortened. Reducing the time required between the application for a permit and its granting will 
be crucial. 
 
We therefore support the proposal made in the Critical Raw Materials Act for a one-stop shop, for 
clearly defined permitting deadlines, and other streamlining measures . These proposals are 
important to reduce the overall administrative burden  and long timelines , while ensuring 
environmental protection and community involvement. 
 
Recommendation: 
Articles 8-13: We support the Commission proposal with regard to permitting. 
 
Sustainability aspects (Articles 29-30) 
We welcome the Commission proposal to also include sustainability aspects in the CRM Act. Raw 
materials are often linked to environmental, social and governance (ESG) issues. These need to be 
addressed. Also, the production and recycling of raw materials have an environmental footprin t. 
Addressing these points in the Critical Raw Materials  Act will ensure that impacts on the 
environment or on communities in other regions globally are minimized, and that there is a good 
understanding of the environmental profile that raw materials have. 
 
ESG due diligence and environmental footprinting are already addressed under other EU 
legislation, program mes and initiatives , such as e.g., the EU Battery Regulation, the upcoming 
Corporate Sustainable Due Diligence Directive, the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation 
and the EU Product Environmental Footprint Programme. Aligning the requirements in the Critical 
Raw Materials Act with these above-mentioned regulations is necessary to avoid duplication of 
efforts. 
 
The metals and mining industry have worked over the past years on several ESG standards and 
responsible sourcing frameworks. The standards reflect the diversity in the metals and minerals

Strategic partnerships (Article 33) 
We appreciate the Commission proposal acknowledging the importance of strategic partnerships 
with resource rich regions outside the EU to satisfy the EU domestic raw materials demand. Raw 
materials diplomacy is the third important pillar besides improving domestic supply with primary 
raw materials and fostering recycling.  
 
It usually takes up to 15 years before new mining projects produce the first ton of nickel. Existing 
publications and studies guide us on where the future deposits within and outside the EU are found 
to satisfy the growing demand for the green and digital transition.  
 
We therefore believe that the Commission proposal should be more explicit when it comes to 
taking action on strategic partnerships. For strategic raw materials such as nickel, we know in which 
countries and regions potential deposits lie. We therefore believe that the Commission proposal 
should put forward some concrete short-term actions and request the Raw Materials Board to 
identify those regions, enabling any necessary steps to support mining projects in those areas. 
 
Critical Raw Materials Board (Article 34 and 35, Recital 55) 
The proposed Regulation foresees the establishment of a Critical Raw Materials Board and defines 
the wide range of tasks that the Board has to deal with. Currently, the Commission proposal 
provides that the Board shall consist of representatives from EU Member States and the European 
Commission.  
 
We believe that the Critical Raw Materials Board would greatly benefit from involving experts from 
other stakeholder groups, notably from the raw materials industry. The EU Raw Materials Supply 
Group, which has accompanied the work of the Commission on critical raw materials over the last 
years, showed the great added value that industry representatives with their expertise and 
knowledge of the respective critical and strategic raw materials can bring; in particular, with regard 
to discussions on e.g.  reserves and resources, mine supply and production, the production 
processes applied, sustainability and ESG issues as well as markets and end of life management.  
 
Recommendation: 
Articles 34-35: The Commission proposal should include industry as a  relevant stakeholder group 
to the Critical Raw Materials Board.

 
 
Yara position on the Commission’s “Proposal for a Regulation of the European 
Parliament and of the Council amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1009 as regards the 
digital labelling of EU fertilising products” 
 
Notice to readers (27/05/2023): This position is drafted from the perspective of a global mineral 
fertiliser manufacturer (Yara International ASA). However, we believe that much of it is also 
relevant for (big and small) economic operators in the wider EU fertilising products sector. 
 
Contact person: Dr. Koen Van Keer, Regulatory Affairs and Labelling Manager 
 
Summary: 
 
Yara welcomes the Commission’s proposal to amend Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, aiming to 
facilitate (“partial”) voluntary digital labelling of EU fertilising products. But we see scope for  
improvement, fostering a future-proof piece of legislation, supporting EU economic operators, 
label users (especially farmers), and environmental goals. We suggest, in particular, to: 
 
1) Include more Annex III mandatory label elements, ao “Instructions for intended use”. 
2) Grant economic operators flexibility in the use of novel “electronic digital solutions”.  
 
With proper digital labelling rules in place, economic operators can i) make labels available 
that are “clear, understandable, legible and intelligible” ; ii) optimize label management; iii) 
improve labelling compliance performance; iv) reduce monetary and environmental costs. 
 
Glossary of terms & abbreviations used in this document 
 
Demurrage costs: Fees charged when cargoes are left too long in port yards prior to collection. 
EOPs (Economic Operators): All actors involved in the production , distribution, and labelling 
of mineral fertilisers, along the entire supply chain (manufacturers, importers, distributors). 
ERP: Enterprise Resource Planning (tools) 
Essential information: Information deemed “essential” (by law) for display on a physical label. 
EU FPR: EU Fertilising Products Regulation (ie Regulation 2019/1009). 
EU FPR product formula designation: A specific label requirement in EU FPR Annex III that is 
actually not so clear, although illustrated in the (non-binding) “Communication of the visual 
appearance of labels”  - 2021/C119/01; section 6.1.1 ; illustrative e xample: “NPK(Ca,Mg,S) 
mineral fertilizer with micro-nutrients, 16-9-12 (+3+2+15) / 16-3,9-10 (+2,1+1,2+6).” 
EU FPS: EU Fertilising Products. 
Fertiliser “sweepings”: Fertiliser “waste” generated when cleaning production lines, following 
changes in production from one batch to a (chemically) different batch. 
Label: All written and graphic information displayed on a label. Can be fully physical or fully 
digital, or a combination of both formats.  
Label elements: Individual pieces/sections of mandatory/voluntary label information.  
Physical labelling: Labelling solutions based on various types of physical data carriers, holding 
printed label information (eg bags, boxes, stickers, tag labels, leaflets, …). 
Digital labeling: Labelling solutions based on various types of electronic data carriers, holding  
and transmitting digital label information (see further down for some more details). 
Labels users : manufacturers, transporters, warehouse operators, distributors,  surveyors, 
customs and market surveillance authorities, and last but not least the farmers. 
Label/bag “splitting”: The artwork-operational need to “split” an existing physical label/bag in 
two or more labels/bags, as a consequence of adding more (mandatory) information.   
Labelling workflows: The whole chain of managerial and operational activities, carried out by 
EOPs, to produce final label artwork, and make labels available to the intended users. 
OPPs/TPPs: Own-Produced Products / Third Party Products. 
Printing plates: One-time-use metal sheets, essential in offset printing of labels, bags, boxes. 
Time to market: The length of time a new label takes from production till reaching the market.

1. Physical labelling of fertilising products is cumbersome, costly, and eco-unfriendly 
 
Labelling of fertilising products is key for their safe and efficient transport, handling , storage 
and final agricultural use, from factory to field. However, regulatory requirements on labelling 
and packaging materials are becoming increasingly burdensome, in the EU [eg Regulation 
2019/1009; changes to REACH /CLP regulations], and globally . What concerns the EU  in 
particular, the introduction of Regulation 2019/1009 imposes more extensive labelling rules 
than was the case under Regulation (EC) 2003/2003. 
 
Physical labelling and packaging management activities are thus representing increasing 
organizational, operational-, cost -, compliance - and e nvironmental challenges to EU 
EOPs (see section 3), impacting both large and smaller players in the market:  
 
1) Large players. Are typically selling a large and diverse portfolio of products to multiple 
(EU and non-EU) countries. Thus managing hundreds or even thousands of complex 
physical labels/packaging materials, with multi-country/language information, covering 
numerous (mandatory and voluntary) label elements - see examples below. 
2) Small players. May not face label management challenges to the same extent as large 
players, cause managing a smaller product portfolio and lower production volumes. 
They may be disadvantaged though, cause lacking the resources needed to manage 
and optimize complex physical labelling operations (manpower, subject matter 
experts, storage facilities, ERP- and labelling IT tools). 
 
 
 
Examples of CE-marked mineral fertiliser physical labels:  
 
a) Multi-country label, with several EU/non-EU country sub-labels, and diverse label elements. 
b) Single-country label (France), with ao a statement about the permitted use of the product in 
biological agriculture; detailed  agronomic advise; and a voluntary barcode sub-label. 
 

 
 
2. Yara’s view and comments on the Commission’s proposal   
 
Digital labelling is now common in many industries. It is also observed on EU FPR labels, but 
only for making voluntary information available to users. It has multiple tangible benefits (see 
section 4), tackling the hassles typically associated with physical labelling (see section 3). 
 
Yara welcomes the Commission’s proposal to amend Regulation 2019/1009, a proposal which 
aims to facilitate (“partial”) voluntary digital labelling of EU FPs. We fully support the pursued 
key objectives of the proposal, which are as aimed by the Commission: 
 
1) Improving the readability of labels - thus improving their communication effectiveness. 
2) Easing labelling management - thus reducing monetary and environmental costs. 
 
Yet we see room for further improvement of this proposal, without compromising the general 
objectives of Regulation 2019/1009: i) the well-functioning of the EU internal market; and ii) 
ensuring a high level of protection for human health and the environment.  
 
We encourage the Commission to draft a flexible and visionary proposal, embracing 
the opportunities shaped by digital labelling technical progress. To support EU EOPs, 
label users (farmers in particular), and the protection of human health and the environment.  
 
We advocate to broaden the scope of digital labelling (2a); and to leverage the progress and 
use of innovative digital labelling solutions (2b). 
 
2a) Broadening the scope of digital labelling 
 
We support the general labelling requirements for CE-marked mineral fertilisers , set out in 
Annex III of (EU) 2019/1009, and further clarified in (2021/C119/01) “ Communication from the 
Commission concerning the visual appearance of the label on EU fertilising products, referred 
to in Annex III to Regulation 2019/1009 ”. We also support, under the present amendment 
proposal, the label elements already identified as candidates for voluntary digital labelling.  
 
The key question we raise is “Which mandatory EU FPR label elements, currently not in 
scope of the Commission’s proposal, could/should enter into scope?”. From a strictly 
EOPs labelling management point of view, we would argue “The more label elements that can 
be voluntary provided in a suitable digital format, the better!”. Yet to align our objectives with 
important regulatory objectives, the following aspects are to be evaluated: 
 
• Which label elements are “truly essential” to present in a physical format? What 
are the potential (unwanted) consequences if they are not physically displayed? 
• Which label users across the fertiliser supply chain need which label information? 
And what is their readiness to handle digital information? 
• What are the practical pros and cons of physical vs digital labels? 
 
Based on these three yardsticks, we propose to enter the following mandatory EU FPR label 
elements into scope for “physical and/or voluntary digital labelling”: 
 
PFC (Product Function Category) designation. This regulatory information is not essential to 
farmers. It is potentially useful to local market surveillance authorities, yet we anticipate that 
they have the proper knowledge, skills and tools needed to handle digital label information. 
 
“FPR product formula designation”  (see glossary of terms ). We consider this a redundant 
(physical) label element, because i) labels already display detailed nutrient declarations  
(mandatory information) and ii) fertiliser labels typically display “industry-accepted” product 
formulas/grades (voluntary information). Eg: “Urea 26% N” / “NPK 15-15-15 + Mg + S”. 
 
 
Agronomic use instructions:  “Instructions for intended use, including application rates, timing,  
frequency, and target plants or mushrooms” - a new and important label element introduced 
under the EU FPR. We advocate to give EOPs the option to make this information available 
under a physical and/or digital label format, because: 
 
1) On physical labels, due to lack of printing space, EOPs are often forced to give concise 
agronomic use instructions (a couple of lines of text). Yet even then, this element adds 
to “physical label overload”, especially on multi-language/country labels. 
2) Concise use instructions, displayed on a physical label, are of limited value to farmers, 
nor to the benefit of achieving environmental goals (eg nutrient use efficiency). A solid 
agronomic advice must take into account a wide range of crop /site-specific factors. 
Typically, farmers will get such advice via (private or public) agronomic professionals, 
and/or via the use of digital nutrient management tools.  
3) We expect that facilitating access to (voluntary) digital agronomic advice will improve 
the quality of such advice, benefitting farmers and environmental goals. 
 
Nutrient declaration “physico-chemical details and formatting”. We do not elaborate on this for 
now, as this is a fairly detailed product-technical matter. We obviously support proper nutrient 
declarations on fertiliser labels, but question whether certain details (eg on solubility) are really 
“essential” for display on physical labels? Follow-up item for further alignment. 
 
2b) Leveraging the progress and use of innovative digital labelling solutions  
 
We appreciate that the Commission needs more time to evaluate the technological aspects of 
digital labelling. We cheer amendments fostering a more “agile and future-proof” legislation, 
supporting EOPs and label users to embrace novel, effective and cost-efficient digital labelling 
solutions. Taking into account that: 
 
• There are diverse levels of digital readiness among the diverse users of labels.  
• Various mature technological solutions exist (while others are still in progress ), all 
having their specific pros and cons, and different cost implications.  These solutions  
range from “basic” (URL addresses), to “advanced” (bar- or QR codes), to “novel”  
(“smart bags” equipped with NFC/Bluetooth technology or RFID tags). 
• Yara has practical know-how about digital labelling solutions, encompassing electronic  
technologies and specific use-cases (eg carbon credits certification) . We welcome 
further interchanges of ideas on these important matters. 
 
3. Multiple challenges associated with operating (complex) physical labels 
 
• Operational inconvenience of physical printing. The main disadvantage of physical 
labels is that each label change, small or big, requires the printing of new labels. This 
triggers a cascade of operational-, commercial- and compliance challenges, subject to 
label type, extent of the label content/design change, product origin (OPP/TPP), and 
label/packaging supplier lead times. 
• The TTM of new /updated physical labels tends to be l engthy, ranging from several 
weeks to several months, or even longer. 
• Physical labelling limits flexibility to respond quickly and efficiently to changing market 
conditions, such as new (strategic/one-off) business opportunities; regulatory changes; 
logistic changes; new input suppliers; and the introduction or termination of economic 
sanctions (eg Russia). 
• Poor readability and communication efficacy. When legislation introduces new label 
elements, physical labels risk to become unreadable. Because of information overload, 
and (too) small font sizes (used to avoid “label splitting”). Note that at present, the EU 
FPR does not prescribe a minimum font size for label text. 
 
 
• Labels/bags proliferation. Label/bag changes and frequent needs to “split” labels/bags 
lead to an increase in the number of labels/bags produced, stored and handled. Which 
in turn generates operational hassle and risks, delays, and costs.  
• Production breaks. Multiple physical labels and packaging materials complicate batch 
scheduling in production lines  (stop-clean-refill-restart), causing longer lead times in 
production, lower production-efficiency, longer TTM, and more fertiliser sweepings. 
• Cost proliferation. The regular and sporadic costs associated with the production and 
management of physical labels are complex and manifold, comprising ao: 
o Man-hours performed by numerous people involved in the labelling workflow 
(label managers; chemical compliance experts; internal or external artworkers; 
purchasers of packaging materials; logistic operators). 
o Diverse costs related to the sourcing of labelled packaging materials (ao, the  
production of new printing plates, each time a new label is printed). 
o Reduced production-efficiency. 
o Inventory-, storage-, and handling costs. 
o Sporadic costs of manual re-labelling (eg in case of urgent/unforeseen orders; 
reuse of “old” bags). 
o Sporadic costs of shredding and disposal of old bags (external service). 
o Sporadic costs of non-compliance incidents (demurrage fees, fines, lawyer 
fees, product recalls). 
o Investment c osts for introducing dedicated labelling workflow management 
tools (software licenses and consultancy services).  
o The total costs can be very significant , subject to the size of a company , its 
product portfolio, and total production volumes. They can range from a couple 
of thousands up to tens of thousands  of Euros for “regular” label/packaging 
material changes. And up to hundreds of thousands or even millions of Euros 
for “mass” label changes (triggered by national/regional/international regulatory 
changes; such as for example the EU FPR).  
• Plastic waste generation, when destruction of old packaging materials is unavoidable. 
 
4. Multiple benefits associated with operating (complex) digital labels 
 
• Operational convenience of non-printing. The main advantage of digital labelling is that 
changes to label elements in a digital format do not require the printing of new labels. 
Which has several important operational benefits, including: 
o Less different labels/packaging materials and lower inventories, which implies 
less storage facilities requirements and associated costs.  
o Much faster TTM. Depending on the ERP/labelling management tools in place, 
label changes can be executed in real time, or with a slightly longer time span 
(depending on how frequently databases are updated). 
o Less labelling errors. And sporadic labelling errors can quickly be corrected.  
o Faster and better compliance performance. 
• “Digital backup” for old labels/bags in the market. The time span in between the placing 
in the market of a packaged fertiliser lot, and the full depletion of this lot by the end 
users, can be significant, sometimes up to several years. When packag ing materials 
are equipped with digital data carriers, the users are always able to access the latest 
label information updates.  
• Significant (opportunities) for labelling costs savings. 
• Less plastic waste. 
• Easier and better internal quality control and auditing of labels.  
• Enhanced readability of the physical labels. With (partial) digital labelling, the physical 
labels become less heavy and can adopt larger fonts. 
 
 
 
• Enhanced information delivery. EOPs can (voluntary) provide additional information, 
better tailored to the specific needs of diverse label users, for example: 
o Agronomic use instructions (see above). 
o Handling and storage instructions. Under EU FPR, physical labels must display 
handling and storage instructions, but it is not detailed how this has to be done. 
Due to limited space on physical labels, it is pragmatic to opt for only graphical 
instructions (pictograms). Digital labelling encourages the (voluntary) option to 
provide additional and more detailed written instructions. 
o Links to Safety Data Sheets can easily be made when packaging materials are 
equipped with electronic data carriers.  
o Links to diverse environmental sustainability information and/or services. 
o Assorted other information and services. Digital developments go fast, and it is 
hard to predict how the future of digital labelling will look like. We anticipate 
though that the introduction of a forward-looking regulatory framework will 
stimulate and accelerate innovative labelling solutions, to the benefit of all EU 
FPs stakeholders, and to society and the environment at large.

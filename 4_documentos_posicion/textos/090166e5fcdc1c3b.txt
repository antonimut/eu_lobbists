Response of the Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation (GLEIF) to the European Commission’s Consultation on the Proposal for a DIRECTIVE OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL amending Directives 2009/102/EC and (EU) 2017/1132 as regards further expanding and upgrading the use of digital tools and processes in company law,  as published on 29 March 2023   7 June 2023   Introduction  The Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation (GLEIF) is pleased to provide feedback to the European Commission's Consultation on the Proposal for a DIRECTIVE OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL amending Directives 2009/102/EC and (EU) 2017/1132 as regards further expanding and upgrading the use of digital tools and processes in company law.  GLEIF will focus its comments on the (potential) use of the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) in the Company Law Directive.  There are a few topics in the proposal that are related to the (potential) use of the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI; see also ‘about the LEI’ at the bottom of this document): - The use of the European Unique Identifier (EUID); - The introduction of a digital EU Company Certificate, authenticated by using trust services as referred to in the eIDAS Regulation; - The introduction of a multilingual standard model for a digital EU power of attorney which will authorise a person to represent the company in another Member State; this will be authenticated by using trust services as referred to in the eIDAS Regulation and has to be compatible with the European Digital Identity Wallet.

Opinions endorsing the LEI The European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB)1 recommended in September 2020 an EU framework on the use of the Legal Entity Identifier for all legal entities involved in financial transactions by June 2023. The ESRB notably asks the European Commission to propose:  Union legislation incorporates a common Union legal framework governing the identification of legal entities established in the Union that are involved in financial transactions by way of a legal entity identifier (LEI), paying due regard to the principle of proportionality, taking into account the need to prevent or mitigate systemic risk to financial stability in the Union and thereby achieving the objectives of the internal market. Similarly, the joint-European Commission and European Central Bank Committee on Monetary, Financial and Balance of Payments Statistics (CMFB) comes strongly in favour of the use of the Legal Entity Identifier in a May 2023 Opinion2, it notably:  encourages all initiatives which help broaden the coverage and use cases of the LEI, minimizing or even eliminating the cost of issuance and renewals for European companies. (…) asks the Commission to take into account the statistical usefulness of a broader LEI coverage when reporting on whether or not to make the LEI mandatory for a wider range of legal entities across the EU All legal entities are meant to be involved in financial transactions – whether it is the multinational European headquartered business that wants to operate across the globe, the local business that buys and sells products, or the association that receives donations to fund its activities. The use of a European identifier and a global identifier The proposal to amend the Company Law Directive calls for the use of the European Unique Identifier (EUID). In this context – i.e. fostering better tools for cross-border identification of businesses – it would be worth considering the value of a global identification scheme that applies even beyond EU borders. European companies do not stop doing business at the borders of the EU; and EU authorities and companies equally need information about non-EU entities. Furthermore a global identification scheme enables discovery of complex group structures or legal arrangements that involve both EU based and non-EU based entities.5 The EUID (an EU only identifier) is very relevant for use within the EU, but will not help when it comes to businesses outside the EU or EU-based companies doing business outside the Union. The EUID could be compared to a national (or regional) ID card whereas the LEI is a passport to do business on a global scale. This means that the EUID and the LEI perfectly function alongside each other and complement each other.

Furthermore the EUID is only relevant for companies, less than half the legal entities existing in the EU. The LEI is relevant for all unique parties that are legally or financially responsible for the performance of financial transactions or have the legal right in their jurisdiction to enter independently into legal contracts, regardless of whether they are incorporated or constituted in some other way (e.g. trust, partnership, contractual). It includes governmental organizations, supranationals and individuals when acting in a business capacity), but excludes natural persons. It also includes international branches. As such the LEI can serve as a passport for doing business across borders globally but also as a tool for comprehensively understanding legal entities doing business within the EU.  And, looking from the other side, already using the LEI in systems in the EU would also make it much easier to track non-EU legal entities. Transparency and clarity about non-EU entities are more needed than ever today, both for the purpose of simply properly identifying non-EU counterparts but certainly also for anti-money laundering purposes. As noted in the recent publication by the Bank of International Settlements Project Aurora – the power of data, technology and collaboration to combat money laundering across institutions and borders, money laundering is a global problem that undermines the integrity and safety of the global financial system. The amount of money laundered globally is estimated to be between 2 and 5% of global GDP or between $2 trillion and $5 trillion. However the estimated total sum seized annually amounts to less than 1% of this – between $20 billion and $50 billion. FATF has identified that data-sharing, advanced analytics and data standardization are critical for effective AML and CFT efforts. The LEI is the first layer of data standardization required to improve AML/CFT efforts. The LEI could help identify a greater range of money laundering activities involving legal entities. The LEI could connect a greater range of data sets and capture different relationships which could be useful in AML efforts. It could also address the challenges faced by monitoring systems from inconsistencies in how entities are identified. As noted in the project Aurora report, it takes a network to defeat a network.  Furthermore, it is widely considered that most business is local, especially in the SME space. This is true when looking at the customer base. This changes completely when looking at the supplier side. Virtually every business today sources goods and services from other parts of the globe, with lots of burdens on SMEs, such as language barriers and access to register information. Another good example is access to capital markets where many investors, lenders and trade finance platforms sit outside the EU.

Adding the LEI to the Company Law Directive When considering to add the LEI to the current text of the Company Law Directive, the following items could be in scope: - Adding a recital, such as: o ‘To enhance transparency further, especially in doing business internationally, also with third parties outside the Union, Member States will issue a Legal Entity Identifier (LEI), according to ISO standard 17442, for each of the companies for which a file has been or will be opened in a central, commercial or companies register.’ - Adding text to Article 16 section 1, such as: o Member States shall ensure that for each such file a unique company number is issued as well as a Legal Entity Identifier (LEI), according to ISO standard 17442. - Adding text to Article 19 section 2 sub (c), such as: o the registration number of the company and its Legal Entity Identifier (LEI). - Adding text to Article 30 section 1 sub (c), such as: o the register in which the company file referred to in Article 16 is kept, together with the registration number in that register and the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) of the company, if available; - Adding text to Article 37 sub (d), such as: o where that law so provides, the register in which the company is entered and the registration number of the company in that register and the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) of the company, if available; - Adding text to Article 123 section 2 sub (b), such as: o the register in which the documents referred to in Article 16(3) are filed in respect of each merging company, and the number of the entry in that register and the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) of each merging company;

The new digital EU Company Certificate The proposal to introduce a digital EU Company Certificate, authenticated by using trust services as referred to in the eIDAS Regulation, brings by itself three key assets: Authentication, Integrity and Non-Repudiation.   The inclusion of the LEI into the certificate following the LEI ISO 17442-2 standard will add an extra layer of security authentication wise. By parsing and accessing the GLEIF global database all legal entity data can be accessed which should match with the records indicated in the certificate.  Additional fields indicating corroboration and validation levels regarding the legal entity reference data are accessible too. And the LEI Reference Data will always contain information on the registration in the national business register and the register number, which could be linked to BRIS for automated navigation across the 2 systems. A related example is the use of the LEI in the Japanese eSeal initiative together with the EU.  The new Digital EU power of attorney The introduction of a multilingual standard model for a digital EU power of attorney will authorise a person to represent the company in another Member State; this will be authenticated by using trust services as referred to in the eIDAS Regulation and has to be compatible with the European Digital Identity Wallet.  Including the LEI into the legal representative’s eIDAS certificate following the LEI ISO 17442-2 standard will add an extra layer of security authentication wise (as noted above). This certificate may be added as an attribute to those EU wallets that are based on eIDAS certificates.   Additionally, a verifiable credential that contains the role of the legal representative in relation to the legal entity associated, can be issued to him/her. This is the ‘vLEI’ (verifiable LEI). Member States could further issue vLEI credentials for each of the companies for which a file has been or will be opened in a central, commercial or companies register. This would enable EU businesses to conduct business digitally in a globally recognized and secure way all the while ensuring compliance with eIDAS2 and its European Wallet Reference Architecture.

About the LEI The ISO 17442 LEI was developed under the auspices of the G20 leaders and the Financial Stability Board during the 2008 financial crisis to create transparency amongst counterparties to financial transactions. GLEIF’s day-to-day work is overseen by more than 65 public authorities – including 25 from the EU.The LEI is an interoperable, open, and machine-readable identifier. It is available to all legal entities worldwide and is already in use in over 75 pieces of EU legislations for reporting purposes (e.g., the European Single Electronic Format (ESEF), the Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID), the European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR), the Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive AIFMD, the Transparency Directive and many others). The political drivers behind the LEI initiative, including the G20, the FSB, and the European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) have emphasised the need to make the LEI a broad public good. The Global LEI Index provides complete LEI data at the disposal of any interested party, conveniently and free of charge. This includes real-time snapshots of key company data and provides a history of any changes to it. In addition, data access is conveniently provided via website search functions, full file downloads and an Application Programming Interface (API). It also enables automated security checks where the LEI of the filing entity is also embedded in the signing tools or digital certificate About GLEIF The Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation (GLEIF) is the not-for-profit foundation that manages the ISO 17442 Legal Entity Identifier (LEI).

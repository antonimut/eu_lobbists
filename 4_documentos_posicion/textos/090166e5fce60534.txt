Born Free feedback: Revision of the Union Customs Code  
 
9 June 2023 
 
Born Free welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the revised Union Customs Code. 
Our organisation has a long history working on wildlife trade and trafficking and we believe 
Member States’ Customs authorities play a central role in the regulation and control of wild 
animals and wildlife products that enter, circulate within and are exported from the EU.  
This role needs to be clearly recognised and its importance raised in the revised Union Customs 
Code in order to effectively address the threats wildlife trade and trafficking present to human 
health, biodiversity, the environment, national security, and animal welfare. 
Specific measures should be adopted ensuring that wildlife trade is prioritised by Customs 
authorities, and that adequate resources, training and tools are provided to enable those 
authorities to effectively monitor, control and act upon instances of wildlife trafficking. This should 
be reflected in the relevant sections of the proposed revised Regulation. 
THE RISKS ASSOCIATED WITH WILDLIFE TRADE AND NEED FOR TIGHTER CONTROLS  
Wildlife trade (both legal and illegal) poses serious risks to conservation, biosecurity, local 
and national economies, national security and animal welfare . The EU is a major 
destination, transit hub and source region for the trade in and trafficking of wildlife. It is 
therefore important to ensure that EU policies and their application enable legal trade to be 
tightly controlled, and illegal trade to be vigorously addressed .  
• The line between legal and illegal wildlife trade is often blurred. Legal activities can 
stimulate the demand for wildlife products and provide pathways for traffickers to launder 
illegal products, undermining enforcement and wildlife protection efforts. T he Covid-19 
pandemic has also highlighted the public health risks associated wildlife: in a 2020 report, 
the Intergovernmental Science -Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services 
(IPBES) identified wildlife trade – both legal and illegal – as a major driver of the global 
biodiversity and extinction crisis and an important risk factor for zoonotic diseases. 
 
• Illegal wildlife trade, also referred to as wildlife trafficking, is the fourth largest and one of 
the most profitable crimes worldwide, estimated to be worth $US7-23 billion according to the 
UN Environment Programme, and as much as US$69-199 billion when illegal logging and 
fishing are included. Considered a ‘low risk – high reward’ activity by criminals, illegal wildlife 
trade is often conducted by transnational organised crime networks that may also be 
involved in drug and human trafficking, money laundering and other serious crimes, creating 
substantial liabilities in terms of governance and security in the countries involved. 
 
• By its very nature, illegal wildlife trade also presents particularly high risks to wildlife 
conservation, biosecurity and animal welfare. Trafficking activities increase pressures on 
populations of endangered and protected animals. Since traffickers pay no regard for 
biosecurity protocols or animal welfare, they also create the ideal conditions for the 
emergence and spread of and pathogens, some of which may have zoonotic potential.

RECOMMENDATIONS  
The specific risks associated wildlife trade should be prioritised in the new Customs Regulation 
to ensure that Member State Customs authorities are provided with the necessary tools, 
training and resources to monitor, control and act upon instances of wildlife trafficking 
effectively. The new Regulation should also send a strong political message that wildlife 
trafficking is a serious crime that will be treated with the highest priority by customs 
authorities, and in a harmonised way, across the EU. 
1. Prioritise wildlife trafficking in customs activities 
In spite of increasing international recognition of wildlife trafficking as a serious crime, most 
recently through UN Resolution 75/311 from July 2021, it remains inadequately regulated and 
insufficiently controlled in the EU, as recognised in the European Commission’s Roadmap for 
the revision of the EU Action Plan against Wildlife Trafficking published in October 2021. The 
new Union Customs Code must urgently address these failings by making wildlife 
trafficking a key priority for EU and Member State customs services. This should ideally be 
reflected in the Regulation in order to send a strong and clear message to Member States. 
• Increased priority would notably translate into enhanced scrutiny and control  (cf. Article 
43 of the proposed Regulation) of shipments entering the EU, that could contain live wild 
animals or wildlife products, and the associated documentation. Many illegal wildlife 
specimens enter the EU using inadequate or fraudulent documentation .   
➢ Articles 63 and 64 of the proposed Regulation stipulate that “all goods intended to be 
placed under a customs procedure shall be covered by a customs declaration” that "shall 
contain all the particulars necessary for application of the provisions governing the 
customs procedure for which the goods are declared" and that "the Commission shall 
specify, by means of implementing acts, the procedure for lodging the standard customs 
declaration." We understand that the intention is for most declarations to be submitted 
electronically in the future. In the case of live wild animals or plants and wildlife products 
covered by international or national regulations, this should include relevant 
health/sanitary certification, export and import permits, official declarations of legal 
acquisition and non-detriment, and evidence that no stricter measures have been imposed 
restricting the trade in the products into or from the EU, or from the country of origin (such 
as CITES compliance decisions or negative opinions of the EU Scientific Review Group). 
 
• Increased priority would also require the Commission to establish common priority 
control areas and common risk criteria and standards and provide policy 
orientations for the risks associated with wildlife trade and trafficking, as well as ensure 
these are reflected in the EU and national customs risk management and mitigation 
plans referred to under Chapter 3 of the Regulation proposal.   
 
➢ The Covid-19 pandemic has demonstrated that zoonotic diseases can have serious 
impacts on human and animal health and welfare, as well as on the wider economy. 
Epidemics and pandemics of animal origin are becoming increasingly common, and 
scientists warn o f the increasing risk of future zoonotic pathogen emergence, 
proliferation and spill-over. The EU Customs Code has an important role to play. It 
should include risk management and mitigation measures to help prevent the 
emergence of zoonotic pathogens from  wildlife trade activities through the adoption of 
precautionary, binding, biosecurity protocols  at customs points across the EU. A 
reference to such protocols should be included in the Regulation.

➢ Many wildlife species traded in the EU are protected under International (CITES) and 
EU law (Wildlife Trade Regulations - WTR) because of their conservation status. The 
WTR require import permits for Annex A and Annex B species, to verify that the import 
of these species to the EU is legal and non -detrimental to the conservation status of the 
population from which it is derived. However, the requirement for import permits for 
hunting trophies only applies to those species in Annex A, and the six Annex B species 
listed in Annex XIII to Regulation (EC ) No 865/2006. The import permit requirement 
should be extended to all hunting trophies from Annex B species , in order to 
simplify and harmonise requirements, and facilitate enforcement.  
    
2. Appropriate and sufficient tools, training and resources 
Given the transnational nature of wildlife trafficking, the harmonisation and standardisation of 
customs rules and procedures at EU level is essential to enhance the capability and 
effectiveness of customs services in tackling these activities. We welcome the elem ents of the 
Regulation proposal that aim to enhance the cooperation and collaboration of Customs 
authorities within and across Member States, and at international level, as well as those that 
aim to establish homogenised tools and processes to facilitate t his work. 
• We welcome the establishment of an EU Customs data hub . This tool must be designed 
to ensure robust and consistent data collection and intelligence sharing, in addition to 
being user-friendly to encourage and facilitate access and reporting by the competent 
authorities at all levels. 
 
➢ To facilitate efforts by the EU and Member States to effectively regulate wildlife trade and 
combat wildlife trafficking, all wildlife trade data should be recorded in the EU 
Customs data hub (including both CITES and non-CITES-listed specimens entering, 
leaving and transiting through the EU). Such data would facilitate the work of customs 
authorities, but also enable effective monitoring of the impact of EU efforts to tackle 
wildlife trafficking, particularly in the framework of the implementation of the revised EU 
Action Plan Against Wildlife Trafficking. It would also inform decision-making in relation to 
future EU and Member State laws and policies in this area, and inform EU positions at 
CITES and other international forums. 
 
➢ The EU-TWIX database could be integrated within the EU Customs data hub to 
create a unique data compilation platform and centralise reporting processes for 
competent authorities and institutions. The data currently collected under EU-TWIX 
should be expanded to non-CITES-listed species. Also, the following wildlife -related data 
should be collected in the new hub: 
- The species 
- The type of specimen (live wild animal / type of wildlife product) 
- The number or quantity of specimens 
- The country of origin 
- The country of destination 
- The legal status of the species, including whether it is protected internationally or 
under EU law (including under which Annex of the WTR) 
- Relevant incidents (e.g. mortality of live specimens in transit) 
- Whether the import / export is legal or illegal under EU legislation (legal vs illegal trade) 
- Purpose of trade (e.g. pet trade, medicine, food, fashion, hunting trophy, etc)

• As part of the identification measures referred to in Article 47 of the Regulation proposal, 
competent services within customs authorities should be provided with adequate and 
homogeneous training across Member States, and supported by mentorship networks 
and guidance established at EU level . 
 
➢ Homogeneous and customed training should be provided to the competent services within 
customs authorities on wildlife regulations, specimen identification, and the management 
and disposal of seized or confiscated live wild animals and wildlife products.  
 
➢ Guides to facilitate species identification are available for some taxa covered by 
international regulations. Further training and guidance should be sought through the 
development of partnerships with relevant taxono mic experts and those with expertise 
in the handling and management of live specimens, including wildlife rescue centres 
and associated networks. 
 
• The development of EU guidance on the disposal of wildlife products  should be 
included in Chapter 4 on Disposal of goods of the revised Regulation. Wildlife products, 
and particularly live animals in trade, require specific management measures given the 
biosecurity/phytosanitary risk they may present. The management and disposal of live wild 
animals must be carried out with due regard for relevant international and EU regulations 
and associated guidance and to ensure compliance with Article 13 of the Treaty on the 
Functioning of the European Union which states that live animals are sentient beings, and 
that full regard should be paid to their welfare requirements.  
 
➢ CITES Resolution Conf. 17.8 (Rev. CoP19) regarding the Disposal of illegally traded 
and confiscated specimens of CITES -listed species provides guidance on the disposal 
of confiscated specimens. However, these guidelines are not comprehensive or 
particularly user-friendly, and are not consistently applied by Membe r States, 
particularly in relation to the management of confiscated live animals. Among key 
issues, they do not provide indications on the appropriate handling and management of 
live wild animals from the point of seizure or confiscation. They also lack sa fety / 
biosecurity / phytosanitary considerations, which are vital given that customs authorities 
are typically the first point of contact with potentially dangerous wild animals or wildlife 
products arriving on EU territory. To address these omissions, we  recommend that the 
Regulation proposal includes: 
- The development of comprehensive and user-friendly EU guidelines for the 
management of live wild animals aligned with the above-mentioned CITES 
guidelines (CITES Resolution Conf. 17.8), and the IUCN Guidelines for the 
management of confiscated live organisms. The new guidelines should aim to identify 
and address areas not adequately covered in existing guidelines, ensure the humane 
and safe management of confiscated animals at all stages, from their seizure or 
confiscation through to their disposal, and deliver on relevant associated EU policies 
and objectives. They should include additional guidance on immediate handling 
and care at the point of confiscation, as well as on biosecurity, health and s afety 
protocols, and be designed to support Member States in the coordination of rescue, 
transport and placement of confiscated/seized animals. To facilitate their use and 
effectiveness, they should constitute a single comprehensive source of information, 
and provide simple and clear guidance on all relevant elements. When designing 
these guidelines, the following points should be considered: 
▪ The need for immediate action, prioritising animal welfare and human safety, 
where there is suspicion of illegal trade involving live animals.  
5 
 
▪ Biosecurity / phytosanitary protocols should be included to ensure customs 
services know how to handle potentially dangerous wildlife and wildlife products 
appropriately, in order to prevent injury and the transfer of zoonotic diseases.  
▪ The guidelines should recommend the destruction of confiscated wildlife 
products in order to prevent them re -entering trade, or wh ere appropriate, 
their donation to appropriate public entities for genuine educational and 
conservation purposes. 
▪ The guidelines should prioritise swift decision -making and action when live 
animals are involved, in order to prioritise animal welfare.  
▪ While EU guidelines are being developed, Member States should implement 
the IUCN’s guidelines for the management of con fiscated live organisms. 
- The EU should establish a network of species-specific experts  who can be 
contacted at the time of confiscation to help confiscating authorities identify the 
species, provide guidance on appropriate handling and care including biose curity 
and safety measures, and advise on disposal.  
- EU Member states should be required to develop National Action Plans on the 
disposal of confiscated live wild animals , as per Annex 3 of CITES Resolution 
Conf. 17.8 (Rev. Cop19), which fulfil international and EU legislative requirements, 
align with the proposed EU guidelines, and clearly define tasks, roles and 
responsibilities ensuring inter -agency cooperation. 
 
• Adequate national and EU budgets must be allocated to support the measures detailed 
above, as well as facilitating the monitoring and control of customs activities. Given the 
increasing trend in wildlife trade and trafficking, associated customs activities will inevitably 
increase. This should be anticipated and reflected in the allocation of resources. 
 
3. The need for an increased focus on customs tools and training to combat online 
wildlife trafficking   
We welcome the Commission’s recognition of the risks associated with the increase in e-commerce. 
Wildlife traffickers increasingly use online platforms and networks to facilitate illegal wildlife trade. 
Such platforms and networks offer greater opportunities to criminals, while being harder for 
enforcement authorities to monitor and control. We strongly support the establishment of a 
structured framework for cooperation between customs authorities at national, Union and 
international level, to help address online trafficking activities (cf. Recital 58).  
However, the measures relating to e -commerce included in the draft revised Regulation mostly 
focus on VAT requirements for online sellers, as well as references to the data they must 
provide to the customs a uthorities to secure the release of their goods for free circulation in the 
EU. Additional measures must be included on the role that customs authorities have to 
play to monitor, control and act upon cases of online trafficking .   
• Effective control systems must be put in place to help customs authorities identify, share 
information with foreign counterparts when relevant, and act upon illegally traded 
wildlife on online platforms.  
 
• Processes should be established to ensure that customs authorities work efficiently 
alongside digital service providers to strengthen traceability and checks on traders in 
online marketplaces to tackle illegal content, and ensure products placed on the Single 
Market are safe, as per the obligations of the Digital Services Act.

• Specific training should be organised at EU level for customs authorities on the 
identification of suspicious online wildlife trade activities, and the actions that should be 
prioritised in a homogeneous way across the EU.  
 
➢ A paper entitled ‘Assessing online wildlife marketing - A tool for monitoring illegality’ by 
the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organised Crime provides guidance to 
facilitate recognition of suspicious advertisements and to prioritise action. 
 
4. Remarks on the origin of goods (Chapter 2 of the Regulation proposal) 
We note that the Regulation proposal includes a number of rules around the origin of goods. 
Alongside our partner organisations, Born Free has been advocating for the development and 
adoption of new legislation to close loopholes in the EU wi ldlife trade legislative framework. 
Currently, wildlife specimens from species protected in and exported illegally from their country 
of origin are frequently imported to the EU, where they can be traded legally.  
As a result of the adoption of the revised EU Action Plan Against Wildlife Trafficking, the 
European Commission will be commissioning a feasibility study to assess the possibility of 
adopting legislation that would outlaw the import and trade in wildlife p roducts obtained 
illegally in their country of origin . The creation of appropriate mechanisms within the 
proposed EU Customs data hub  would facilitate the implementation of such legislation  
by customs authorities, which would contribute to the conservation  efforts of third countries.  
 
 
 
We thank you again for this opportunity to share our feedback and we are available 
to provide further information. 
 
About the Born Free Foundation  
Born Free is a UK -based international wildlife protection charity. We promote compassionate 
conservation to enhance the survival of threatened species in the wild and protect natural 
habitats while respecting the needs and safeguarding the welfare of individual animals. As a 
leading wildlife charity, we oppose the exploitati on of wild animals in captivity and campaign to 
keep them where they belong – in the wild.

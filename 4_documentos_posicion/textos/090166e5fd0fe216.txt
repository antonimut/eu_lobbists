Introduction 
ASD welcomes the European Commission’s legislative proposal for a Regulation aimed at securing the supply 
of critical and strategic raw materials.  Given the EU’s aerospace and defence sector’s almost exclusive reliance 
on raw materials imports, we strongly support attempts by EU decision-makers to build more resilience in the 
supply of CRMs. It is crucial for the proposed measures  to be successful to ensure coherence with other EU 
policies affecting the materials sector, in particular with the chemicals regulation. Overall, ASD supports the 
Commission’s ambitious proposal, but the latter is only a partial response to the CRM needs of the aerospace 
and defence industry.  
ASD wishes to provide considerations on the Commission’s legislative proposal, which includes several points 
that need to be clarified. 
 
1. The scope of the CRM Act only partially covers our aerospace and defence needs  
 
1.1 ASD welcomes t he newly developed concept of strategic technologies. However, we regret that it only 
encompasses the defence and space part of our sector but not its civil aeronautics leg. While defence and 
space must remain in scope, we call for the inclusion of aerospace and defence as a whole for two reasons: 
○ There is no resilience of the defence sector without the volumes of the aerospace sector, both 
are intricately linked  as aero space covers both military and civilian platforms and 
technologies. Therefore, there is no possible EU CRM strategy for defence and space without 
civil aeronautics because the consumption of CRMs by the defence and space sector only is 
not sufficient to develop the sector’s resilience.  
○ The CRM industry is capital -intensive and requires common understanding of action at 
European rather than national level to bring certain ty to investors and end -users of critical 
raw materials. To illustrate this point, implementation of Article 23 (company risk 
preparedness) in the regulation proposal can become difficult for large pan -European 
aerospace and defence companies. “ Member States shall identify the large companies that 
manufacture strategic technologies using strategic raw materials on their territory ”. Indeed, 
it may come that some products of one pan -European aerospace and defence company are 
considered “strategic technologies” in a Member State but not in another one where it has a 
similarly important footprint. A way to overcome this issue is to include commercial aircraft 
as a strategic technology. 
Aerospace is a strategically important industry to the EU with global aerospace manufacturers 
generating significant revenues and which are dependent on critical raw materials in their 
supply chain.  Lack of access to these could result in customer concerns about resilience of 
supply chains which could impact European aerospace losing its global market share. 
       Therefore, we call for the inclusion of aerospace (instead of space) into the definition of strategic raw 
materials, strategic technologies and thereby, strategic projects.

1.2. ASD welcomes the introduction of a harmonized definition and methodology for critical and strategic raw 
materials. In particular, the new concept of "strategic raw materials" (SRMs) will help focus efforts and 
guide EU action where it is most needed. In li ght of the rising geopolitical tensions and resource 
competition, some key materials for the aerospace and defence sector are listed in the SRM list and must 
remain in the core focus of the text: 
 
○ Titanium: Considering the high vulnerability of EU’s titanium supply chain (the level of 
extraction, refining and production in the EU being 0% for this raw material) and its use for 
critical application (health, energy, space, aeronautics, defence…), it is essential to keep this 
raw material in the new SRM list. However, the designation “Titanium metal’ must 
imperatively encompass aerospace grade titanium1. Indeed, the aerospace industry continues 
to suffer from supply disruptions for this specific grade of titanium. The inclusion or the 
confirmation that a erospace grade titanium is included in the SRM list would encourage 
investment in this highly concentrated market and support existing European players in their 
development to become more competitive. 
○ Nickel: This raw material is fundamental for forming su per alloys used in the aerospace and 
defence equipment (in particular where excellent strength and creep resistance at high 
temperatures are required), while our industry faces significant stresses in the nickel -based 
super alloy s supply chain. ASD deplore s that this SRM is narrowed to battery grade and 
recommends removing this restrictive denomination to include nickel for superalloys, which 
are critical components for several key aircraft equipment with civil and military application. 
○ Rare Earths Elements: Given the crucial importance of rare earths elements in electronic 
components used in aerospace and defence products as well as China's over dominance in this 
market (98% of rare earth production), we call on the European Commission not to limit these 
to Rare Earth Elements for magnets when listing this raw material as strategic.  
○ Cobalt, silicon metalloid, platinum group metals: ASD welcomes the European Commission’s 
decision to include these three raw materials in the SRM list, as they are essential components 
in our products with supply chains increasingly under threat. For example, cobalt is used as a 
superalloy in safety-critical parts of aerospace and defence products, but China controls about 
70% of the cobalt mines in the Democratic Republic of Congo, which is the EU's main supplier 
of this raw material. Similarly, it is urgent to secure the EU's supply of silicon metalloid, a key 
raw material used in semiconductor technologies and for which China dominates 76% of 
global refining capacity.2 
 
However, other materials would benefit from being listed as SRM in the Act:

○ Aluminium: our industry sector relies on the crucial supply of aluminium as a key component 
in aeronautics, defence and space structures, which often have similar composition. 
Aluminium is also a key enabler in the green transition. While we do recognize that the ore on 
which it is derived (bauxite) is on the new Critical Raw Materials list, it would greatly benefit 
from the prioritized activities outlined in the CRM Act if it were included in the list of Strategic 
Raw Materials.  We therefore advocate for its inclusion in the SRM list. 
 
2. ASD regrets the absence of any measures to level the playing field in the package and the lack of 
financing measures to meet the EU’s CRM ambition  
 
2.1. The level playing field point from ASD in its position paper prior to the CRM Act remains valid. ASD 
recommends dedicated measures to level the playing field for CRMs contributing to European strategic 
autonomy, in today’s energy geopolitical context in particular. The examples of the loss of a magnesium 
industry in Europe could be a basis for lessons learn ed to protect EU CRM industries facing unbalanced 
economic conditions in certain third countries. Some foreign companies benefit from national preference 
for their strategic sectors. In the EU, the CRM strategy will need to combine defence and civilian industry 
requirements to secure volumes for the whole sector’s resilience. Consequently, one of the main policy 
objectives of the CRM law should be to ensure a level playing field with third country competitors for EU 
civil volumes. 
 
2.2. In addition, ASD also recommends beefing up the EU’s trade defence instruments to level the playing field 
vis-a-vis third countries having unfair trade practices.  
 
2.3. The extraterritorial effect of certain U.S. law implementation measures could hamper European defence 
and aviation industry in their access to strategic or critical raw materials.  The EU needs to support 
impacted companies through a strategic dialogue with partners, to avoid pre-emption of raw materials to 
occur. 
 
2.4. Undertakings benefiting from EU funds should g uarantee access to raw materials to critical sectors on 
European soil and prevail over other extraterritorial jurisdictions, even if they originate from a third 
country. 
 
2.5. ASD supports the concept of Strategic Projects; however, there is a real risk of making it an empty shell if 
no dedicated funding accompanies it and if playing field conditions are not levelled vis a vis third country. 
The CRM industry is capital intensive and requires long learning curves due to technical complexities 
inducing long-term breakeven. Consequently, CRM investors in the EU will require funding support and 
long-term visibility on level playing field conditions at the same time. 
  
2.6. ASD welcomes the fact that Strategic Projects would gain easier access to financing and faster permitting 
amongst others. However, we regret that there is no dedicated funding associated with the CRM Act . 
Without public support, this initiative puts at risk the EU’s ambition to set up complete SRM value chains 
from extraction , mining, processing, refining  up to recycling  and developing substitutions. Providing

support only at the national level risks fragmenting the EU’s approach. We welcome that the 
accompanying EC Communication COM (2023)165 proposes an envelope for Circularity Hubs (€200 
million), as recycling processes represents one of the key levers to reduce the European industry 
dependencies as regard SRM, but we regret that this Communication does not provide for any funding for 
the other segments of the SRM value chain, in particular for mining to refining projects. ASD therefore 
suggests launching a dedicated EU direct grant programme of a magnitude similar to that of the European 
Chips Act. The yet ill-defined European Sovereignty Fund could well be used for this purpose.  Moreover, 
with 10 years of growth perspective ahead for European industry, this would contribute to  the 
improvement of EU employment.  
 
2.7. ASD regrets that the Temporary Transition and Crisis Framework  recently adopted by the European 
Commission does not allow for the relaxation of state aid in the value chain of raw materials used in the 
strategic aerospace and defence sectors as it only encompasses the production and recovery of raw 
materials "for the transition to the net-zero economy" (defined in the Communication as batteries, solar 
panels, wind turbines, heat pumps, electrolysers and CCUS3), thereby leaving our sector out of the benefits 
of this framework.  
 
2.8. Although the European Commission excluded the idea of setting up a specific CRM fund within its impact 
assessment, ASD suggests earmarking a dedicated financial envelope for project support to the different 
segments of the SRM value chain during the next MFF mid-term review. Nevertheless, this should not be 
at the expense of other existing priority programs, such as Horizon Europe and its Clean joint undertakings 
(such as Clean Aviation), which aim to accelerate the ecological and energy transition of the a erospace 
sector.  
 
3. ASD concurs with potential new constraints to develop risk monitoring and mitigation solutions from 
the upstream industry (mining) to downstream industry (strategic technologies) , provided that the EU 
supports the creation of CRM capabilities and short-term resilience solutions 
 
3.1. The introduction of supply risk monitoring along the CRM value chain by the European Commission (Article 
19) is key to increasing risk-awareness and anticipation. ASD takes note of the provisions to make results 
of the stress tests conducted by the European Commission publicly available. While this will allow industry 
to better identify its vulnerabilities and – where possible - adopt its business model with a view to 
mitigating supply risks, it will also expose the most strategic industries’ supply chain weaknesses. 
 
Hence constraints need to be counterbalanced with de-risking action plans encompassing the 
development of capabilities and short-term resilience solutions to bridge the gap.

3.2. To avoid jeopardizing the competitiveness of European companies, a solution could consist in sharing the 
results of the stress tests only with the companies that have accepted to share information on their supply 
chain. Based on the concept of reciprocity, this would prevent possible use of such information to gain a 
competitive advantage at the expense of European companies. 
 
3.3. During the information gathering process of a stress test, it is of utmost importance to guarantee the 
confidentiality of information  and data  communicated by companies. Article 44 (treatment of 
confidential information) needs to be strengthened by specifically excluding company-based reporting and 
proposing sector aggregated data gathering.  
 
The aerospace and defence sector is particularly concerned about the survey that Member States would 
be empowered to send to the key market operators along the CRM value chain (Article 20) when 
monitoring their activities. In addition to the risks of leakage of confidential infor mation (whose 
consequences could be even more damaging when it comes to information from the aerospace/defence 
sector), this survey could create overburden for European industry. 
 
Therefore, ASD companies could agree to participate in the monitoring exercise, with the caveat that the 
confidentiality of sensitive trade data is enhanced.  A way to protect confidentiality could be that national 
trade associations consolidate the aggregation of data, anonymize them in a sort of black box and convey 
the final processed data to public authorities. 
 
3.4. Finally, ASD calls for clarification regarding monitoring and risk mitigation: 
o On how far large companies need to audit their supply chains (Tier-X) referenced in Article 23; 
o On how the Commission will monitor that audits and stress testing requirements aimed at large 
companies that manufacture strategic technologies using SRMs (Article 23 “ Company risk 
preparedness”) have been carried out and reported to the companies’ board of directors.  
 
4. The concepts of strategic stocks and joint purchasing need to be clarified  
 
4.1. The European Commission is going in the right direction with tools such as strategic stocks to increase 
resilience as an interim solution before strengthening EU CRM capabilities. ASD considers strategic stocks 
as one lever amongst the tools that can correct an urgent supply vulnerability , on a temporary basis. 
However, stocks must imperatively be managed by industry.  
 
4.2. ASD welcomes the idea of stock coordination by the Commission, based on inputs from Member States as 
long as financial incentives are made for private stakeholders to develop strategic stocks while not putting 
at risk their global competitiveness , and that costs are monitored to prevent steep raises . Such financial 
incentives could include for example the financing of the working capital costs incurred by companies for 
financing requirements to increase stockpiling above operational level to reach resilience.

4.3. ASD supports the fact that joint purchasing at EU level remains on a voluntary basis. However, ASD is 
concerned by the unclear governance regarding the management of such joint procurement, in particular 
the following points:  
o How would the prioritization of beneficiaries be made? How would this be articulated with the 
need to prioritise CRM for the defence needs as highlighted in the recently developed joint 
defence procurement initiative?  
o For which SRMs is this foreseen? Does this include secondary SRM? 
o How does it fit with some “consolidated purchasing strategies” developed by some large 
companies in the aerospace and defence sector?  
 
5. Measures fostering the development of SRM/CRM circularity in Aerospace & Defence are not sufficient. 
 
5.1. Recycling should be one of the main priorities of the EU strategy on critical and strategic raw materials 
given the triple benefit it provides: reducing the carbon footprint of primary raw materials production, 
alleviating dependence on single supplier countries and developing the circular economy in Europe. 
Regarding recycling, the CRM Act proposed by the Commission is particularly weak. On the one hand, 
the main measures on recycling provided for in the CRM Act are particularly abstract and vague since 
they are based on Article 25 that entrusts the Member States with the responsibility for adopting and 
implementing circularity plans. On the other hand, it is worth mentioning that actions on recycling will 
depend mainly on the good will of the Member States.  
 
5.2. In particular, ASD regrets the lack of incentives to foster the recycling of ‘scraps’ (i.e., “raw materials 
waste” generated in factories during the p roduction process) in the EU. This problem is even more 
critical as European manufacturers often have no choice but to send their scraps abroad to be 
recycled, therefore making the EU lose a non-negligible resource when production levels are already 
significantly low or absent. To support the development of a scraps recycling industry in the EU, the 
CRM Act could develop regulatory and financial support for EU companies o perating in the scraps 
industry to help them become more competitive. Complementarily, the new legislation could put in 
place financial incentives to encourage manufacturers to recycle their scraps in Europe. On this point, 
the EU should learn from the United States that provides economic support for scrap recycling through 
the implementation of a precious metals recovery program run by the U.S. Department of Defense, 
which offers companies the opportunity to recycle certain precious metal scraps generated during 
their production cycle and resell them at half the market price. 4 The regulation could also propose 
incentives to increase the recycling of titanium.  
 
5.3. The aerospace and defence sector is working on improving the recycling and circularity of CRMs, this 
in order to reduce the quantity of virgin raw materials used in our supply chain as well as the 
environmental and human impacts of CRM production (scraps, parts at end of life,  etc.). Various 
research, innovation and development (R&D) programs aimed a t reducing the use of CRMs in

aeronautical parts (e.g. use of titanium scraps in 3D printing to reduce the raw materials’ consumption) 
or improving the recycling of CRM from end-of-life products (e.g. development of processing methods 
to recycle certain alloys and CRM from end-of-life products that can currently not be reintroduced into

CRM Act.  
 
5.4. Finally, the defence-related exemption of the labelling requirement for permanent magnets relates to 
‘’products primarily designed for defence application” . ASD notes that such a categorization of 
products is unclear  as it does not clearly relate to existing categories of products.  Most products 
primarily designed for defence application, or Specially Designed for a Military Use, can be found on 
the EU Military List (updated annually). This list contains a wide range of items, some that would 
definitely be covered by the proposed Regulation, others that would require judgements to be made 
and a third set where the Regulation would not apply.  In addition to the Military List, there is a long 
list of Dual-Use Items in Regulation 2021/821  which have a military application as well as legitimate 
civil uses. Therefore, a clarification of which types of products are precisely concerned could bring 
legal certainty for the purpose of the application of the proposed exemption.

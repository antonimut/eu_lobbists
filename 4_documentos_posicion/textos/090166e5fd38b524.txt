European Critical Raw Materials Act 
 
 
 
Ref. Ares(2023)4187100 - 16/06/2023
VDA COMMENT ON EUROPEAN CRITICAL RAW MATERIALS ACT  2  
 
1 In General 
The German Association of the Automotive Industry (VDA) consolidates about 650 manufac-
turers and suppliers under one roof. The members develop and produce cars and trucks, 
software, trailers, superstructures, buses, parts and accessories as well as new mob ility of-
fers. We represent the interests of the automotive industry and stand for modern, future-ori-
ented multimodal mobility on the way to climate neutrality. The VDA represents the interests 
of its members in politics, the media, and social groups. We work for electric mobility, climate-
neutral drives, the implementation of climate targets, securing raw materials, digitization and 
networking as well as German engineering. We are committed to a competitive business and 
innovation location. Our industry ensures prosperity in Germany: More than 780,000 people 
are directly employed in the German automotive industry. 
E-mobility is the cornerstone of the automotive industry's decarbonization strategy. To 
achieve the ambitious goals of ramping up e-mobility, a critical amount of battery raw materi-
als, such as lithium, nickel sulphate, graphite, manganese and cobalt, but also rare earths, 
such as neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium for the permanent magnets in 
the traction motors are needed. In this context, the automotive industry is heavily dependent 
on imports from non-EU countries. To achieve better diversification and long-term stable sup-
ply of CRMs needed for the decarbonization of the transport sector, a reorientation of the Eu-
ropean raw materials strategy is indispensable. Other major world regions are actively on-
shoring respective value chains to support their industries. Europe is at risk of being left be-
hind. Therefore, the German Automotive Industry is fully committed to the goals and inten-
tions of the European CRMA. However, we also see several shortcomings in the current pro-
posal, which could be fixed during the following consultations. This paper highlights these is-
sues and provides concrete advice and suggestions for solutions. 
2 Important impulses to be strengthened 
The automotive industry needs significantly pure materials for its production (e. g. battery-
grade for battery production). Europe is highly dependent on the procurement of refined ma-
terials as it is on the procurement of raw materials. Therefore, the automotive industry fully 
concurs with the strategy to support the whole raw materials supply chain from mining via re-
fining up to recycling to reduce dependencies and building up a closed European battery pro-
duction chain. 
The CRMA acknowledges the challenge to join the efforts of member states to identify and 
invest in strategic projects throughout the entire raw materials supply chain. The proposed 
Raw Materials Board is an important impulse. In an international context and in the competi-
tion with other regions, however, it can only be a very first step. 
Joint purchasing of critical raw materials can make a difference in the procurement of strate-
gic raw materials. However, the Commission’s plan on how to organize joint purchasing must 
be specified in more detail. Different commercial forms and purity requirements of these met-
als must be taken into account in a differentiated manner 
In this context, the reduction of the previous list of 30 critical raw materials is highly important, 
because it does not guide any strategic investments. We welcome the approach to define a 
new list of strategic raw materials. Unfortunately, the reduction could only be achieved while 
counting rare earths and PGMs as group of materials. Without this aggregation the list of 
strategic raw materials still consists of 28 raw materials. This shows only a minor difference to 
the previous CRM-List and, hence, a further reduction of materials would be desirable.

Besides, in some cases it is not clear why this raw material is listed in the strategic list. For 
example copper, which is traded globally on transparent and liquid markets, finds itself on the 
list right next to gallium and germanium. This could lead to an incorrect focus and unwanted 
side effects. 
Furthermore, ambitious targets for the duration of the permission processes present a good 
first step in the right direction. The industry needs decisive and fast decisions to speed up the 
permission of projects along the entire value chain of strategic raw materials. However, it is 
also crucial to support projects already at an earlier application stage. Therefore, the pro-
moter should provide information to the board in such a manner that the national permission 
processes will accept these documents as well. The Commission’s plan of a “One-Stop-
Shop” must be specified in more detail. 
Finally, the CRMA should focus on strengthening the supply of strategic raw materials. In its 
current form, we see a major focus on regulation which would lead to more bureaucratic bur-
den for the companies of the green and digital transformation. This is a fatal political signal 
and does not lead to attracting investment in Europe. Positive incentives must be created to 
ensure the strategic raw materials, not prohibitions and regulation. 
3 Shortcomings to be fixed 
Raw Materials Fund to support ambitious self-sufficiency benchmarks is missing 
It is striking that no active support measures are defined that support the achievement of 
these goals, but that the member states are called upon to define national measures  and 
sanctions. This approach will result in fragmentation, hidden "local-content" requirements and 
governmental stockpiling, which puts further pressure on the already tense commodity mar-
kets. Furthermore, the CRMA should support the supply of strategic raw materials with addi-
tional funding or in form of tax credits for mining and processing activities. To support the am-
bitious self-sufficiency benchmarks, the CRMA should be equipped with funding measures. 
Instead, it refers to already existing methods of funding i.e., the EU Innovation Fund and the 
Global Gateway. However, these funds are not appropriate to support raw material projects. 
This leaves the industry with uncertainties that could have been avoided with the definition of 
a European Raw Materials Fund.  
Self-sufficiency benchmarks for strategic raw materials are counterproductive 
The "one-size-fits-all" approach of self-sufficiency for strategic commodities is well intended 
but counterproductive, because different market conditions apply to each strategic raw mate-
rial. Besides, the diversification goal is too ambitious for many strategic commodities since 
individual countries' market shares of more than 80% are common. The recycling targets are 
not feasible, at least for technologies that are in the ramp-up phase (such as electric cars and 
wind power plants). For battery raw materials, the benchmarks are even higher than the al-
ready ambitious targets in the recently adopted battery regulation! And failing to achieve the 
benchmarks should not lead to even stricter regulations in consequence. In general, and also 
due to the lack of recyclable materials, recycling can only supplement the availability of raw 
materials, but not be the solution. 
 
More transparency should not lead to a disclosure of company secrets 
The creation of a dashboard to share information on potential resources and production ca-
pacities in the EU will create more transparency, but publishing the results of stress tests 
goes far beyond the target. Public recommendations to develop strategic commodity reserves

may also result in European vulnerabilities becoming public, which in turn leads to a weaken-
ing of the European negotiation position.  
The CRMA also requires companies to ensure that new raw material projects are carried out 
in a sustainable manner and more specifically to consider international instruments that cover 
all aspects of sustainability highlighted in the EU Principles for Sustainable Raw Materials . 
These, in turn, contain a number of requirements that may overburden companies. A strong 
focus on sustainability and ESG requirements is certainly important but should always be 
considered within the framework of international requirements and must not lead to a compet-
itive disadvantage for Europe as an industrial location. In any case, stress tests should not be 
published at either country or company level. 
4 Adjustment recommendations for the CRMA  
Establishing a European Agency for Strategic Raw Materials Projects and a European 
Raw Materials Fund 
A European Raw Materials Board as proposed by the Commission is not sufficient. Instead, 
we call for an independent European Agency for Strategic Raw Materials Projects, which di-
rectly invests in strategic raw materials projects and thus strengthens the supply of strategic 
raw materials. Mining and processing projects are highly capital intensive. To promote and 
support projects at an early stage, the automotive industry recommends the establishment of 
a European Agency for Strategic Raw Materials Projects, which supports companies to dis-
cover and develop mining and refinery projects for strategic raw materials. It should also in-
vest directly in the projects in an early stage with venture capital which comes from a Euro-
pean Raw Materials Fund. The European Commission and European Parliament should take 
a supervisory role in this agency. Especially in difficult economic times, a monetary commit-
ment by the European Union can help to encourage companies to invest in certain areas.  
The EU and the member states should support this projects through risk-minimizing 
measures. 
Stockpiling of strategic raw materials is a strategic decision of companies and does 
not need a central approach or legislative rules  
Stockpiling seems to be a good way to gain more resilient supply chains and to be prepared 
for disruptions and “black swans” in the supply chains of raw materials, however it needs to 
be done strategically. Companies know best how much raw materials they will need in the fu-
ture and how much stockpiling they can afford. The CRMA should support companies in 
building these stockpiles for example with tax credits and leave the decision on how many 
raw materials they want to stockpile to the companies. If for example national stockpiles of 
raw materials set a target to be prepared to supply the industry with 60 days worth of raw ma-
terials, the yearly demand for the strategic raw materials will increase by 16% . This will put 
more pressure on the supply and will lead to higher costs in the procurement of strategic raw 
materials.  
Taking-out medium-sized companies in the definition of large companies 
The CRMA defines measures and requirements for large companies that consume a larger 
amount of strategic raw materials. However, the current definition with the threshold of 500 
employees and a global net income of 150 million euros would also affect medium-sized 
companies. Therefore, the definition of "large company" should be changed by taking out the 
mid-caps as defined by the industry. Therefore, the definition should be as follows: ‘large 
company’ means any company that has more than 1500 employees on average and has a 
net worldwide turnover of more than EUR 2 billion in the last financial year for which annual 
financial statements have been prepared.

Avoidance of a patchwork quilt regarding support measures and sanctions 
The CRMA requests the member states’ need to implement measures and sanctions within 
their national laws to meet the mentioned benchmarks. To avoid a patchwork of measures 
within the European Union and competition at the wrong place, it should provide possible 
measures in an annex to harmonize these measures and sanctions within the European Un-
ion. Without a harmonization, wealthier member states will profit more of the CRMA then oth-
ers. This could lead to conflicts within the Union in times when it should be staying stronger 
together than ever.  
Comprehensible stress tests increase the EU's strategic vulnerability, and the results 
should not be available to the public via the dashboard 
Member States are requested to perform stress tests to provide the information to the Com-
mission and to publish the results in the proposed dashboard. The publication of the results 
means that any vulnerabilities in the raw materials supply chains will be uncovered, too. This 
is a serious threat to Europe as a production location. Therefore, the VDA rejects stress tests 
as proposed by the European Commission. All information needed to be capable of acting 
and of securing strategic raw materials are already available in public scientific studies .  
Furthermore, the CRMA proposes that large companies, which are consuming large amounts 
of strategic raw materials have to perform stress test as well. The results should be then dis-
cussed on c-level. Of course, large companies are already performing these tests and taking 
the results into consideration for strategic corporate decisions. Since stress tests are part of 
companies’ daily business, the CRMA does not need to address this specifically in the regula-
tion. Besides, it is crucial that these information are valued as company secrets and there-
fore, should stay in the company.  
New requirements for motor vehicles should be incorporated in one single regulation 
The CRMA is currently not sufficiently coordinated with other legislation and regulations. For 
instance, the Commission urgently needs to address all circularity requirements in the rele-
vant eco-design regulations. The key regulation to improve the circularity in the automotive 
industry (collecting, recycling, recycled content quota) is the End-of-Life-Vehicle Directive, 
which is currently under revision. To ensure a proper implementation in the automotive indus-
try new requirements should be incorporated in one single regulation. The increasing number 
of delegated or implementing acts as proposed by the Commission is leading to further un-
certainties and poor results. Therefore, Chapter 5 should either be removed or be clearly 
linked to the existing eco-design regulations. 
Requirements of circularity should address the whole supply chain 
Vehicles are very different from other consumer goods in terms of their complexity. A vehicle 
consists of around 7,000 components and many times more sub-components. All economic 
operators in the value chain are involved in implementing the requirements of circularity. Reg-
ulation should take this into account by defining ‘places on the market’ more precisely. To 
avoid complexity and inconsistency with eco-design regulations, chapter 5 should either be 
removed or be clearly linked to the existing eco-design regulations as mentioned above. 
Circularity of Rare Earth should focus traction motors  
Measures concerning the recyclability and recycled content of permanent magnets should fol-
low a hotspot analysis. Modern vehicles contain up to 100 small sized motors e.g., for wind-
screen wiper, pumps, electric windows, seat adjustments. Permanent magnets are also used 
in loudspeakers or to fasten interior panels. But since 90% of the total weight of permanent 
magnets in electric vehicles is used in the traction motors. Specific requirements to improve

the circularity of permanent magnets in vehicles should therefore concentrate only on the 
traction motors. 
Lead time should take the development processes in the automotive industry into ac-
count 
The implementation of new requirements concerning permanent magnets can only be applied 
for new type approved vehicles and at the earliest seven years after entry into force, due to 
the usual duration of a car development process. 
For effective permanent magnet recycling and circularity, the European Union needs to 
set up its own magnet recycling chain 
The EU has no fully-developed magnet recycling chain. In addition, there is currently very few 
permanent magnet manufacturing in the EU. Further to providing necessary product infor-
mation to the recyclers and increasing the amount of recycled materials, the EU needs to es-
tablish its own magnet recycling chain including recovering and processing of magnet materi-
als as a precondition to magnet recycling at a larger scale. Since permanent magnets are pri-
marily produced outside the EU, the EU should also explore the idea of providing incentives 
to permanent magnet manufacturing in the EU. Otherwise the recycled and processed mate-
rials might be exported and will be used for magnet manufacturing abroad.

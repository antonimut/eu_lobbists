Critical Raw Materials Act – Feedback (June 2023) 1 
 
 
Our feedback: 
The EU Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) 
June 2023 
 
Today, the EU sources more than 95% of its rare earth elements (REE) from China. REE s are, among 
other, important for the production of permanent magnets, which are crucial components in wind 
turbines, EV motors, and other technologies needed to reach the EU climate targets. In its 2023 study 
on Critical Raw Materials , the European Commission ranks magnet REEs a s the critical raw material 
(CRM) with the highest supply risk  (figure 9), also placing magnet REEs on its list of ‘strategic’ CRMs  
(CRMA Annex I).  
 
Rare Earths Norway holds an exploration permit covering most of the Fen REE deposit, which is situated 
in Nome municipality in Telemark in Southern Norway.  Investigations by the Geological Advisor to the 
County Council and the Geological Survey of Norway, supported by our ongoing exploration drilling 
programme, indicates that Fen is potentially the largest REE deposit in continental Europe . The 
deposit contains, among other, substantial amounts of neodymium (Nd) and praseodymium (Pr), which 
are REEs used in permanent magnets.  
 
Rare Earths Norway is supported by the European Raw Materials Alliance (ERMA) and EIT Raw 
Materials. Our R&D activities include ongoing cooperation with partners like Montanuniversität Leoben 
in Austria to develop new technologies for efficient, safe , and low-emission underground mining – an 
‘invisible mine’ concept ensuring the lowest possible environmental footprint and land use impact.  
 
One of the key success criteria and competitive advantages of the Fen deposit is its location adjacent 
to Herøya Industrial Park, one of the largest industry clusters in Norway comprising an international 
deep-water port, located only 35 kilometres from the mine. This presents a unique opportunity for a 
complete and compact mine-to-magnet value chain in Europe, with the Fen deposit as the source.  
 
Rare Earths Norway endorses the EU Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), and we look forward to 
contributing to the ambition  of extracting at least 10% of annual European consumption of 
strategic raw materials by 2030. In the ongoing co-legislative process, Rare Earths Norway encourage 
policymakers to consider the recommendations presented below.  
 
Our recommendations  
 
The overall ambition  
 
Rare Earths Norway fully support s the inclusion of magnet REEs on the list of strategic raw 
materials (Annex I), and the overall ambition of the European Commission as set out in Article 1. 
However, to translate into real impact on the ground, we would encourage policymakers to consider a 
“per-material”-target, rather than a cumulated target for all raw materials on the list.

The Joint Research Centre (JRC) in their foresight study (2023) underlines that magnet REEs represent 
one of the highest supply risks. A “per-material”-target could therefore mitigate the risk that sudden and 
unforeseen enhanced supply of one of the other strategic raw materials on the list – although welcomed 
– would result in lowered ambition for magnet REEs, or any of the other CRMs on the list.  
 
Inclusion of third country projects 
 
Despite the Regulation being marked as EEA -relevant, and Norway is a member of the European 
Economic Area (EEA), we welcome the inclusion of third country projects in the proposal, as supply 
from like -minded partners will be crucial to achieve the 2030 targets , as  set out by the European 
Commission.  
 
We therefore encourage policymakers to maintain the language in Article 5  paragraph 1(e) and 
Article 6 paragraph 5. We would also urge policymakers to seek for more clarity with regards to how 
the objectives of the CRMA will translate into industrial partnerships with third countries in this regard.  
 
Faster permitting processes 
 
As highlighted in the European Commissi on’s accompanying impact assessment , “Permitting delays 
can put the whole operations of a company on pause for years depending on the waiting time. In extreme 
cases, the company is dissolved before the permit issuance due to its inability to sustain the costs of 
waiting.”  
 
The Commission has put forward an ambitious framework for speeding up permitting processes (Articles 
8-11), and we are especially positive to the establishment of ‘one stop shop ’. However, concern 
remains with the feasibility of implementing this in a harmonised manner at the national level. We would 
therefore encourage the European Commission to provide guidance for Member States  on the 
implementation of the time limits as set out in Article 10 and Article 11.  
 
Further, the national priority status  (Article 9) should be clarified and include a full picture of  the 
bureaucratic hurdles met by project developers  – for example by referring to the definition of ‘permit 
granting process’ as set out in Article 2 paragraph 19 directly in Article 9 paragraph 2. 
 
Access to finance  
 
Due to lengthy permitting processes, it normally takes decades for investors in the mineral industry to 
see returns. The recognition of ‘strategic projects’ sends an important investment signal to financial 
institutions, which is of crucial importance to foster sustainable mining in Europe.  
 
In addition, European projects must compete in a challenging world market dominated by China’s 
heavily subsidized mineral industry . Other countries are increasingly introducing similar measures to 
support its national industries (e.g., the U.S. and Japan). The CRMA should contribute to levelling the 
global playing field for European companies by incentivising measures which can help safeguard the 
European mining industry against dumping, price manipulation and unfair subsidisation  from 
other regions of the world.  
 
For the early stage (‘feasibility phase’), Rare Earths Norway is facing barriers for access to de -risk 
capital. Significant investment is needed to finalise preparations including exploration, drilling and R&D 
activities before the project has reached the level of full investment decision for which it is easier to 
attract investors due to the lower risk.  
 
The world-class extraction technologies being developed by Rare Earths Norway will inevitably be more 
expensive than the extraction methods currently used in countries like China . In addition, European

companies must take huge financial risks by simultaneously investing in exploration programmes and 
developing R&D capacities in processing technologies, while facing stricter European environmental 
and regulatory requirements which may result in additional administrative and financial burdens. Public

Rare Earths Norway further recommends that the definition of  ‘exploration’ (as set out in Article 2

R&D funding could be made available as risk reduction of the early-stage investments.  
 
About Rare Earths Norway   
 
The company was established in 2016 with the purpose of developing the Fen Carbonatite Complex in 
the county of Telemark, Norway. Rare Earths Norway's exploration permit covers approximately 90% of 
the REE-bearing iron-dolomite carbonatite (FDC) at Fen. Rare Earths Norway's vision is to become an 
important and significant supplier of mixed rare earths concentrate in to a value chain for permanent 
magnets in Europe, the source and basis of raw materials enabling the green industrial transition, 
climate change mitigation, and fuelling the digital revolution.  
 
Rare Earths Norway has two owners: Hustadlitt AS and Cappelen Group AS. Hustadlitt AS is a holding 
company with majority ownership in R are Earths Norway  and in Norsk Mineral AS  (www.nomin.no). 
Norsk Mineral AS owns Brønnøy Kalk AS (www.bronnoykalk.no) and is 50% partner in The Quartz Corp 
AS ( www.thequartzcorp.com). Hustadlitt  AS holds a portfolio of world leading mineral companies 
developed through long term partnerships with European large industries from France and Switzerland. 
As Hustadlitt has 75 years of successful mineral history, Cappelen Group (www.cho.no) is a holding 
company located in Ulefoss, close to the Fen deposit. The history Cappelen Group and their involvement 
in local mining and melting industry dates back to 1657.

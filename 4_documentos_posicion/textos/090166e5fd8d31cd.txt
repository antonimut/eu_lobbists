Towards a more resilient and competitive EU: harnessing the full potential 
of strategic net-zero technologies 
Proposal for a Regulation for strengthening Europe’s net-zero technology products 
manufacturing ecosystem (Net Zero Industry Act) 
 
In a geopolitical context marked by the return of industrial policy to international regulatory 
agendas, Repsol welcomes the proposed Net -Zero Industry Act, presented by the European 
Commission on 16 March 2023, within the framework of the European Green Industrial Plan. The 
Net-Zero Industry Act will be instrumental in laying the foundations for the region's future 
prosperity and defining its importance in the global economy.  
The aim of this Act is to scale up technologies that can contribute to a decarbonised and resilient 
energy system, while maintaining competitiveness and security of supply. Therefore , this 
approach should favour technological inclusivity, understood as the extension of the scope of net-
zero strategic technologies to those technologies with a proven potential to achieve the dual 
objective of reducing both emissions and the EU's external energy dependence. 
This approach has several advantages: 
• It enables market actors to find the most efficient and cost -effective solutions to meet 
climate targets.  
• It encourages innovation and the development of new and more sustainable 
technologies.  
• It promotes competition and industrial competitiveness.  
To achieve this, European institutions still have room for work and improvement in which Repsol, 
as a global multi -energy company of European origin, wishes to collaborate by contributing its 
experience and vision. These are our recommendations: 
• Extending the scope of strategic net -zero technologies. Net-zero renewable energy 
technologies as defined in the Renewable Energy Directive (RED), sustainable 
alternative fuels, renewable fuels of non -biological origin (RFNBOs) as well as CO2 
capture and utilisation (CCU) should be considered as net -zero strategic technologies . 
This would ensure consistency with the other measures of the FF55 and RePowerEU 
package and the achievement of the required emission reduction targets by 2030.  
 
• Exploiting the potential of sustainable alternative fuels. The use of sustainable 
alternative fuels should not only be limited to the maritime transport and aviation sectors. 
Their use would need to be extended to road transport in order to make the 
decarbonisation of road transport agile, resilient, safe and socially just. 
 
• Support for the 2030 CO2 injection target and measures to facilitate its 
achievement. The recognition of carbon capture and storage (CCS) as a net -zero 
strategic technology and the establishment of an injection target of 50 million tonnes in 
the EU by 2030 is an important milestone. However, it is essential to create a more 
homogeneous and favourable environment for its development in all Member States in 
order to reach the  proposed target. Measures focused on reducing investment risk in

1. Definition and scope of strategic net-zero technologies 
The proposed Net-Zero Industry Act establishes which technologies are considered net-zero and 
additionally recognises some of them as strategic. Strategic projects based on the latter will be 
able to benefit from a number of additional advantages such as: increased support, faster 
permitting processes, top priority status, the possibility of public interest overriding environmental 
impact assessments and direct approval of the project in case of no administrative response 
within the set deadlines, as well as the contribution to the EU's 40% annual manufacturing target 
(Art. 1(2)a). 
However, taking into account the selection criteria defined in Article 10 , the exclusion from the 
category of strategic net-zero technologies of a number of technologies rel evant for 
decarbonisation such as net-zero renewable energy technologies1 , sustainable alternative fuels 
and renewable fuels of non-biological origin (RFNBOs) as well as CCU is questionable.  
In line with the Fit-for-55 package and the RePowerEU communication, the EU also needs these 
technologies - and should give them the same priority - to achieve an efficient and rapid 
decarbonisation of transport and other sectors of the economy . This would at  the same time 
strengthen the EU's security of supply and its leadership in low- and zero-carbon technologies. 
Therefore, net-zero renewable energy technologies, renewable fuels2 to the full extent, including 
sustainable alternative fuels and RFNBOs , as well as CCU should be included in the Annex of 
net-zero strategic technologies. This would ensure their deployment and support the investments 
required for their scale-up and production in the 2030 horizon set out in this Law. 
1.1. Sustainable alternative fuels 
We regret that in the current draft legislation the use of sustainable alternative fuels is strictly 
limited to the maritime and aviation sectors. This approach neglects the potential of sustainable 
road transport fuels as a lever to accelerate the energy transition and make the decarbonisation 
of road transport agile, resilient, safe and socially just. 
The use of sustainable alternative fuels for transport has several advantages. These include:

• Ensure the reduction of CO2 emissions and boost the circular economy, in line with the

• They bring forward CO2 reductions as they are already available and compatible with the

• They are a lever to boost renewable hydrogen consumption in Europe without waiting for

In addition, the technologies for manufacturing sustainable aviation and maritime fuels are in 
many cases the same as those for manufacturing sustainable road fuels, so it does not make 
sense to differentiate by use.  
Indeed, the exclusion of these fuels from road transport would threaten the viability of the 
investments that will be necessary fo r the development of sustainable aviation and maritime 
transport fuels, as all these products are often produced simultaneously. 
Consistent with the RED and the rest of the Fit for 55 package, it is necessary that the Net-Zero 
Industry Act recognises sustainable alternative fuels for the decarbonisation of the entire transport 
sector as a net zero strategic technology. In this way, the definition of sustainable alternative fuels 
should be replaced by the definition of renewable fuels in the RED DirectiveIII2 . 
1.2. Carbon Capture and Use (CCU) and Renewable Fuels from Non -Biological Origin 
(RFNBOs) Technologies 
Carbon Capture and Use (CCU) technology increases the resilience of the EU economy and its 
industrial systems by reusing captured carbon and p roviding an alternative carbon feedstock to 
produce fuels, chemicals and materials and replace fossil equivalents. In doing so, it contributes 
to the EU's independence from imports of fossil resources, provides significant emission 
reductions (including ca rbon removals in some value chains and according to certain carbon 
sources and pathways), increases circularity in manufacturing systems and contributes to 
maintaining the EU's leadership in clean technologies. 
CCU technology, as well as non-biological renewable fuel manufacturing technology, are also not 
recognised as strategic in the Commission's proposal . However, these technologies are critical 
to achieving the EU's climate and energy ambitions and to meeting the EU's hydrogen targets, as 
recognised in the 6th Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 
(IPCC). They are also promoted in a number of recent EU legislative efforts: 
• In the RED III, FuelEU Maritime and ReFuelEU Aviation proposals, by defining mandatory 
targets for RFNBOs to reduce emissions from sectors that are difficult to reduce.

• In the revision of the ETS Directive adopted in December 2022, by allowing permanent

On the maturity of CCU, it is already recognised by the EU framework through the funding 
provided for this type of projects by the Innovation Fund, recognising pre -commercial maturity, 
first of a kind (FOK). A number of projects based on this technology are expected to start operating 
in the next 2-3 years in the European Union. In any case, CCU technologies with a lower level of 
technological readiness could also benefit from the Net-Zero Industry Act, e.g. through regulatory 
sandboxes. In this respect, it could be useful to change the proposed national approach so that 
sandboxes are directly supervised by the Commission, thus avoiding fragmentation between 
Member States.  
2. Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) technologies 
With regard to other technologies mentioned in the draft Net-Zero Industry Act, the incorporation 
of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technology as a "net-zero strategic technology" is positive. 
With the Commission's proposal, a more favourable investment climate could be created, even 
among Member States that were not so strongly committed to this technology in their National 
Energy and Climate Plans.   
We welcome the target for EU CO2 injection capacity to reach 50 million tonnes by 2030, 
imposing an obligation on Member States to determine where storage sites can be authorised on 
their territory. The consequence of this obligation is that authorised oil and gas producers will also 
be subject to an annual injection contribution based on their share of EU oil and gas production 
between 2020-2023. The obligation would represent an unprecedented investment for oil and gas 
operators and has not taken into account the need to establish value chains from emitters to 
storage operators.  
It will be a challenge for the industry to reach this capacity target within this timeframe. Therefore, 
the CO2 injection capacity target should be accompanied by measures to reduce risk and support 
the financing of such projects. This ambition needs to be supported by policies that allow access 
to exploration and storage permits and faster financing for the whole value chain. For CO2 to be 
efficiently captured, transported, processed  and permanently stored, all entities along the CCS 
value chains need to have viable and sustainable business cases with signed agreements that 
support investments and define their locations, capacities and timelines.  
While the EU has long subsidised and incentivised the energy transition, the reality is that we 
have done so in an uncoordinated and inefficient way. The EU now has the opportunity to use the 
Green Deal Industrial Plan to take a holistic approach that includes the entire European value 
chain, from raw materials to technology, development, innovation and investment. In addition, 
should avoid the development of a two -speed European competitiveness plan. Otherwise, 
differences between Member States could jeopardise the cohesion of the internal ma rket. A

To ensure a successful energy transition, it is essential to guarantee the competitiveness of

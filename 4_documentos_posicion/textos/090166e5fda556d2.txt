Bioenergy Europe’s Position on the Net-Zero Industry Act 
 
Bioenergy Europe supports the objective of the Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) which seeks to enhance 
the competitiveness of European net-zero industries which will be vital to achieving the EU’s ambitious 
climate and energy goals. Net -zero solutions  are the only  enabler to climate neutrality, and th eir 
deployment should  be even further accelerated , by simplifying and fast -tracking permitting 
procedures and supporting the upscaling-up of sustainable technologies. 
 
SIGNIFICANCE OF THE STRATEGIC ANNEX  
The proposed legislation defines “net-zero technologies” all renewable energy technologies as defined 
by the Renewable Energy Directive, which also includes bioenergy. However, in Section II, the proposal 
identifies “ net-zero strategic projects ” which would be eligible for even more favourable treatment 
based on Annex  I which identifies  a list of  “strategic net -zero technologies ”. These benefits inclu de 
notably faster permitting processes for the infrastructure and facilities that will need to be built for 
large-scale emissions reductions, as well as additional access to funding. Currently, the annex lists 8 
different solutions:  
1. Solar photovoltaic and solar thermal technologies  
2. Onshore wind and offshore renewable technologies  
3. Battery/storage technologies  
4. Heat pumps and geothermal energy technologies  
5. Electrolysers and fuel cells  
6. Sustainable biogas/biomethane technologies  
7. Carbon Capture and storage (CCS) technologies and  
8. Grid technologies.  
To ensure that all renewable solutions are properly acknowledged as strategic sectors, solid bioenergy 
technologies should be added to point 6. On top of this to guarantee that the EU achieves negative 
emissions which will be pivotal to reach carbon neutrality by 2050 bioenergy with carbon capture and 
storage (BECCS) and other negative emissions technologies should be explicitly added to point 7.  
 
BIOENERGY 
Bioenergy technologies have emerged as a crucial component of the European clean energy industry, 
as they  enable the use of a renewable fuel that is , storable, low-carbon, reduces greenhouse gas 
emissions, increase energy security, and support s rural development. Additionally, bioenergy is a 
stable energy source which can easily work together with other renewable solutions and ensure a 
stable supply while shielding the grid from peak loads or providing an alternative when other solutions 
are not available.

The EU’s bioenergy fuel supply is an important fundament to our energy security with 95% of the solid 
biomass used in Europe being locally sourced and the remainder overwhelmingly sourced from 
trusted allies . It is vital that it is produced sustainably, which is ensured by the Renewable Energy 
Directive sustainability criteria. Beyond these important protections the supply of sustainable 
bioenergy should not be curtai led – whether internationally or domestically sourced. The prospects 
for bioenergy technologies in the European clean energy industry are promising  and this is reflected 
in all international scenarios . According to the European Commission's long -term strategy for a 
climate-neutral economy, bioenergy is expected to play a significant role in the EU's transition to a 
low-carbon economy, providing up to 27% of the EU's total energy demand by 2050.  
If solid bioenergy technologies are not considered as  strategic net-zero technology in the Net -Zero 
Industrial Act, they may not receive sufficient support and could push EU-based bioenergy technology 
providers to outsource their production to  non-EU territory. Given the existing 50.000+ businesses 
and manufacturers situated in the EU and the 900.000+ jobs across the value chain 1, this would then 
weaken the EU economy and decrease its industrial competitiveness. The EU is a leader in the 
bioenergy sector: currently, more than 70% of bioenergy’s equipment manufacturers are based in 
Europe, and only 7% are based in China, making bioenergy a truly European industry 2. 
The EU should not wait until this competitive position is eroded before taking action but should rather 
provide the necessary support to make sure this strategic position is maintained.  
 
BIOENERGY WITH CARBON CAPTURE AND STORAGE (BECCS)  
In addition, the absence of bioenergy with carbon capture and storage  (BECCS) from the list of 
strategic net-zero technologies also shows a clear missed opportunity from the European Union. This 
heavy focus on CCS technologies overshadows the negative emission technologies and carbon dioxide 
removal technologies (including BECCS) that are necessary for achieving a European net-zero industry.  
The Commission’s Proposal lacks vision to support an activity recognised by the IPCC as necessary to 
reach the Paris Agreement’s targets. Further still focusing only on CCS tilts the benefits towards fossil 
based uses  and ignores the ability of CCS combined with bioenergy to remove carbon from the 
atmosphere. By enforcing the EU market for BECCS, many of these innovative projects would be 
quickly realised and would contribute to achieving negati ve emissions on a large scale. One prime 
example of this is the EU-funded project for BECCS operated by Stockholm Exergi, the project aims to 
remove 800.000 tonnes of carbon dioxide annually.

BECCS will play a pivotal role in achieving carbon neutrality as the International Energy Agency (IEA) 3 
clearly identifies bioenergy as the only carbon negative solution for industry and a significant 
contributor.  
 
TRL LEVELS 
Upscaling support to BECCS is essential, as it would enable Europe to spearhead BECCS technologies 
at the global level, and further enhance bioenergy's leadership role among other industrial players. In 
order to enable this change, the Technological Readiness Level (TRL) requirement should be slightly 
altered and lowered from 8 to 7. In the current proposal, the legislation requires TRL8 “system complete 
and qualified ” but by relaxing this requirement to TRL7 “system prototype demonstration in 
operational environment” this would ensure that the attention is still focused on projects that are at 
commercial readiness or very close to it and ensure that these innovative technologies become widely 
adopted quicker.  
 
CONCLUSION  
The increasing production of bioenergy in the EU in recent years, together with a sustainability 
framework provided with the Renewable Energy Directive, indicates that the industry is on the right 
track on the decarbonisation path, and with continued investment and innovation  in solid bioenergy 
technologies, bioenergy has a bright future in Europe's clean energy mix.

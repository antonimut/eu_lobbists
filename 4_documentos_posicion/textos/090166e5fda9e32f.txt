Following the European Commission proposal of a Net -Zero Industry Act  (NZIA), ERA welcomes this 
legislative initiative with the aim to support the production, deployment, and supply of net -zero 
technologies that will enable EU’s decarbonisation ambitions. The NZIA has a crucial role enabling the 
European industrial decarbonisation while reaching the goals of the Green Deal.  
 
ERA believes that to be fully successful and be able to meet climate objectives, no technology should be 
left behind, such as sustainable aviation fuels (SAFs), synthetic fuels, battery electric  aircraft 
technology and hydrogen that are currently not considered strategic net-zero technologies. 
 
Decarbonising aviation as a global challenge:  
We know that decarbonising aviation is going to rely on four main pillars, as defined by the Destination 
2050 roadmap (commissioned to NLR/SEO by A4E, ACI Europe, ASD, CANSO and ERA) : 
- Technology improvements, including electric and hydrogen aircraft 
- SAFs (like biofuels, synthetic fuels, RNFBOs) 
- Smart economic measures as carbon pricing 
- Improvements in ATM and operations 
 
The first two pillars are projected to account for over 70 per cent of the total CO2 reduction, so it is vital 
that European policy supports them appropriately and that it is aligned with the Fit for 55 and ReFuelEU 
policies which define the percentage of SAF that European airlines need to uplift in the coming years.  
 
In the US, we are already seeing subs tantial incentives supporting the production of SAF and green 
hydrogen through tax credits for producers and distributors like ‘Clean Fuel Production Credit ’ (CFPC) 
and ‘Sustainable Aviation Fuel and Low -Emission Aviation Technology Grant Program ’. Other major 
foreign economies are also investing in industrial energy policies to turn them into strategic action plans: 
Japan has prepared the Green Transformation programme and India has the Production Linked Incentive 
cheme. Our concern is that Europe may be left behind in terms of production capacity rollout and 
that companies invest in clean technologies abroad so the European Union will have to import 
SAF or rely on technology, such as hydrogen or battery electric propulsion, developed outside 
the EU . 
 
Equally it is critical the EU does not miss the opportunity to drive innovation in new and pioneering 
aeronautical technologies, including hydrogen and battery electric aircraft, that are likely to support the 
industry efforts to decarbonise and offer alternatives to the use of SAF in the long term. Given the initial 
use of hydrogen and battery electric aircraft on regional and short essential routes we also believe that 
there is a strong case for recognising these technologies as being in the overriding public interest as 
defined in the NZIA. 
 
ERA welcomes the decision to include SAF and RFNBOs as net -zero technologies enabling a 
greater and cheaper production. However, NZIA’s support for SAF is limited as it is not considered 
strategic. It is therefore important to classify enabling technologies for SAF as ‘strategic’ to 
encourage investments and fundings for SAF production . As an exa mple, one of the EU’s major 
funding programmes, the Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework (TCTF), already proposes specific 
benefits for ‘strategic’ net-zero sectors, and the forthcoming European Sovereignty Fund may copy this 
categorisation.

Destination 2050’s paper, ‘The Price of Net Zero’, estimates that at least €820bn of additional investments 
will be needed over the next 27 years to achieve aviation’s decarbonisation. 
 
Therefore, without investments, no SAF production technology will be able to meet global needs. 
Nine ‘pathways’ have already been certified by ASTM, the globally recognised certifying authority for 
aviation fue l. But the vast majority of currently available SAF is produced using the so -called HEFA 
pathway which is based on used cooking oils and fats. A great deal of research and development is under 
way into new SAF production pathways and production using other  certified pathways is expected to 
scale up over the coming years.  
 
New and more efficient pathways are expected to be certified. It is vitally important that the other 
certified pathways can be developed into commercial -scale production, and that funding  can be 
made available for new pathways. We believe that this merits the designation of SAF as ‘strategic’ 
in the NZIA.  
 
Meanwhile, synthetic fuels will be made by combining hydrogen  (obtained from electrolysers 
powered with green electricity) and carbon (captured from the atmosphere). The NZIA includes 
electrolysers and fuel cells as net zero technologies, but doesn’t include hydrogen liquefaction, which 
could reasonably be defined as an innovative net-zero technology.  
 
Similarly, carbon capture will be essential to obtain the carbon needed to make synthetic fuels – but the 
NZIA as it stands does not include Carbon Capture and Use (CCU) as a strategic net-zero 
technology. 
 
For alternative aviation fuels (whether bio-based SAF, synthetic fuel, or hydrogen) to be developed and 
scaled as quickly as possible, it is important that the EU policies are aligned, and that NZIA recognises 
the strategic nature of these technologies.

Key suggestions:  
- The NZIA already references the EU’s RED III directive which defines allowable feedstocks for 
sustainable fuels, but given the urgency of ramping up SAF production in Europe, the NZIA 
needs to classify SAF and liquid hydrogen as ‘strategic net-zero’ technologies.  
- ReFuelEU Aviation defines a sub-mandate for synthetic fuels (RFNBOs), and these fuels will 
require CCU technology. NZIA should prioritise CCU at the same level as SAF, in order to 
enable synthetic fuel production to scale up quickly. 
- New aviation propulsion technologies will certainly emerge from the laboratory in the coming 
years. Rather than prescribing a fixed list of strategic net-zero technologies, NZIA should 
incorporate a revision clause allowing it to reflect new technological developments in a timely 
way. 
- The NZIA should also label the SAF market developments as Important Projects of Common 
European Interest (IPCEI), whereas IPCEIs in the field of hydrogen  and battery electric should 
continue and be further facilitated. 
 
 
Co-operation among the institutions and the industry:  
These measures will strengthen the co-ordination between Member States in research and development.   
 
ERA encourages the Net Zero Platform to work closely with RLCF and AZEA industrial alliances 
that can state the financial needs and provide best practices for developing such projects and 
their supply chain s to improve the dialogue between the platform and the industry. Significant 
investments and further collaboration between public and private sectors a re critical to making these 
technologies economically viable.

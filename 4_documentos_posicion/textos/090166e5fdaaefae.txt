 
 
SolarPower Europe Position Paper on  
The Net Zero Industry Act
SolarPower Europe welcomes the proposal for a regulation on the Net -Zero Industry Act (NZIA), as part 
of the Green Deal Industry Plan, to improve the regulatory environment for solar PV manufacturing in 
Europe.  
 It sets the ambition of reaching at least 30GW manufacturing capacity by 2030  across the solar 
PV supply chain, which is in line with the 30GW of committed investment for PV manufacturing 
by 2025 set in the context of the European Solar Industry Alliance.  
 It creates the Net-Zero Strategic Project c ategory as a mechanism for projects to benefit from 
accelerated permitting procedures and access to financing.  
SolarPower Europe, however, believes several aspects of the proposal need to be substantially 
clarified and improved.  
 
Solar Power Europe’s key recommendations for & comments on the Net Zero Industry 
Act: 
 Financing frameworks are inadequate to reach the 2030 ambition for solar PV supply 
chains in Europe: 
 
- The EU should further revise the State Aid rules under the Temporary Crisis 
and Transition Framework if it is to attract investment in solar PV supply 
chains in Europe at scale.  
- The EU must establish a European financial instrument dedicated to Net Zero 
technologies with high dependency on one source of supply, like solar PV, 
under the Net Zero Europe Platform (Art 15). 
 
 Non-price criteria in public procurement and auctions are welcome on the condition 
that they are crystal clear, comprehensive, harmonized and applied only in 
technology specific auctions (Chapter IV).  
 
- Further development & clarification of non-price criteria should be set out 
within a dedicated Delegated Act. 
 
 European Net Zero Industry Academies should specifically include the role of industry 
stakeholders and provide funding for existing training and certification content.

1. Financing frameworks are inadequate to reach the 2030 ambition for solar PV supply chains in 
Europe 
SolarPower Europe estimates the financing gap for reaching 30GW across the solar PV supply chain by 
2030 to be around EUR 30 -60bn1, capex and opex combined . The European Commission’s own Staff 
Working Document (SWD) refers to 7-8bn EUR – capex only referring to polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells, 
modules and recycling – necessary for reaching 24GW by 2030.  
That SWD also concludes that “while funding possibilities have recently increased, the current EU budget 
has insufficient possibilities for supporting the objectives of the Net -Zero Industry Act and for ensuring 
a level-playing field between Member States, relative to the identified public investment needs”. 
The critical issue of fi nancing for production sites should be adequately addressed, both within and 
outside of the NZIA: 
 
 Revision of the State Aid rules under the Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework (TCTF): the 
points 85 and 86 in Section 2.8 of the TCTF do not allow for  aid for operational expenditure in 
compensation for energy costs as well as other structural disadvantages during the scale -up 
phase of the industry across the solar PV supply chain. This is particularly concerning in respect 
to point 86 of the TCTF which is presented as a clause to allow for matching aid vis-à-vis Europe’s 
global competitors, for example in China and the United States where energy costs are two to 
three times lower and where industries can benefit from OPEX related state aid.  
 
In addition, point 86 comes with burdensome requirements on information provision (under Annex II) 
and geographical location that are at odds with the reality of cluster-based industrial siting and supply 
chains. These conditions prevent a broad and effective respon se to the United States’ Inflation 
Reduction Act. By focusing on assisted areas, other important criteria are neglected such as 
sustainability aspects of a ‘closed loop’ production site where all side- and waste products are captured 
and reused.  
 
While we fully recognize the need for cohesion in the EU internal market, we call on European decision-
makers to clarify the recognition in related/triggered investments in the value chain when evaluating 
the cohesion effects. Here, special attention should be giv en to the challenge of establishing new 
locations across the EU/EEA for certain value chain steps of the PV value chain. Polysilicon 
manufacturing sites are for instance highly complex and integrated industrial installations that require 
a set of special c onditions with regards to (chemical) infrastructure needs, safety and environmental 
requirements, and make it therefore very difficult to be established at new locations. More clarity 
should be provided with respect to the  matching aid  calculation method a nd to the maximum 
contribution achievable, through a dedicated specific guideline. To simplify the qualification processes, 
Member States, companies and their registered associations, and research institutions should be 
allowed to provide blueprint  studies instead of individual proof  (as demanded in Annex II) for 
technologies which qualify as a proof that the aid in a particular non -EU country is per se higher than 
in the EU.  
We therefore call on European decision-makers to consider revising the point 86 of the TCTF in 
reflection of the above concerns as soon as possible. 
 Establishing a European financial instrument dedicated to Net Zero technologies with high 
dependency on one source of supply, like solar PV. Such instrument would complement national

spending rules via the TCTF . Particularly in recognition of the need to maintain cohesion in the

A technology-specific fund is justified given the prime importance of solar PV in Europe’s energy system

high dependency on one source of supply, well above the threshold of 65% as outlined in Article 19 2d. 
We argue that technologies that are above the 65% threshold should get access to a dedicated fund 
in order to reach the goals of the Act in the shortest possible time.  A fund for 65%+ depende nt Net 
Zero technologies could be set up under the auspices of the Net Zero Europe Platform’s role  under 
“coordination of finance” (Art 15) and sourced either from existing EU funding, like the Innovation 
Fund, or new EU funding, for example in the form of a European Sovereignty Fund. 
 The Net Zero Europe Platform should also look at contributing to transparency and simplicity in 
financial support by creating a mandatory EU register of the different support regimes on EU and 
Member State level, published on an open and accessible EU webpage (Art 15)

2. Scope and ambition of the Net Zero Industry Act 
 Recital 17: SolarPower Europe supports the ambition of reaching at least 30GW manufacturing 
capacity by 2030 across the solar PV supply chain, which is in line with the 30GW of committed 
investment for PV manufacturing by 2025 set in the context of the European Solar Industry 
Alliance.  
 
SolarPower Europe supports bringing the technology specific ambitions from recital to legislation, 
for example under Art 2 a. It should be noted that, given that solar deployment is projected to 
accelerate well above the European Commission expectation s – in the direction of 125GW by 2030 
according to SolarPower Europe latest European Market Outlook – the 40% equivalent capacity is close 
to 40-55GW across the solar PV supply chain.  
 
 Art 3.1(a), Annex: The solar PV supply chain includes all relevant value chain segments, spanning 
from raw material extraction to final solar product manufacturing, including all intermediate 
manufacturing step, as well as connected production chains for industrial equipment technology, 
inverters and other BOS components.  
 
 
 
We call on European decision-makers to, therefore, extend the definition of Net-Zero technologies to 
the full solar PV supply chain, explicitly including input material and raw material extraction as well 
as inverter in this article and in the Annex. SolarPower Europe suggests the following wording in the 
Art 3.1(a) Annex:   
“Strategic Net-Zero Technologies Solar PV supply chain, including all relevant value chain segments, 
spanning from raw material extraction to final solar product manufacturing, including all 
intermediate manufacturing steps, as well as connected production chains for industrial equipment 
technology, inverters, and other balance of system components; and solar thermal technologies.”

 Art 19.2 d: the methodology to calculate the 65% threshold is not clear. It can be interpreted as 
relating to the market shares of the final product (the product that is subject to public 
procurement or is deployed following auctions, i.e. solar modules), or as relat ing to an average 
of input materials and components going into the final product. We urge European decision -
makers to clarify this as soon as possible by providing specific guidance defining the 65% 
threshold methodology and its rationale, as soon as possible and no later than June 2023.  
 
 
 
 Art 19.3: European lawmaker should improve the legal clarity of this article by specifying that the

including criteria referred to in paragraph 2, points (a) and (b), of the same article.

 Art 19.4, Art 20.3:  
 
- SolarPower Europe  supports the principle of the cost proportionality clause, given the 
importance of accelerating the pace of solar deployment and the energy transition in general for 
the EU’s climate and security goals at affordable cost to society. It is, however, hard to define a 
specific percentage threshold which could vary by technology. While we support the inclusion of 
 
 
the provision for applying a cost proportionality threshold from the outset, we strongly suggest  
a proper ex-post analysis after two years following the entry into force of the Regulation. 
 
- It is currently not clear how the cost difference would be calculated. It can be interpreted as the 
difference between the lowest and the highest bid, the difference between the market price and 
the average price of all bids submitted or of all contracts awarded in the tender or on a historic 
basis in comparable past tenders, etc. We urge European decision-makers to clarify this as soon 
as possible and no later than June 2023.  
 
 Art 20: It is important to maintain a synchronization between solar deployment and reshoring 
solar PV supply chains in Europe, which takes time to build up. Such synchronization can be done 
via EU -wide coordination of solar auctions and procurement policies that reflect a realistic 
projection of scaled -up production capacities in Europe – for example 5GW in 2025, 10GW in 
2028 and 30GW in 2030. In light of that, SPE suggest the non-price criteria to apply only to solar-
specific auctions reflecting these capacities. This is also important to ensure solar PV can compete 
on the same level playing field with other technologies in non -technology sp ecific or hybrid 
auctions.

 Art 22: It is essential for investor certainty to guarantee a fully harmonized approach to non-price 
criteria in public procurement and auctions across EU Member States. The categories, therefore, 
need to be further developed, outlining exact details on criteria and thresholds that are clear, 
meaningful and practical. SolarPower Europe suggests this exercise be developed in a Delegated 
Act. Ultimately, auction design is subject to approval by the EU competition authorities under the 
Guidelines on State aid for climate, environmental protection and energy ('CEEAG'). A Delegated 
Act would, therefore, be justified as a legal tool as it leverages changes to such primary legislation.

 Art 23: European Net Zero Industry Academies  
The Commission shall support, including through the provision of seed -funding, the establishment of 
European Net Zero Industry Academies, which have as their objectives to:  
(1) develop and diffuse learning and certification programmes, content, and learning and training 
materials for training and education on developing, producing, installing, commissioning, 
operating, maintaining and recycling net -zero technologies, on raw materials, as well as to 
 
 
support the capa cities of public authorities competent to issue permits and authorisations 
referred to in Chapter II and contracting authorities referred to in Chapter IV of this Regulation; 
 
(2) enable and promote the use of the learning programmes, content and materials by education 
and training providers, such as industry stakeholders , in the Member States, among others by 
training trainers and develop mechanisms to ensure the quality of the training offered by 
education and training providers in the Member States based on the above learning 
programmes, content and materials;  In the dev elopment and deployment of training content 
and certification schemes European Net-Zero Academies should pay particular attention to the 
already existing or to be developed online training and certification courses of Net -Zero 
industry stakeholders and make sure that this content is made available throughout the Union. 
 
(3) in close cooperation with all relevant stakeholder s develop and deploy credentials, including 
micro-credentials, to facilitate the transparency of skills acquired and enhance the transferability 
between jobs and the cross -border mobility of the workforce, and to promote matching with

EURAXESS.

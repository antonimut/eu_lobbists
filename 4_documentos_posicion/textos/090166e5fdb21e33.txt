Carbon Capture Utilisation and Storage is key to meet 
the Green Deal objective of climate neutrality by 2050 
A clear regulatory framework is needed for the successful and 
rapid development of a CO2 pipeline network in the EU 
 
Context  
The latest Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change  (IPPC) report1 highlights carbon 
capture and storage (CCS) and Carbon Capture and Utilisation (CCU) as critical 
technologies to mitigate climate change  and  reach net-zero emissions by mid-century. In 
particular, CCUS will play a fundamental role in decarbonising heavy industry across 
Europe. Enabling conditions such as policy instruments and de-risking project investments  
are key to allow for their roll-out. 
European Commission Energy Commissioner Simson underlined the importance of CCUS for 
the EU's Green Deal at the last CCUS Forum: " Without CCS and CCU, it will be practically 
impossible to limit the global warming to the 1.5 degrees Celsius objective. The 
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change report is clear about it.  And the Commission 
modelling also shows that the EU will need to capture and utilise or store between 300 and 
640 Million Tonnes of carbon dioxide per year by 2050 to meet its climate goals." 
Fluxys’ ambition 
Fluxys is a fully independent gas infrastructu re group with 1, 300 employees active in gas 
transmission and storage and liquefied natural gas  that operates across, among others, 
Belgium, Germany, France, Switzerland, The Netherlands and the UK. 
As an infrastructure operator, Fluxys is ready to deliver on the EU’s climate and energy goals 
by developing fit-for-purpose infrastructure for the molecules of the future. By 2030, Fluxys’ 
wants to develop a transport capacity of 30 TWh of hydrogen and 30 million tonnes of CO2 
per year to our customers . Fluxys is investing in an open access CO2 infrastructure, 
supporting the decarbonisation of the Belgian industry and its neighbouring countries such 
as Germany, France, Switzerland and the Netherlands (Fig. 1). Fluxys is looking at three exit 
points for CO2 to reach safe storage sites  from Belgium: Antwerp, Ghent and Zeebrugge . 
Two of these projects have been awarded Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) funding. The 
Antwerp@C CO2 export terminal has received CEF funding for works. Its capacity will 
amount to 2,5 Mtpa (with the ambition to reach up to 10 Mtpa by 2030) and commissioning

Key Recommendations for the development of a CO2 infrastructure in the EU 
1. Develop a clear r egulatory framework  enabling decarbonisation of industry  and 
ensure the independence of CO2 infrastructure operators 
The development of a CO2 infrastructure network (transmission pipelines, liquefaction 
terminals, etc.) will require significant CAPEX expenditure. It makes no economic sense to 
construct several parallel infrastructures in  densely populated  areas serving a given 
industrial cluster or a CO2 source. Hence, a CO2 infrastructure network can, in most cases, 
be considered as a natural m onopoly. Similarly to the development of natural gas  and 
hydrogen infrastructure, non-discriminatory, cross-border and third -party access to CO2 
transport infrastructure shou ld be the guiding principles for a n enabling  regulatory 
framework. The CCUS value chain is still in an early phase in Europe so it is crucial that the  
future regulatory framework remains flexible to the developments of the market  while 
providing a clear a nd predictable direction to back the  required investments in essential 
infrastructure.  
Given the monopolistic character of CO2 infrastructure,  network operators should be  in 
charge of developing and operatin g it  in a regulated manner  allowing for its efficient 
development from an economical and technical point of view . The regulatory framework 
should ensure the independence of network o perators from companies that are emitting 
or reusing CO2 . This will prevent the undesirable practice of players develop ing the most 
profitable segments/clusters of such a network . While in the short term,  industrial clusters 
should be the focus for decarbonisation, t he future CO2 transmission network should be 
accessible to any emitter who wants to transport its CO2 to safe storage sites.  
Summary of key recommendations: 
1. Develop a clear regulatory framework and ensure the independence of CO2 
infrastructure operators 
2. Provide predictable revenue for CCUS projects 
3. Update the European Storage Atlas 
4. Standardize CO2 specifications 
5. Require Member States to declare the planned role of CCUS in the next revision 
of their National Energy and Climate Action Plans and long-term climate 
strategies 
6. Diversify access to storage sites by applying similar rules on CO2 storage under 
different ETS systems 
7. The Net-Zero Industry Act should include objectives for CO2 transport 
infrastructure development, not only storage

As with hydrogen, where possible, the onshore and offshore CO2 grid , will be made  from 
repurposed natural gas pipelines. This is true in particular, for distances below 250 km. Whilst 
each Member State will need to determine the approach mos t appropriate for its specific 
circumstances, it is logical that the natural gas TSO would be responsible for building out the 
'essential facility' parts of the future CO2 grid. This would go a long way to alleviate issues of 
legal unbundling and non -discriminatory access , and be , by far, the most cost -effective 
approach. 
2. Provide predictable revenue for CCUS projects 
De-risking investments in CCUS projects is key to kick-start the market. Funding opportunities 
should be revised and increased to avoid the ‘c hicken and egg’ challenges across the 
CCUS value chain, and take into account the need to  rapidly increase storage capacity. 
The application process to access EU funding  is also quite len gthy and often takes several 
years.  
Funding mechanisms need to address the different commercial risks along the value chain 
and over the lifetime of CCUS projects. There are already several existing EU funding 
mechanisms that can contribute to the develop ment of a European CO2 transport and 
storage infrastructure network. But existing funding schemes could benefit from some 
improvements. For example, a separate funding window for CCUS projects in the EU 
Innovation Fund would simplify the process and avoid timing issues when establishing back-
to-back contractual rights and o bligations between emitters, transporters and storers.  The 
Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) also foresees funding for CO2 infrastructure projects but in 
the coming years, it will be crucial to ensure sufficient money is available when more CO2 
projects will want to apply.  
Furthermore, additional funding mechanisms are needed to de -risk long -term revenue 
streams for investors along the CCUS value chain beyond the initial project phase . While 
public funding remains of utmost importance,  Important Projects of  Common European 
Interest (IPCEIs) represent a  further effective mechanism to ensure commitment from 
Member States, both practical and financial, and galvanise industry to action.  Similarly to 
the recent IPCEIs on hydrogen, the E uropean Commission should also pave the way for 
IPCEIs on CCUS. CCUS technology significantly contributes to the decarbonisation 
objectives of the EU and often involves several EU countries. This makes CCUS an  ideal 
candidate for an IPCEI.  
Finally, Carbon Contracts for Difference ( CCfDs) could be a good funding mechanism to 
guarantee a fixed price that rewards CO2 emission reductions . This will allow  to hedge 
against future carbon prices under the ETS and will enable large scale investments that will 
reduce emissions in industrial sectors, such as cement, steel or basic chemistry. 
3. Update the European Storage Atlas 
Existing mature  storage projects are already over -subscribed and this threatens to be a 
major bottleneck for the EU and Member States in meeting their climate goals.  While there 
is no lack of potential for CO2 storage in Europe, more effort must be put into translating this

potential into usable storage projects. At the technical level, this involves supporting 
prospective storage promoters to swiftly identify suitable stor age sites. And limited access 
to information has been highlighted as a key barrier in site appraisal and ch aracterisation.  
Therefore, competent authorities should outline a pathway that enables future storage 
operators to access non -confidential informati on required to undertake site appraisal 
activities such as re-use of wells, well maintenance records, annulus pressure data, reservoir 
performance etc. Access to this information by the successful bidders , on mutually agreed 
commercial terms, would benefit the development of storage sites in Europe and should be 
an integral part of the update of the existing European Storage Atlas. 
4. Standardize CO2 specifications 
The transport of CO2  will require handling CO2 streams from different technologies and 
sources. Driving a standardised approach in key areas of the CCUS value chain will be 
important in promoting interconnections a nd cost efficient deployment of new 
infrastructure. 
From a technical perspective there is a need for standardisation of CO2 specifications (both 
gaseous and liquid) , taking into account the different technologies and segments of the 
CCUS value chain. Standardisation should address matters linked with the commodity 
aspect such as composition, purity, pressures and temperatures, as well as standards linked 
with the design of pipelines, valves, ships, and other parts of the transport value chain (e.g., 
loading and off -loading). This will support interoperability across Europe, across national 
borders and different transport modalities. 
5. Require Member States to declare the planned role of CCUS in the next revision of their 
National Energy and Climate Action Plans and long-term climate strategies 
An enabling EU level policy framework should include a requirement for Member States to 
clearly outline their intentions for CCUS technology in the decarbonisation of industry. This 
can be done through the National Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs) and long-term climate 
strategies.  
The corresponding requirement for temporary domestic storage development or  CO2 
export to other States and concrete steps to achieve these goals are all important elements 
to highlight. This will give industry, investors and the whole value chain , visibility on future 
developments of the market. 
6. Diversify access to storage sites by applying similar rules on CO2 storage under different 
ETS systems  
Diversification of access to safe storage sites for captured CO2 will be key in the coming 
years. For example, The UK southern part of the North Sea holds great potentials for storage 
of CO2. 
Emitters in the EU and UK ETS need to be able to store CO2 across CCS systems, while 
benefitting from the same incentives. UK emitters storing their CO2 in a UK site are exempted 
6   
 
from the need to surrender allowances under the UK ETS. Emitters in the  EU ETS storing their 
CO2 in a site located in the EU ETS are also exempted from surrendering allowances under 
the EU ETS. It is unclear, however, what type of rules apply when an emitter in one ETS stores 
CO2 in the other ETS . Greater legal certainty woul d provide clear financial incent ives to 
emitters and CO2 transporters and create a CO2 transport and storage network with larger 
economies of scale, lower costs, and enhanced innovation.  To create the best possible 
conditions for cross-border CO2 transport, an agreement between the EU and UK ETS is key. 
7. The Net -Zero Industry Act should include objectives for CO2 transport infrastructure 
development, not only storage 
CO2 transport infrastructure contributes to the competitiveness of the EU’s net-zero industry 
supply chain and has a positive spill-over effect on it. 
Fluxys supports the objective of at least 50 million  tonnes of CO2 annual injection capacity 
by 2030 for storage in the EU under Article 16. However, a value chain approach is required 
to deliver t his objective. CO2 capture and transport infrastructure need to be developed 
together with storage.  
Fluxys welcomes provisions to streamline permitting and licensing processes under Articles 4 
to 15. However, it is crucial that the streamlining of permitt ing and licensing processes 
applies to all parts of the value chain including transport of CO2.  
Finally, the Act mentions the importance of establishing a Union si ngle market for CO2 
storage. While this is positive, it is crucial that the EU market is su pplemented by a Europe -
wide market for CO2 storage that covers the EEA and the UK  given the important of 
countries like Norway in providing safe storage sites fo r the decarbonization of heavy 
industry in the EU.

Brintbranchen/Hydrogen Denmark’s consultation response  
Regulation on establishing a framework of measures for strengthening 
Europe’s net-zero technology products manufacturing ecosystem (Net 
Zero Industry Act) 
 
Hydrogen Denmark (Brintbranchen) is the national association for hydrogen and Power-to-X 
and represents more than 30 members across the entire value chain: from technology 
manufacturers to energy producers and consumers, as well as hubs, knowledge centres , 
financers, infrastructure developers and consultants in the field. 
We welcome the Net Zero Industry Act (NZIA) as a key piece of legislation in safeguarding the 
EU’s supply chain of the future, and thereby ensuring that we can meet  our climate and 
energy goals. Below we detail some considerations on specific points. 
 
One-size-fits-all approach is not the most sensible. 
While we welcome specific targets in general and believe that concentrating this regulation 
on a limited set of technologies is the right approach, we believe the target of strategic net -
zero technologies as listed in the Annex reach 40% of the Union’s ann ual deployment needs 
(article 1.2.a) may be unhelpfully wide.  
Firstly, because each of the technologies in the Annex have different starting points, and they 
serve diverse goals, and can therefore benefit from technology -specific targets, that are 
defined in line with other relevant European goals and targets (from other parts of the 
regulatory framework).  
Secondly, because for example in the case of hydrogen technologies, electrolysers and fuel 
cells are bundled together in the Annex.  These technologies are not one and the same. 
Furthermore, components , although mentioned throughout the regulation (and specially 
under the definition of ‘net-zero technologies’), are not explicitly supported. 
Hydrogen Denmark believes the specific target  for electrolyser manufacturing capacity 
should reach 25 GW by 2025 already. This is what is estimated to be necessary to meet the 
goal of 100 GW of installed capacity in 2030 (as per REPowerEU). Moreover, key components 
and parts of the value chain in which the EU has a competitive edge should be identified and 
assigned a target. Compressors, hydrogen refueling stations and storage tanks are some of 
the first ones to look into in this context.

Provisions for support of research on next-generation technologies are missing. 
In establishing the framework for “innovating and scaling up the manufacturing capacity of 
net-zero technologies” (article 1) it is important to keep this development sustainable. That is, 
that there must be some focus on what comes after the first “push”.  
In this conte xt, we miss provisions that support research on next -generation technologies. 
This is not only for the benefit of these industries, but also for the benefit of other goals of the 
Union. A critical and urgent example of this is  the upcoming rules on PFAS  (per- and 
polyfluoroalkyl substances), by which the hydrogen industry is threatened with a potential 
ban that would affect the production of key components and the overall development of in 
particular PEM technology (proton exchange membrane electrolysis).  
Without support and/or a clear plan for research on alternatives and next -generation 
technologies, the goals of the NZIA will not be sustainable in the longer term, i.e. beyond 2030 
(and in the PFAS example, they will likely not even be achieved, as they w ill be trumped by 
other EU regulation). 
 
Missing funding needs to be addressed. 
The NZIA sends a strong signal to industry, but it is not necessarily aligned with existing 
funding instruments. For instance, the Temporary Crisis and and Transition Framework offers 
only a short-term opportunity for Member States to fund electrolyser manufacturing, but not 
fuel cell manufacturing. Else, there is a (limited) range of other EU funding options, but these 
are not necessarily dedicated to hydrogen technologies, and are based on rather burdensome 
processes for application and approval. 
Meeting the goals of the NZIA from the “electrolysers and fuel cells” part of the Annex, 
requires a dedicated EU fund for the deployment of factories across the EU. The proposed EU 
sovereign investment fund could also be a viable instrument, offering marked -based capital 
through various forms of equity. An EIB willingness to increase their debt risk exposure would 
provide another alternative for EU-backed capital. 
 
Public procurement rules and criteria need sharpening. 
The scope of the rules is at times somewhat unclear. For example, on fuel cells it is not entirely 
clear whether they apply to standalone fuel cells, or also to fuel cells integrated into vehicles. 
We urge the Commission to sharpen the scope to avoid such interpretative complications. 
The resilience criterium should be more precise, so that the 65% of single source trigger is 
adjusted upwards (or entirely removed) for countries with whom the EU has entered into a 
free trade agreement. After all, these markets do not constitute the same type of risk in terms 
of resilience as markets with whom the EU does not have a free trade agreement , and so 
applying the same resilience criterium could ri sk locking some competitive bids out of the 
procurement process, for a minor improvement in the level of risk to resilience.

And lastly, the threshold for disproportionate costs is at this point not consistent with reality, 
in our view. Various offtakers have cited a 20-30% green premium to make the business case 
for e.g. green steel work. While this green premium will most likely decrease over time, 
currently a 10% threshold would rather have a negative impact in developing the supply chain. 
We therefore suggest that instead of a 10% threshold, a higher level is accepted in the first 
few years, and that the threshold is then gradually reduced.

Introduction 
The Net Zero Industry Act (“NZIA”) – the flagship file of the EU Green Deal Industrial Plan (“GDIP”) – aims to boost European clean 
technology manufacturing. To support this aim, Articles 19 and 20 of the NZIA seek to favour new supply chains and support demand 
for European products through non-price criteria in auctions for the allocation of renewable support schemes and public procurement 
procedures.  
As currently drafted, the NZIA non-price criteria threaten to negatively impact the achievement of the EU 2030 renewable and 
decarbonisation targets and impact economic recovery while increasing electricity prices for businesses, SMEs and households. 
While the NZIA provisions encourage downstream operators to purchase locally manufactured solar components, there is currently 
neither sufficient availability of such local components, nor is there any indication that these will become available in the EU at 
competitive prices in the short to medium term. While the ambition to “de-risk” European clean technology supply chains is an 
important strategic goal, the current proposal instead suggests a “de-coupling” approach. 
Setting the scene: no “one-size fits all” approach 
To be truly effective, the NZIA should recognise that not all technologies have the same starting point in terms of European 
manufacturing. For solar, reaching the NZIA ambition is far from a simple task. The EU solar manufacturing capacity experimented a 
rapid decline in the mid to late 2000’s and has not held a relevant market share in most steps of the global solar supply chain – being 
almost negligible in the ingots, wafers and cells segments – for at least a decade1. 
For solar panels, reaching the 30GW / 45% manufacturing target (as per the Commission's Staff Working Document of 23 March 2023) 
by 2030 will be very challenging while it would be much easier (and even too conservative) for other technologies. 
Not only would imposing a full local requirement along the full solar manufacturing supply chain be very costly, but it would be 
counterproductive against the superior objectives of low and stable electricity prices, higher energy security and accelerated 
deployment (as further developed below).  
Targeting all activities along the clean tech value chain will not provide true resilience. On the contrary, targeted financial support to 
the manufacturing activities where European businesses have high skills and competences and are closer to achieving competitiveness 
will better contribute to long-term stability and sustainability of manufacturing of clean technologies in Europe.

Renewable support schemes will continue to play an essential role in enabling the deployment of solar in the EU necessary to achieve 
this target. The consulting firm Aurora estimated that almost 60% of the solar capacity expected to come online in the region by 2030 
will be financed through competitive auctions2, with Contracts for Differences (CfDs) being the most popular scheme. 
Introducing local content criteria in EU renewable auctions at a moment where the volume of EU manufactured solar components is 
manifestly insufficient to accommodate demand (e.g., 10GW of existing solar module manufacturing capacity – not all of which will is 
necessarily available for the domestic market – vs. c. 50GW annual deployment needed to achieve the EU Solar Strategy objectives) 
may de facto paralyse the renewable auction market and drive electricity prices for business and households upward. 
To avoid the risk of auctions coming to a near complete halt, the NZIA obligation for Member States to consider non-price factors 
should consider the specificities of each technology. Again, far from a “one-size-fits all” approach, the resilience criteria of Article 
19.2(d) should only apply to a carefully selected number of technologies where the EU can secure leadership: technologies where the 
technological and innovation components are most significant, where a push for scale up is required and where a more discrete cost 
competitiveness gap exists. 
In this line, we would encourage a derogation of the “resilience” criteria of Article 19.2(d) for renewable auctions for the deployment 
of utility scale solar. A mature technology for which there currently exists a limited EU manufacturing base, the cost competitiveness 
gap is significant, and which accelerated short term deployment is a key contributor to the EU’s decarbonisation goals, to lower and 
stable electricity generation prices and to the competitiveness of the European economy in comparison with other regions. 
As such, the resilience criteria would apply only to “traditional” public procurement procedures with a focus on installing electricity 
generation units that do not impact businesses and continue benefiting households as the economic cost comparison of such units is 
measured against electricity retail tariffs and not wholesale prices. 
This way, the impact of the resilience criteria on the pace of EU solar deployment enabled by renewable support schemes is mitigated. 
Introducing non-price criteria in renewable auctions will increase electricity prices to 
the detriment of European households and the industry 
As mentioned above, to set up a competitive solar supply chain in Europe, the industry would need to overcome significant hurdles. 
European companies are structurally disadvantaged compared to other regions by the higher administrative, material, utilities, and 
capital costs.  
Other regions capitalise on a multi decade-long learning curve in terms of scale, cost and operational efficiency, vertical integration 
and already established viable ecosystems of suppliers and equipment providers. In fact, according to the Becquerel Institute / 
International Solar Alliance report “Building Resilient Solar PV Supply Chains”3, 85% to 90% of the competitive cost gap between 
producing in Europe to existing supply chains is due to economies of scale and vertical integration.  
The consultant firm McKinsey estimates that, even if European manufacturers achieve highly optimised levels of excellence and scale,

Requiring bidders in renewable auctions to comply with local content criteria will result in using more expensive material, higher bid 
prices and more expensive electricity prices for European households and industry.  
To mitigate this effect, it is imperative that.  
● The 10% cost safeguard of Article 19(3) of the NZIA is maintained and is of mandatory application for Member States;  
● In line with the current CEEAG, the non-price criteria of Articles 19 and 20 shall not exceed 30% in the weighing of all selection 
criteria; and 
● When designing auctions Member States need to estimate the increase in bid prices that will result from the application of the 
NZIA non-price criteria, and factor such increase in the auction price ceilings. This will mitigate the risk of auction 
undersubscription that can occur if auction price ceilings are under-estimated by not considering the structural cost of requiring 
non-price criteria. 
Similarly, given the high cost of having a supply chain approach in Europe, we suggest that the economic support of the NZIA is given 
to the areas where European manufacturing businesses are close to being cost competitive and play a role in the international supply 
chain contributing to the diversification and resilience objectives.  
Non-price criteria create legal uncertainty and increase the bureaucratic burden of 
renewable auctions 
Price-based renewable auctions incentivise market operators to develop, design and build projects at competitive prices. Price is a 
simple, objective and transparent criteria that encourages competitiveness by incentivising cost efficiency through technological 
advances, economies of scale and other factors and guarantees that the most cost-efficient operator will be awarded the support.

On the contrary, non-price criteria will increase entry barriers for participation in renewable auctions. Non-price criteria are difficult to 
design, measure, assess and monitor once the tender has been allocated. They increase the bureaucratic load of public authorities and 
create legal uncertainty for bidders. Non-price criteria lend themselves to less transparency and increase litigiousness.  
Developers across the EU are currently facing other mounting pressures such as inflation, electricity price caps, market interventions 
generating regulatory stability, permitting delays, too-low reference prices, supply chain restrictions and increased cost of capital.  
These pressures are materialising across Europe – with 2022 seeing an increasing trend of undersubscription (see, for instance, the 
renewable auctions held in Germany, Italy, Spain and Poland in the second half of 2022).  
To mitigate the impact of the NZIA non-price factors leading to the failure of renewable auctions across Europe: 
● Member States shall have an obligation in the NZIA to design the auction criteria in a manner that is clear, transparent, and 
announced sufficiently in advance of the auction celebration to allow developers to know the rules of the game and prepare their 
bids in advance, 
● The NZIA non-price criteria of Article 19 shall be predicated exclusively of the final product. On the contrary, if the criteria were 
required of the full value chain, public authorities would be forced to perform full traceability checks on all components and 
machinery involved in the PV module value chain – an exercise which may prove nearly impossible considering the existing 
complexity of the solar PV supply chain with the involvement of a myriad of companies and geographies. 
Final remarks 
While de-risking clean technology supply chains is an important strategic objective for achieving the EU 2030 renewable and 
decarbonisation targets, it is imperative for the NZIA to get it right. The EU should focus on the sectors where the competitiveness gap 
is more discrete and where the NZIA can really make a difference in the medium-to-long term without compromising other EU 
decarbonisation objectives. This is particularly the case for the solar industry, which continues to be in an upward growth trajectory in 
terms of deployment, making it one of the key technologies for achieving the EU’s decarbonisation targets and energy security.  
In the same vein, renewable support schemes will continue to be an essential tool for the decarbonisation of the energy sector and for 
achieving the EU’s climate goals. Up until now, they have also been designed to achieve the most competitive prices, for the benefit of 
European businesses, SMEs and consumers. We should proceed with caution in the design of non-price criteria and ensure that 
additional regulatory burdens do not convert auctions into slow, costly, unworkable and ultimately futile exercises, thus slowing down 
renewable deployment and increasing electricity prices to the detriment of the EU’s competitiveness.

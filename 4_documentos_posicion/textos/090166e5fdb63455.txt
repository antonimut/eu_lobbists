 
The European Critical Raw Materials Act 
AEGIS Europe position  
 
Introduction  
This document sets out AEGIS Europe’s views on the Commission’s Proposal on a European 
Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) published on 16 March 2023. The EU will need massive 
amounts of strategic raw materials in the coming years to meet the requirements of the twin 
transition while preserving Europe’s strategic autonomy.  
The EU industrial sectors grouped within AEGIS Europe represent the entire value chain 
ranging from commodities such as ferro -alloys, non -ferrous metals or ceramics  to end 
products like railway equipment, bicycles or solar panels. Our members account for an annual 
turnover of €530 – €550 billion and 1.65 – 1.7 million of jobs across the EU. 
Bearing in mind the initiatives taken by the United States (i.e., Inflation Reduction Act), Japan 
(i.e., Green Transformation Programme) and China (i.e., latest Year Plan), the AEGIS Europe 
membership acknowledges the importance of the Critical Raw Materials Act and the Net-Zero 
Industry Act.  
First of all, AEGIS Europe would like to point out that the CRMA , together with the Net Zero 
Industry Act, are essential for the EU industry to achieve the objectives of the European Green 
Deal and REPowerEU. However, the EU proposals need further improvement and must be 
fully complementary with one another in order to be able to really support the European 
industry in their pledge to decarbonise and do away with third country strategic 
dependencies.  
Thus, echoing Commissioner Thierry Breton’s statement, these two Acts could represent the 
bedrock of the re -industrialisation of Europe but in order to achieve such ambitious goals 
much more is needed. First, any EU Industrial policy should better recognise that European 
manufacturing is central to Europe’s values and prosperity. Second, priority should be given 
to ensuring favourable framework conditions in order to maintain and support the existing 
EU production, while diversification through imports should be only a flanking measure. Third, 
there must be consistency among the various instruments and equal access to financing 
across the Union. Fourth, the EU must invest in strategic sectors  be it clean technologies or 
strategic/critical raw materials . Fifth, compulsory  self-sufficiency targets for raw materials 
and certain clean technologies are needed.

1. Implementing adequate framework conditions to ensure long -term autonomy and 
a level playing field 
In order to achieve strategic autonomy and reduce overdependence, we must first strengthen 
existing European value chains and ensure a  safe/robust level of self -sufficiency1 by setting 
the key framework conditions to boost investments and expand the EU industrial base. 
These goals will not be reached without a global level playing field through a well -designed 
trade policy and strong trade defence instruments. 2 The latter need to be used in order to 
protect the mining and pr ocessing/recycling industries which could be prevented from 
establishing themselves in the EU because of unfairly traded imports. To achieve this, China’s 
massive overcapacities in certain raw materials need to be addressed urgently 3. The 
introduction of long-term mechanisms to facilitate the production, accessibility, and 
consumption of decarbonised electricity and energy at globally competitive prices is also a 
non-negotiable for the global competitiveness of EU manufacturing industries. Critical and 
strategic raw materials are key elements for the production of clean technologies and the 
correlation with the Net-Zero Industry Act should be established ensuring a win-win situation 
for relevant stakeholders. 
While boosting EU manufacturing should be the Act's primary focus, we must also strengthen 
trade relationships with like -minded and resource -rich partners adhering to fair trade 
principles and sustainability standards. In addition, the EU’s Free Trade Agreements (FTA’s) 
must promote the highest possible environmental and social standards. 
Finally, policy consistency across EU regulations, especially dealing with energy, trade, 
environmental and chemical aspects, is necessary to ensure predictability and inspire investor 
confidence. 
2. Improving the classification of raw materials 
 
Our sectors consider that the current critical raw materials and strategic raw materials lists 
should be complemented with materials which are relevant for the Green and Digital 
transition or might become critical 4/strategic in the future . Thus, aluminium5, ferro-alloys, 
synthetic graphite (in addition to natural graphite) and silver should also be deemed strategic.

AEGIS Europe developed a set of criteria to identify which raw materials should be considered 
critical and/or strategic to achieve our ambition of resilience and autonomy in the face of the 
Green Deal and its related policies:  
▪ Their relevance to the twin transition and ecosystems/applications/technologies;  
▪ The r isk of carbon leakage in Europe, to assess the overall outlook of potential future 
import dependencies; 
▪ Their relevance for societally critical sectors in Europe, such as pharmaceuticals, mobility, 
transport, defence, renewable energy technologies, aerospace, etc.; 
▪ Their cross-sectorial relevance: the higher the cross -sectorial impact of a raw material, 
the higher the classification of a material as strategic should be; 
▪ The thresholds for supply shortage risks and supply concentra tions, along with an alert 
mechanism triggering a set of actions for when a material is close to the threshold; 
▪ Their sensitivity level as regards continuity of production and the difficulty of establishing 
new production (for example because of their high capital-intensity); 
▪ The environmental aspect; 
▪ The replacement risk by third-country suppliers, provided it is properly defined; 
▪ The high or perfect substitutability in the metal group (aluminium & copper, natural & 
synthetic graphite) which render the exclusion of substitutes logically irrational.   
 
3. Recognising the strategic importance of certain post-consumer recycled materials 
Our sectors requests that the EU recognizes the strategic importance of secondary , post -
consumer raw materials6 (e.g. metal scrap) for the Green and Digital transition or which might 
become critical in the future. The same criteria as those outlined above can be used to identify 
those recycled materials which are of strategic importance.  For instance, the recycling  of 
ferrous scrap in the steel industry is one of the clearest examples of the possibility to combine 
the main goals set by the EU for the coming decades: circular economy, decarbonisation and 
energy saving. The ambitious decarbonisation path towards carbo n neutrality will involve a 
radical transformation of the European steel industry, requiring huge investments in new 
production technologies, but also the maximisation of the use of a low-carbon secondary raw 
material such as ferrous scrap. Demand for ferr ous scrap is expected to grow much faster 
than availability in the EU, overcoming availability well before 2030. 
4. Boosting circularity & recycling  
Rules that facilitate  the recovery of strategic recycled/secondary raw materials (e.g., scrap) 
and incentivise their use in Europe7 will play a fundamental role in ensuring our autonomy.  
Measures meant to increase the efficiency of collection, sorting, and pre -treatment of 
secondary raw materials are needed across the value chains, with pa rticular focus on the 
recycling of the “difficult” post -consumer scrap. This requires prioritising strategic materials 
circularity in waste legislation, harmonizing waste classification systems across Member

5. Faster permitting procedures and easier access to financing 
Identification of priority projects when it comes to activities of mining, refining, and recycling 
operations is also necessary to  improve the security of supply for EU industries. Once such 
priority projects that cover the full value chain s of critical raw and recycled materials are 
identified, accelerated national level permitting procedures and easier access to financing are 
critical to ensure strategic autonomy in those value chains. 
Last but not least, financing is also needed to boost research and development in order to 
achieve EU leadership in sustainability . As such, it is important to consider the role of the 
European Investment Bank and strengthen the presence of the European Raw Materials 
Alliance in supporting European investments and increasing investment confidence to create 
a greater pool of public and private finance for the European raw materials industries. 
Conclusion 
The EU should put forward initiatives that promote European content in products across the 
value chain. Public procurement should be a role model for the end -consumer and prioritize 
goods whose components originate in the European Union, especially in case that such 
products are of strategic importance or destined for essential uses. 
The Green Deal Industrial Plan must be a comprehensive roadmap that supports and boosts 
our industries, creating jobs, and generating economic growth, while ensuring a cleaner and 
greener Europe for generations to come. This historic opportunity must be seized to build a 
more re silient and sustainable Europe that works for all.  Furthermore, the envisaged 
framework should foster reciprocity in international trade exchanges and provide leverage to 
push third countries to open their markets in the same manner that the EU has opened its 
market in order to ensure a level playing field that the global trade system needs.

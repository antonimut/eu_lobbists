    Feedback from the Korea Business Association Europe                
on the European Critical Raw Materials Act 
The Korea Business Association Europe (KBA Europe), representing over 380 Korean 
companies in Europe, appreciates this opportunity to provide feedback regarding the 
European Commission’s legislative proposal of the  European Critical Raw Materials 
Act (CRMA).  
We welcome  the CRMA as part of the Green Deal Industrial Plan to ensure a 
diversified and sustainable supply of critical raw materials for businesses in the EU. In 
particular, we welcome that the draft CRMA does not include a discriminatory element 
that would unduly affect non-EU companies.  
 
However, below we express our concerns and explain our seven recommendations : 
(i) several benchmarks on extraction, processing, and recycling must be understood 
as indicative figures; (ii) a clear definition of the selection criteria for “Strategic Projects” 
is needed; (iii) Member States must be refrained from making excessive requests to 
companies for the purpose of monitoring; (iv) unnecessary requirements imposed on 
large companies regarding an audit of their supply chains must be removed; (v) 
administrative burden for labeling requirement on permanent magnets must be limited; 
(vi) close cooperation with businesses is required when setting up the minimum shares 
of recycled critical raw materials; and (vii) clear guidance on “environmental footprint 
declaration” is necessary. 
 
1) The benchmarks as indicative figures and gradual increase of benchmarks 
 
Article 1(2)(a) of the draft CRMA provides several benchmarks on extraction, 
processing, and recycling to ensure that EU capacity for strategic raw material supply 
chain significantly increases by 2030. 1 These benchmarks should be understood as 
indicative, not as regulatory requirements imposed on companies or Member States.  
 
If the benchmarks are understood as regulatory requirement s, we are worried that 
companies could potentially face negative impacts when the benchmarks are not met.

Thus, we suggest the Commission: (i) clarify that the benchmarks are merely indicative; 
and (ii) introduce a gradual increase of the benchmarks over time to allow relevant 
stakeholders to adopt them.  
 
2) Clear definition of the selection criteria for “Strategic Projects” 
 
Article 5 of the draft CRMA provides the criteria for being recognized as “Strategic 
Projects”.2 Projects will be selected  based on their contribution to the security of the 
supply of strategic raw materials, their technical feasibility, and sustainability standards. 
Projects in third countries need to mutually benefit the EU and third countries. However, 
some of these criteria are not clearly defined.  
 
First, concerning the sustainability standards (Article 5(1)(c)), we generally agree with 
the need to respect ESG-related international standards and sustainable practices to 
protect human rights and the environment . However, we are concerned that the 
sustainability standards would potentially impose undue burdens on co mpanies, in 
particular for SMEs  who have limited resources , and slow down the recognition as 
“Strategic Projects”.  
 
Thus, we suggest the Commission exclude SMEs from meeting sustainability 
standards and  ensure that  these standards are not abused as trade barriers for 
projects in third countries. In any case, excessive administrative burdens on 
companies must be avoided. 
 
Moreover, concerning the projects in third countries (Article 5(1)(e)), we welcome that 
projects contributing to building strategic raw materials capacities across all value 
chains, both inside and outside the EU, can apply for the status of “Strategic Projects”.  
 
However, the current text only refers to “third countries that are emerging markets or 
developing economies”. I n our view, this limits the geographical scope of “Strategic 
Projects” and rules out projects in like-minded countries that share the same interest 
as the EU and who have advanced technologies. 
 
Accordingly, we propose the Commission expand the geographical scope of third 
countries from developing countries to like -minded countries. In our view, this would 
help the EU to further diversify its supply of critical raw materials.

3) No excessive requests from Member States to companies for monitoring  
 
In order for the Commission to monitor supply risk related to critical raw materials, 
Member States are obliged to collec t certain information from companies. In this 
regard, Article 20(2) of the draft CRMA requires Member States to identify “key market 
operators” along the critical raw materials value chain established in their territory and 
to monitor their activities through surveys. 3 We raise concerns that this requirement 
is far-reaching for the purpose of monitoring and would impose undue burdens on 
companies. In particular, companies could be forced to provide sensitive information 
that includes business secrets. In this regard, the final text should explicitly state that 
each Member State is required to set up its own mechanism to protect any critical or 
confidential information of companies acquired through the monitoring process  from 
leakage. 
 
Further, the definition of “key market operators” is not clear. Although Article 2(28) 
provides the definition” 4, the term “ downstream companies consuming significant 
amounts of critical raw materials” needs to be further clarified regarding: (i) the scope 
of “downstream companies” concerned; and (ii) how to measure “significant amounts” 
of consumption of critical raw materials. The current wording of “key market operators” 
would, in essence, include all companies in value chains of critical raw materials. For 
instance, with this broad definition, automobile or home appliance producers would be 
required to track the supply and demand of critical raw materials from their suppliers.  
 
Thus, we suggest the Commission: (i) limit  the scope of information that  Member 
States could request from companies and allow companies to reject the Member 
States’ request when it concerns business secrets; (ii) obliges Member States to set 
up its mechanism to protect any critical or confidential information of companies;   
and (iii) narrow the scope of “key market operators” with a clear definition.

We also emphasize that other EU legislations (e.g., Corporate Sustainability Due

Therefore, we request the Commission to align the CRMA with other EU legislations 
that impose due diligence obligations and harmonize relevant requirements  and 
definitions of relevant terms. For instance, the definition of “large company” under the 
CRMA6 and the scope of the company subject to the Corporate Sustainability Due 
Diligence Directive 7 would need to be aligned to avoid legal uncertainty and help 
companies understand the key legal term. 
 
Also, the CRMA  refers to large companies “that manufacture strategic technologies 
using strategic raw materials ” and provides several examples of “strategic 
technologies”. With respect to “batteries for e -mobility”, it is unclear whether “large 
companies” include those  producing battery cells and battery pack s, as well as 
automobile manufacturers producing electric vehicles and hydrogen-powered vehicles. 
This risks different interpretations of large companies  “that manufacture strategic 
technologies using strategic raw materials” by different Member States.

6) Close cooperation with businesses when setting up the minimum shares of 
recycled critical raw materials 
 
Article 28(1) of the draft CRMA requires that the share of the recycled content of 
certain critical raw materials in the permanent magnets included in certain products is 
publicly available on a website. 11 Article 28(3) further state s that after 31 December 
2030, the Commission may adopt delegated acts by laying down minimum shares for 
certain recycled critical raw materials that must be present in the permanent magnet 
incorporated in certain products.12

In our view, the use of recycled raw materials will be dependent on the availability of 
high-quality recycled raw materials in sufficient quantities. Since the availability of 
recycled raw materials is now limited to certain types of raw materials, the technical 
feasibility and efficiency must be fully reviewed  before deciding the minimum shares 
of recycled content of critical raw materials in permanent magnets . In this regard, the 
practical ecosystem of recycling must be first established.  
 
Accordingly, we propose the  Commission closely cooperate with businesses when 
setting the minimum shares. 
 
7) Clear guidance on “environmental footprint declaration” 
 
Article 30(1) of the draft CRMA provides that the Commission could adopt delegated 
acts to establish rules for the calculation and verification of the environmental footprint 
declaration of different critical raw materials. Article 30(5) further states that  the 
environmental footprint declaration is not applied to critical raw materials included in 
“intermediate or final products”. 
 
The draft CRAM does not provide the definition of “intermediate or final products” 
which will be excluded from the environmental footprint declaration. The lack of 
definition or guidance makes it difficult for companies to prepare for the environmental 
footprint declaration.  
 
Thus, we suggest the Commission provide a clear definition of “intermediate or final 
products” and guidance on the environmental footprint declaration in due course. 
 
We appreciate your consideration of our comments and would welcome an opportunity 
to discuss this matter with the Commission.

Solar Heat Europe’s contribution to 
the Net-Zero Industry Act 
 
Solar thermal is perfectly positioned to contribute to both climate and security objectives, since 
it counts on a well-established manufacturing base in Europe, providing tens of thousands of jobs 
and supplying over 90% of the domestic demand. As the demand for solar thermal is projected to 
triple by 2030, it is crucial that the European solar thermal industry a lso grows accordingly, 
strengthening its technological leadership and competitiveness domestically and globally.  
Solar Heat Europe ( SHE) is the voice of the European solar thermal industry  since 1992 , with 
members in more than 15 countries representing the whole value chain (from manufacturers to 
service providers). Its activities cover different market segments, such as solar thermal in buildings, 
solar district heating and solar heat for industrial processes. 
SHE welcomes the opportunity to contribute to  the proposal for a Net-Zero Industry Act. As the 
EU accelerates the deployment of net -zero technologies to achieve its climate targets, it is 
imperative to also expand the manufacturing capacity for these technologies in Europe, to support 
industrial competitiveness and avoid creating and/or deepening strategic dependencies that pose 
a threat to our climate goals but also overall security. 
To this end, Solar Heat Europe highlights 5 priorities: 
• Keep the text focused on strategic net-zero technologies, such as solar thermal. 
• Include measures to  maintain EU leadership  in the domestic market for  net-zero 
technologies like solar thermal. 
• Address the needs of SMEs manufacturing net-zero technologies or essential in the value 
chain. 
• Raise the sustainability and resilience contribution in public support schemes to 15%-30% 
and set up effective financing instruments for net-zero manufacturers. 
• Promote synergies across net-zero technologies on the issue of skills.

Keep the text focused on strategic net-zero technologies, such as solar thermal  
As the European Union is legally required to achieve climate neutrality by 2050, it is imperative to 
identify the strategic technologies for the transition a nd accelerate their deployment and 
manufacturing. 
The size of the challenge is enormous and the time at our disposal limi ted. Therefore, the political 
and financial capital available at EU, national, and local level must be wisely used, and the efforts 
concentrated on strategic actions that can make the transition feasible, timely, and cost -effective 
for citizens and businesses.  
The Commission’s proposal to identify a list of strategic net -zero technologies goes in the right 
direction. It is essential that this approach is maintained, and the list of technologies remains 
focused to avoid dispersion. 
Therefore, we propose: 
• Maintain the NZIA focused on the list of strategic net -zero technologies, as proposed by 
the European Commission.  
 
Focus on maintaining EU leadership in net-zero technologies, like solar thermal 
The Commission’s proposal focuses on technologies for which the EU is extremely dependent of 
imports and lacks a strong domestic manufacturing sector . This is evident in the definition of the 
overall benchmark of at least 40 % of the EU's annual deployment needs. Such a target is ambitious 
for certain technologies, but widely inadequate for well-established net-zero technologies like solar 
thermal, where the European industry can already supply approximately 90% of the EU domestic 
demand and is a net-exporter globally. 
The Regulation shall focus on maintaining the existing leadership of European manufacturers in the 
domestic market for net-zero technologies like solar thermal, besides incentivising the emergence 
of new sectors. In fact, the European market for solar heating and cooling is projected to more than 
triple by 2030, as referred in the EU solar Energy Strategy. This means that the EU solar thermal 
industry needs to increase its manufacturing capacity proportionally to maintain the same 
leadership it currently holds in Europe. 
Finally, for the sake of clarity, the overall benchmark shall be calculated as a percentage of the 
European demand, rather than “deployment needs”, for strategic net-zero technologies in 2030. 
Therefore, we propose:

• Include references in the text to the need to maintain the EU leadership in the domestic 
market for those sectors that already hold it, as demand increases  in line with the 
transition to climate neutrality. 
• Include a specific target for solar thermal, reflecting t hat this industry is adequately 
considered in the measures taken at European and national level, and serious efforts are 
made to maintain its leadership in Europe and global competitiveness. 
• Set an overall annual manufacturing benchmark of at least 40% of EU demand , rather 
than deployment needs, for the strategic net-zero technologies listed in the Annex. 
• Introduce an additional target that, in 2030, the Union’s demand for each strategic net -
zero technology listed in the Annex which is supplied by Europe an manufacturers shall 
not be lower than current levels. 
 
Address the needs of SMEs manufacturing net-zero technologies 
SMEs are the backbone of Europe’s economy, providing local jobs and adding to the 
competitiveness of the EU’s net -zero industry. Further more, they are often leading the green 
transition and are instrumental to achieving EU climate objectives, since they play a vital role in the 
manufacturing of many strategic net-zero technologies, as in the case of the European solar thermal 
sector. 
The N et-Zero Industry Act must address the needs of SMEs, not only large corporations. For 
example, in defining strategic net -zero manufacturing projects, the Commission’s proposal states 
they must add significant manufacturing capacity. The concept of “significant” is ambiguous and 
needs clarification to ensure that it also encompasses SMEs. 
Similarly, the draft report prepared by Parliament’s Rapporteur Christian Ehler introduces the 
concept of Net-Zero Industry Valleys, which aim at clustering net-zero industrial activity, increasing 
EU attractiveness for net -zero manufacturing, and further streamlining the administrative 
procedures. These Valleys should cover multiple technologies as well as different segments of the 
value chain and be accessible and open to the specific needs of SMEs.  
Therefore, we propose: 
• Include references in the text on the importance of SMEs and their contribution to EU 
competitiveness in Strategic Net-Zero Technologies. 
• Clarify the meaning of “adding significant manufacturing capacity” in the definition of 
strategic net-zero projects, so that it considers national and regional contexts and allows 
for SMEs to play an active role in the increase of EU’s manufacturing capacity.

Raise the sustainability and resilience contribution in public support schemes to 15%-30% 
Like public procurement and auctions for renewable energy, p ublic support schemes are an 
important driver for the uptake of net-zero technologies, such as solar thermal. The Commission’s 
proposal rightfully introduces the possibility for Member States to reward the contribution in terms 
of sustainability and resilience.  
However, while such contribution could be given a weight between 15% and 30% for public 
procurement procedures and renewable energy auctions, for public support schemes this 
contribution shall not exceed 5% of the cost of the net -zero technology final product for the 
consumer. This penalizes households and consumers, that shall be rewarded and incentivized to 
purchase high-quality products with positive impacts in terms of sustainability and resilience. 
At the same time, interest Interest rates play a major role in energy economics.  Therefore, EU 
manufacturers of strategic net -zero technologies that contributes to Europe’s sustainability and 
resilience could benefit even more from de-risking instruments. Addressing the access, costs and 
timings of the financing processes will be critical to accelerate investment in net-zero value chains. 
Initiatives such as guaranteed interest rate s would also have two additional advantages: first, it 
would eliminate the need for complex support programmes; second, the intensity of public 
interventions would reduce over time, as net-zero projects grow in number and the premium asked 
by investors reduces. 
Therefore, we propose: 
• Include similar conditions for public support schemes as proposed for auctions and public 
procurement and raise the sustainability and resilience contribution  between 15% and 
30%. 
• Set up effective financing instruments for net -zero manufacturers, such as guaranteed 
interest rates  for the manufacturing and early deployment of strategic net -zero 
technologies. 
 
Promote synergies across net-zero technologies on the issue of skills 
The lack of sufficient skilled workforce at multiple levels already represents a bottleneck to the 
rapid deployment of renewable energy and strategic net -zero technologies. The European Skills 
Academies are an interesting proposal, though they need to consider the national and regional 
specificities, such as climate or geological conditions, characteristics of the building stock, skills 
gaps, etc. 
At the same time, setting up specialised Academies per each technology risks missing synergies 
between technologies. For instance, an installer qualified to deal with solar thermal can be trained 
in the additional skills necessary to also install PV or heat pumps. For residential applications, it is 
already possible to find installers that deal with different technologies, which brings versatility and

EDF’s feedback on the Net Zero Industry Act 
 
GENERAL COMMENTS 
 
EDF welcomes the attention given by the European institutions to the subject of 
reindustrialization. As such, the proposal for  a Regulation on  a Net Zero Industry Act (NZIA) will 
facilitate the transition to low-carbon energy. In the context of the IRA and of a broader global clean-
tech race, a European answer is needed. Part of a larger effort for the EU industrial competitiveness, 
namely the Green Deal Industrial Plan, the NZIA will contribute to maintain the EU afloat. It seeks a 
double objective of strengthening the EU energetic independence and ensuring the sustainable 
transition.  
We know that the response to the IRA cannot be symmetrical . That is wh y we support the idea 
that the EU can and should, at the very least, reduce the administrative burden on companies. Indeed, 
the EDF Group develops and operates a range of strategic net-zero technologies and supports all the 
initiatives proposed by the Euro pean Commission aiming at accelerating the deployment of 
renewable and low -carbon technologies , in particular by reducing the red tape , the duration of 
permit-granting process and facilitate access to stable demand for net-zero technologies.  
 
Overall, a strong case needs to be made for the inclusion in the net-zero technologies of all low-
carbon technologies currently in use , including nuclear technologies . Each technology in the NZIA 
should be regarded through their contribution to the EU’s cl imate neutrality goal of 2050 and to 
European resilience and energy security.  
 
The inclusion of certain technologies, such as SMRs, is welcome, but the temporality of the text 
raises questions. The NZIA fails to deliver clear timelines. Setting targets fo r 2030 leaves only a very 
short time to develop these value chains and de facto eliminates some of the technologies mentioned 
as they have not reached a technological readiness level of 8 yet . The NZIA, in order to send positive 
signals to investors, must propose a longer-term vision in line with the development of a solid industrial 
policy.  
 
More precisely, we would like to bring the following comments to the attention of the lawmakers 
and stakeholders.

DETAILED RECOMMENDATIONS 
 
1. Respecting technological neutrality: for a true consideration of all technologies 
capable of decarbonizing important parts of the economy 
 
The scope of application of the NZIA is too restricted  in relation to its objectives. Indeed, some low-
carbon technologies, which are recognized as sustainable and are key to achieving the EU's carbon 
neutrality objectives, are not considered "strategic" or are even completely absent from the text, such 
as existing nuclear powe r technologies, even though they embody a strategic European input, 
contributing massively to EU competitiveness, resilience and decarbonization objectives, notably of 
hard-to-abate industries. Indeed, nuclear accounts for the greatest share of the EU’s el ectricity mix 
with a part of 23% of the electricity produced including 50% of low -carbon electricity. It is therefore 
the first source of low-carbon energy in the EU. The European nuclear industry is an important provider 
of jobs and should be included broadly in the text also regarding the measures on skills and Net Zero 
Academies. Indeed, a qualified workforce will be needed in the years to come.  Just for its 6 planned 
reactors, France will need to recruit 100  000 people.  It is therefore essential to support and 
strengthen the segments of the supply chain in order to maintain the leading position of Europe  in 
nuclear power technologies. 
The NZIA does aim to develop a European value chain in technologies for which the EU is dependent 
on third countries (such as PV, which is largely dominated by China), but the EU must also ensure that 
technologies for which the European value chain is strong remain so.  
- This is the case for hydropower which relies on an essentially European v alue chain (nearly 
90%), hence the interest in maintaining, or even reinforcing this degree of autonomy, in a 
reflection on energy and European sovereignty. Companies, suppliers and subcontractors, 
must be given a clear signal to further develop and modernize their production sites in Europe, 
in order to increase their capacities for the transition to low -carbon energy, in which 
hydropower, and in particular STEP storage, has a major role to play. 
- Regarding nuclear, 43 out 59 nuclear reactors in construction in the world today comes from 
Chinese or Russian technology. The Russian and Chinese industries are expanding their 
capacity and capabilities. If nothing is done to support this industry, operators might t urn to 
extra-European suppliers, and the EU might face in a decade the same level of dependency for 
the other low-carbon technologies. 
- France holds 4 of the 11 European wind power plants, and Europe keeps a leading position in 
wind power. However, this sec tor is beginning to suffer from foreign competition,  it is now 
necessary to protect it, by taking appropriate measures and by maintaining a dynamic 
development of the national and European markets. The EU could strengthen its share of 
added value in wind energy provided by grand financial support and appropriate instruments 
(regulatory framework, simplified procedures, attracting investments, developing the share of 
technical and scientific training…) 
 
It is therefore necessary to ensure that the NZIA includes technologies for which Europe is not 
currently in a situation of excessive dependence, in order to avoid the creation of future 
dependencies.

Suggested changes:  
• Include all nuclear technologies (including related best-in-class fuels), hydropower, renewable 
and low-carbon fuels in the list of Net-Zero technologies 
• Add renewable and low -carbon hydrogen, sustainable alternative fuels, nuclear and 
hydropower technologies to the Annex 
 
2. Expand the scope of eligible tools for innovative technologies 
 
We welcome the inclusion in the NZIA of innovative technologies that will contribute to European 
competitiveness in the clean technology sectors, in the medium or long term. As such, their eligibility 
for regulatory sandboxes is relevant to accelerate their development. 
However, these sandboxes are only one piece of the puzzle. Innovative technologies need to benefit 
from more of the measures developed in the NZIA if the EU is to contribute to their quantitative and 
qualitative development and allow their scaling-up as quickly as possible. Indeed, any new technology 
under development also requires streamlined administrative processes to facilitate permitting, 
better access to information and financing, public procurement measures, and feasibility studies . 
Eligibility for such measures does not jeopardize experimentation in a controlled environment but 
facilitates the process for project developers. The signals sent to the investment will therefore be 
reinforced.  
It is especially of  upmost importance that the permitting is faster for innovative technologies. For 
example, if EU wants the European SMRs to be operational in the 2030’s, their construction must start 
around 2025. The Regulation should therefore help the implementation of manufacturing plants as 
quickly as possible to match these deadlines.  
Moreover, the regulatory sandboxes will be specified by implementing acts and a “Guidance for 
Sandboxes” document in 2023, which currently makes their impact difficult to outline in the absence 
of such documents. The Commission wishes to ensure a level pla ying field for innovation, but the 
regulatory sandboxes remain at the initiative of the Member States (article 26, §1). It therefore risks 
being based on different commitments from one Member State to another. Broadening the measures 
open to these innovative technologies will make it possible to close any potential gaps between 
Member States in the consideration of these regulatory sandboxes. In so doing, it will guarantee them 
more development opportunities and a better level playing field. 
 
Suggested change:  
• Allow the eligibility of innovative technologies to all articles applying to Net -Zero 
manufacturing projects 
 
 
3. Strengthen the resilience criterion 
 
The NZIA aims to support the location in the European Union of strategic value chains for 
decarbonization. In this respect, the provisions aimed at security of supply and resilience must be 
strengthened. This is for example the case of Articles 19 and 20 on public procurement or auctions on 
renewables. The introduction of non -price criteria for renewable tenders and the continued 
recognition of public interest status for SNZT and emergency treatment in court proceedings, appeals 
processing and licensing are highly satisfactory to EDF. However, within these articles appears a 10%

derogation to the use of non-financial criteria: this measure must be eliminated, otherwise the non-
tariff criteria will ultimately be of little use. Indeed, overcosts exceeding by more than 10% costs of 
non-EU projects could be frequent, thus leading many investors to prefer non-EU suppliers. 
EDF is in favor of non -price criteria and consider that they should be more explicit . Firstly  with 
qualification criteria that would allow to select only those candidates who offer guarantees that they 
will respect the essential values of the EU. Secondly, with a criterion that favors local manufacturing, 
at least in part, of components. 
 
Suggested changes:  
• Remove the 10% derogation to the use of non-financial criteria  
• Modify the figures on ponderation of non -tariff criteria (15 to 30%)  to allow this cap to be 
exceeded 
• Add a prequalification criterion on cybersecurity and due diligence 
• Require that a substantial part of product or component is produced in Europe 
 
4. Have a more focused approach on entire value chains than on technologies 
 
It is necessary to support the European existing technologies and supply chains to achieve the very 
ambitious 2030 goals set by Fit for 55, RePower EU and the revision of RED in terms of new capacities. 
For instance, 592 GW of solar PV capacity and 510 GW of wind capacity are required by 2030, which 
requires average annual additions of 48 GW for solar PV and 36 GW for wind. At the same time, there 
is a real industrial tension due to pressure from the United States and China : manufacturers are 
suffering from this competition and from the race to be competitive in calls for tender, which is pushing 
them to drastically lower their prices. Thus, it is urgent to support, reinforce and facilitate the 
implementation of existing technologies and supply chains, and to scale them up.  
 
The text essentially targets the development of "manufacturing projects", i.e. component factories. 
This is obviously essential for the development of the industrial European sovereignty, but it is not 
enough: we need to consider the entire value chain, the final products and identify the key 
components for each clean technology  to meet the ambitions of a domestic integrated value chain. 
Indeed, the production of some of the components is an area where the EU is lagging behind. For 
example, on the PV supply chain, China is overrepresented  its production represents 97% of market 
share for wafers manufacturing capacity and 70% of cells and modules.  
 
The same value chain approach applies for permitting: this should involve both technology installation 
projects and the industries that produce these technologies.  
 
Suggested changes:  
• Clarify and expand the scope of the supply chain for each technology, by listing the 
components needed for wind energy production such as nacelles, towers, generators, cables, 
and blades for wind turbines.

Anglo American’s position on the European Critical Raw Materials 
Act 
 
Anglo American is  a leading global mining company, and our products are the essential 
ingredients in almost every aspect of modern life. Our portfolio of world -class competitive 
operations, with a broad range of future development options, provides many of the future -
enabling metals and minerals for a cleaner, greener, more sustainable world and that meet the 
fast growing every day demands of billions of consumers.  
 
As a responsible producer of diamonds (through De Beers), copper, platinum group metals, 
premium quality iron ore and steelmaking coal, and nickel – with crop nutrients in development – 
we are committed to being carbon neutral across our operations by 2040. More broadly, our 
Sustainable Mining Plan commits us to a series of stretching goals to ensure we work towards a 
healthy environment, creating thriving communities and building trust as a corporate leader. We 
work together with our business partners and diverse stakeholders to unlock enduring value from 
precious natural resources for the benefit of the communities and countries in which we operate, 
for society as a whole, and for our shareholders.  
 
Introduction 
Anglo American broadly welcomes the Critical Raw Materials Act (“CRMA”) and its underlying 
goal of reducing strategic dependencies by improving resilience of critical raw materials (“CRM”) 
supply chains, an essential enabler of the EU’s twin green and digital transitions.  
 
The global human population – almost nine-billion people – cannot be supported without mining. 
Despite mining’s contribution to almost every aspect of modern life, the industry is still seen as 
one “that takes more than it gives”. It supports/enables our modern lifestyle, in particular amongst 
others, food production, construction materials, mobility and transport, clean energy and 
communication solutions – ~45% of the world’s economic activity, directly or indirectly. All this 
while disturbing only a small fraction of the earth’s surface (0.04% of global habitable land). And 
the sector is critical to building a cleaner, greener, more sustainable world.  
 
Against this backdrop we welcome the CRMA as a concrete proposal to address some of the 
supply issues, b ut we also importantly see it as an opportunity to initiate a broader societal 
discourse about responsible mining as a critical industrial activity, as well as the role of its 
products – minerals and metals – and how they relate to everyone’s standard of living. 
 
Below are Anglo American’s initial comments on the CRMA:

As the global population grows (at least for the next four decades; current rate c.70 million per 
year) and as economies develop, so the need for food supply and infrastructure (e.g. housing 
and transport) grows, resulting in higher demand for other materials, such as steel and fertilizer.

In the proposal’s annex, the Commission distinguishes between CRMs and strategic raw 
materials. To preserve flexibility during  rapid advances in clean technology and unexpected 
changes to geopolitical circumstances, we would suggest that the Commission not limit strategic 
projects to a pr edefined list of minerals. Regardless, to accomplish green transition goals, the 
Commission must review any list of raw materials more frequently than currently suggested in 
Article 4 to ensure that it reflects changes in geopolitics and technologies. 
 
Strategic Projects 
The Commission’s proposal to grant 'Strategic Project' status to qualified mining and processing 
projects, which can then benefit from streamlined permitting and enabling conditions for access 
to finance, is very significant, in particular the notion of these projects being prioritised as they 
are in the public interest.  
 
Anglo American supports the proposed permitting deadlines, provided the environmental  and 
social safeguards are not compromised  – as any s uch compromise would risk eroding trust in 
and support for mining , and it is imperative to avoid all administrative capacity focusing on 
strategic projects, and thus extending lead and permitting times for other projects.  Therefore, 
significant capacity building will be required – both in terms of administrations’ expertise and their 
ability to deal with the number of applications that will be submitted. 
 
Considering the 10% target for mining, this means up to 90% of mined product will still come from 
third countries located outside of the EU. This aspect requires a lot more attention and detail in 
approach than is currently provided in the CRMA. Anglo Amer ican also suggests that talks with 
third countries regarding strategic partnerships and/or to identify Strategic Projects should involve 
mining countries and financing institutions/investors at an early stage , to guarantee an ali gned 
approach and avoid unrealistic expectations in terms of project delivery. 
 
Permitting processes

Anglo American welcomes the ambition to achieve more efficient and shorter authorization 
processes for projects and supports the setting of time frames for the length of  authorization 
processes. Slow permitting is a major bottleneck to the progress of European projects today, as 
is also the case in many other regions, with new mines taking 10 -20 years to come online. We 
support the Commission’s proposals for a one -stop shop, permitti ng deadlines, and other 
streamlining measures.  
 
However, Anglo American expresses concern at the capacity of the competent authorities in 
charge of land use and planning. The CRMA needs to address this issue, as without increased 
capacity, there is no realistic possibility to streamline the permitting process.  In addition, Anglo 
American would welcome more information on how to address issues of delays during the public 
consultation period.  
 
Anglo American expresses concern that some projects, which have delivered the required 
documentation, are subsequently delayed due to requests for further or new documents after the 
given timeframe. To avoid that, Anglo American recommends that if the appropriate technical 
documentation is submitted by the applicant to the competent authority, that authority then has 
three months to confirm the documentation is complete and will be processed or otherwise 
request the applicant completes or corrects the documentation. Beyond that three month period, 
the competent authority should no longer be able to request new documents.  
 
Anglo American believes that there is a need for alignment between national governments and 
the competent permitting authorities to avoid situations where competing interests ri sk slowing 
down granting processes. Anglo American can provide several examples of where this has been 
done elsewhere in the world, and the lessons learned: 
 
• South Africa: The office of the Presidency, through the Infrastructure South Africa (“ISA 
Office”), allows companies to register strategic projects that would then get accelerated 
permitting approvals. This has been in existence for about three years and appears to 
be functioning well, the main reason being that there is alignment between all relevant 
bodies on the specific criteria for a strategic project, so there is less tension between 
departments.  
 
• In Chile, there is the Sustainability Projects Office (Gestion Proyectos Sustenibles – 
“GPS”) linked to the Ministry of Economy , which was supposed to coordinate amongst 
the relevant ministries to prioritise the permitting of strategic projects . This has been 
operating with less success, in part because mining is not seen as a priority across the 
various administrations, unlike in  South Africa, and because there is little alignment 
between the departments on the role of the GPS, and therefore companies’ use of it has 
generated some tension.  
 
• In Brazil, the Environmental Agency in the state of Minas Gerais created a team to 
prioritize approvals for strategic projects. Initially this worked well ; however at a certain 
point there were so many projects classified as ‘strategic’ that the team w as unable to 
keep up with the accelerated approval timeline and there were inevitable delays.

From these examples, it is clear that the idea of accelerated permitting for strategic projects does 
work, provided that: 
 
• There is a clear understanding across all departments around the remit of the strategic 
projects’ office and the criteria for these projects, to avoid creating unwanted tension and 
overloading the strategic projects office. 
• That companies use the strategic projects office appropriately, ensuring other relevant 
authorities are also aware.  
 
Exploration  
Anglo American welcomes the CRMA’s emphasis on the importance of exploration to secure the 
future supply of critical and strategic raw materials and supports its suggestion that each Member 
State should develop a national programme for exploration, including geological mapping. 
 
As the Act aims to strengthen all stages of the European critical raw materials value chain, we 
would like to see more detail on how these national programmes collecting geological data will 
translate into incentivising exploration activities to ensure a s ufficient pipeline leading to the 
(strategic) projects that will enable the EU to meet the targets outlined in the Act. 
 
As mentioned previously, these national programmes, as well as dealing with a much needed 
increase in actual exploration activity, need to also focus on building capacity – both in terms of 
quantity and expertise – at the relevant regulatory and administrative bodies.  
 
It is also of great importance not to limit exploration efforts to only those raw materials that are 
critical or strategic today. Most of these occur together with "carrier metals", also known as main 
metals, such as copper, zinc, iron and gold. The greatest economic value lies in the extraction of 
the main metals, which is why they are usually the primary focus of exploration. Extracting critical 
metals as by-products is not only resource efficient, but production is also less sensitive to market 
manipulation and unstable markets often associated with CRM. 
 
Standards  
Anglo American does not believe that developing a new European mining standard is necessary 
or helpful, as there are plenty of existing certification schemes . It is instead important to specify 
the criteria for standards/certification schemes in a way that they support the goal of safeguarding 
and ensuring high standards of environmental and social performance of mining projects . 
Therefore, the criteria should include, for example, that they need to have been developed by a 
multi-stakeholder initiative and build on international best practice. 
 
We further would suggest that such standards should apply to mining activities in general rather 
than just Strategic Projects. Finally, the re are examples of strict standards that can and have 
been applied to responsible mining projects/activities in third countries. Anglo American itself has 
assured mines against internationally respected and renowned standards such as IRMA 
(Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance) or Copper Mark and is happy to share those 
examples.

Financing  
We welcome the Commission’s point in the communication accompanying the CRMA, where it 
notes the impact of tariffs on the ability to import CRMs and we will be closely following this. We 
would welcome further examination of  duty suspensions, in light of the objectives of facilitating 
the diversification of critical raw materials and reducing our dependencies. Although import tariffs 
are set at zero for 92% of raw materials (exceptions include magnesium, processed gallium and 
aluminium) we would welcome additional details on any planned work the EU may undertake to 
reassess import duties where applicable.   
 
The CRMA does not include any new EU funding sources, in comparison to the major incentive 
programmes from the US, China and Japan. To ensure this gap is addressed soon, the text of 
the regulation should make a clear link to the upcoming discussions amongst the European 
Commission and Member States on a sovereignty fund or other new financing vehicle. 
 
Beyond funding incentives, we would welcome and encourage the Commission to ensure that a 
broader programme on grants and incentives is available to encourage competitiveness with 
programmes in other jurisdictions, in particular the Inflationary Reduction Act in the US.   
 
Public acceptance 
In general, Europe is no longer a region with a mining identity and over the last few decades has 
chosen to import the majority of products produced by heavy industry rather than maintaining or 
building its own industrial activity.  
 
Neither the case for mining itself nor the ability for government to effectively regulate mining has 
been made to the public at large. Broad based societal opposition towards mining has increased 
over recent decades and the ability to permit new mines is has become steadily more difficult. 
The CRMA should therefore also ensure that mining projects benefit local communities and , 
equally importantly, seek to raise public awareness about these benefits.  
 
The Commission’s Energy Communities initiatives could be a model to build on. It is both  
necessary and important that industry and policymakers raise awareness among the general 
public that the green transition requires minerals and metals, and that the se can be responsibly 
sourced given today’s modern mining methods. To enable the transition and European citizens’ 
way of life and living standards, we will need to source these minerals and metals both from 
within and outside of Europe. 
 
Setting high standards will be essential for improving public acceptance of the sector, and  
standards developed and governed by multi -stakeholder initiatives will grant strong legitimacy 
and could play an important role in mediating between affected stakeholder groups in the case 
of disagreement.  
 
CRM Board 
In order to ensure that the actions taken by the CRM Board are well aligned with the capabilities 
and expectations of industry, it is important that the Board consults relevant private sector actors 
in a formalised manner. This could be through an industry sub-committee that provides input on 
all matters of strategic and operational importance, but is excluded from the identification of 
strategic projects, to avoid any potential conflicts of interest.

There are some potential coherence issues between how the CRM interacts with other Green 
Deal legislation and the policies of other international government bodies and EU legislatio n 
which need clarification.  For example:   
 
• The OECD Pillar 1 and 2.  Has consideration been given to the impact the CRMA may 
have on multinationals which fall within Pillar 1 or 2 of the OECD’s BEPS Project?  The 
ability for large multinationals to benefit from the CRMA and not be subject to additional 
taxation under Pillar 1 and 2 may be dependent on matters such as where processing 
occurs (Pillar 1 mining exemption) and/or the structure of funding programs (Pillar 2 
minimum tax requirements).    
 
• The EU’s Foreign Subsidies Regulation (“FSR”), introduced this year and applicable from 
12 July 2023, effectively seeks to exten d the EU State Aid rules outside the EU, to 
address “subsidies” granted by non-EU countries. Under the FSR, the Commission can 
exclude non -EU companies from engaging in M&A deals or public procurements, in 
cases where the companies involved, or members of their group, have received financial 
contributions from non-EU countries.  How might this regulation impact companies’ ability 
to benefit from the CRMA? 
 
 
Conclusion  
The EU Critical Raw Materials Act is a critical part of the Green Deal Industrial Plan, a s CRMs 
are the essential components of the green and digital transitions. If implemented, these 
recommendations would assist companies advancing clean technology in bolstering the EU’s 
global competitiveness and fight against climate change.  
 
In addition to this submission, Anglo American has participated in the submission processes of 
Eurometaux, Euromines, Hydrogen Europe and the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance 
(IRMA). Anglo American remains open to engage on any of the above points and looks forward 
to this important piece of legislation progressing.

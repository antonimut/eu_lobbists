  
Feedback to the proposal for a Regulation on establishing a framework 
of measures for strengthening Europe’s net-zero technology products 
manufacturing ecosystem (Net Zero Industry Act) 
 
27 June 2023 
 
General considerations 
Overall, we welcome the EU’s efforts to secure and develop manufacturing capabilities within 
the European space, key to achieve not only net zero targets but also build a more robust and 
self-sufficient economy. 
However, while an important first step to boost EU’s competitiveness, growth and energy 
transition ambitions, the proposal is perceived more as a “list of intentions”, with no clear 
guidance on how Member States will enforce these measures (e.g., how to increase 
administrative capacity or further accelerate its digitization) or the penalties in failing to do so. 
It maintains a focus on regulation (or its flexibility) instead of a direct support to net-zero 
technology manufacturing or production. 
Linked to the above, the proposal does not unlock additional funds or other financial incentives 
such as tax benefits, but rather (re)allocates existing funds. Consequently, and contrary to the 
US Inflation Reduction Act, this proposal fails to give clarity on which amounts will be allocated 
to each technology and the respective application criteria, key to drive investment decisions. 
Also, in addition to more ambitious goals and increased effort to achieve the proposed targets, 
it is also essential to ensure transparency and traceability of some of the proposed measures. 
We believe this will be an important aspect of the overall proposal to ensure the positive 
benefits intended. 
Finally, the efficacy of the current proposal is subject to its transposition to each Member State 
and the overall adequacy and alignment with the regulatory framework currently in place. 
 
2030 Targets 
40% share of EU manufactured deployment needs for strategic net-zero 
technologies 
While the level of ambition is deemed appropriate, it is important to clarify the target itself, how 
it should be enforced and the implications for member states given existing limitations (e.g., 
manufacturing capabilities, administrative setup). 
The document should be clear on whether the 40% target is to be applied at each technology 
level or as an overall ambition for the Union (e.g., greater clarity required on article 1., 2(a)). As 
the document recognizes, for some technologies this target may be quite ambitious given the 
EU’s currently low manufacturing capability, reinforcing the need to clarify if and which 
technologies may be exempted from this target at an individual level and, under that case, which 
minimum targets may apply.

Injection capacity in strategic CO2 storage sites (50mtpa) 
As mentioned in the proposal, the Union may need to capture up to 550 million tons of CO2 
annually by 2050 to meet the net zero objective. Moreover, the European Clean Energy 
Industrial Forum has estimated demand for annual storage services in the European Economic 
Area to grow to 80 million tonnes of CO2 in 2030. 
Considering the above, the 50 mtpa injection target may be perceived as adequate if the above 
80 mtpa target considers non-EU countries (e.g., UK and Norway). 
 
List of Strategic Net-Zero Technologies 
The list of net zero technologies fails to include critical low carbon technologies which address 
two out of the three key criteria within this proposal, namely, contribution to decarbonization 
and competitiveness and security of supply risks. It also fails to tackle the full length of each 
individual supply chain – from production/manufacturing to its respective infrastructure and 
implementation requirements. Namely: 
 RFNBOs, biofuels and SAFs: key to achieve the transportation decarbonization targets, 
as defined under the FF55 and REDIII proposals. Furthermore, on biofuels, highly 
dependent on an already stretched feedstock sourcing, further technology 
development and incentives to prioritize European producers as feedstock destinations 
could reduce feedstock dependency and availability concerns, representing a significant 
advantage for the European biofuels industry. On the replacement of natural gas, the 
proposal fails to mention incentives or measures to retrofit existing natural gas pipelines 
to enable alternative gaseous fuels (e.g., biogas, hydrogen). 
 EVs: despite the fast-paced deployment of an electric mobility network, the proposal 
fails to mention the development of manufacturing capacity and investment allocation 
within Europe to continuously improve the efficiency and size of charging networks. 
 Carbon transport technologies, namely transportation by vessels, will be key to 
guarantee access to storage sites in the medium term. Despite being in early stages of 
development the proposal misses technologies related to carbon utilization, perceived 
key and complementary to carbon capture & storage. 
 
Net Zero Strategic Projects 
Eligibility criteria – Manufacturing 
Net zero manufacturing projects criteria is deemed as appropriate as it covers planned and 
existing projects and safeguards the development of manufacturing capacity, reduction of 
supply chain risks, overall EU competitiveness and job creation. 
However, the following aspects should be further fine-tuned, mostly anchored on setting a more 
quantitative vs. qualitative based criteria: 
  
 Section II, art 10, 1(a) criteria should not be restricted to projects based on the number 
of third countries it relies on along its value chain (criteria currently restricted to one 
country), but on the overall weight of third country dependence (whether one or more). 
 Section II, art 10, 2(b) the selection of three of the four criteria is very vague and should 
rather be assessed on the materiality of each criterion. In this sense, if one criterion is, 
by itself highly material, it should enable the project to be eligible. 
 Section II, art 10, 2(b) should further add criteria in support of securing a Just Transition. 
 
Eligibility criteria - CO2 storage 
Net Zero CO2 storage projects criteria is perceived as appropriate, provided the permit 
requirement for the geological storage of CO2 can be requested at any time and not necessarily 
prior to the publication of the proposal (section II, art. 10, 2c). This aims to include new storage 
sites already identified within the scope of eligible projects but that have yet to apply for said 
permit. 
 
Permitting 
Permit granting agility is critical as one of the key bottlenecks for projects’ deployment, with the 
proposed timings marking a much-welcomed improvement vs. current timings. However, key 
concerns arise on each Member State ability to setup the necessary conditions to speed up the 
permit granting process (e.g., qualified staff, financial, technical, and technological resources) as 
mentioned under chapter II, section I, art. 4(7). 
Likewise, the designation of a single national authority to facilitate and coordinate the process 
as well as the enforcement of an automatic approval mechanism is also positively regarded, also 
as it applies to both net zero technology manufacturing projects and net zero strategic projects. 
On the automatic approval mechanism, it’s important to highlight that every permitting process 
and proposed new project should be framed and evaluated also according to Environment 
Assessment, Climate Risk, Taxonomy and ESG criteria. Should the approval delay be due to one 
of these criteria then it should not be automatically approved until all criteria is met. 
Finally, it’s important to also set up a maximum length for the assessment of an Environmental 
Impact Assessment for net zero strategic projects, as defined for net zero technology 
manufacturing projects (chapter II, art. 7, 30 days since the submission of the project by its 
promoter). 
 
CO2 injection capacity 
On Chapter III – CO2 injection capacity, the following should be considered: 
 Art. 16: The overall 50 mtpa target falls short of the estimated storage demand of 80 
mtpa by 2050, as estimated by the European Clean Energy Industrial Forum. 
 Art. 17: While it is clear that information on storage projects to date need to be made 
public, it is questionable whether the same should be applied to information collected 
from now onwards on storage development projects. Since having an exploration 
  
license does not guarantee a future storage license, making than information public may 
condition the project’s competitiveness. 
 Art. 17, 2(a): Unclear what constitutes a CO2 capture project in progress (e.g., maturity 
stage) subject to mandatory communication. 
 Art. 18: The proposal seems to set the contribution of oil & gas production to the 
injection targets based on its share within the EU from 2020 to 2023, assuming that 
current exploration sites will be depleted by 2030 and can serve as storage sites. 
However, this assumption is not clear, and requires a firm understanding between 
operating companies and local governments to transform these locations into carbon 
storage sites. Likewise, it is unclear whether it will consider new storage sites for the 
attainment of this target, or it will be seen as an additional contribution. 
The efficacy of this proposal requires a clear alignment of the regulatory framework in each 
Member State to enable carbon storage. Even though the Directive 2009/31/EC should have 
been transposed to each state member legislation, a comprehensive and holistic approach must 
be guaranteed to overcome potential current gaps and lack of consistency with other regulation 
in place (e.g., permits to use national maritime space). 
Finally, the proposal does not fully cover the CCUS value chain. While it covers carbon capture 
and storage, if fails to cover transport to designated storage sites. This omission may contribute 
to the disparity on access conditions of the different state members to the storage sites. 
 
Net-zero regulatory sandboxes 
Net zero regulatory sandbox proposal is perceived as adequate. To be highlighted the 
importance of ensuring regulatory stability and innovation (as stated under chapter VI, art. 26, 
3), and cooperation and information sharing across member states (as stated under chapter VI, 
art. 26, 8). While exemptions are welcomed, important to also safeguard competitiveness as 
under the EU law. 
 
Skills (European Net-Zero Industry Academies) 
Further clarity is required on how European Net Zero Industry Academies will be setup, how 
cross border coordination will be ensured (e.g., on the structure of learning programs and 
content/materials) and on the eligibility criteria to access EU funding.  
On the latter, it’s important to prioritize the setup of these academies in areas more impacted 
by the energy transition with higher demands for reskilling and upskilling of workers, 
guaranteeing a just transition. 
Also on funding, important to understand if the 3Mn€ allocated from the budget of the Clean 
Hydrogen Joint Undertaking will constraint the scope of the program (e.g., towards a hydrogen 
related focus or broader scope).

1. Executive summary 
Nucleareurope fully supports the 2050 climate neutrality target set by the European Climate 
Law Regulation1 in 2021 and the other initiatives  such as the Net -Zero Industry Act (NZIA) 
which aim at helping to achieve this goal . As a major industrial sector, nucleareurope and its 
members are fully committed to making the energy transition happen and we believe tha t 
electrification is the cornerstone of a decarbonised energy system. In order to ensure the 
resilience of its energy system, the EU will need to maintain and develop sufficient low-carbon 
electricity production capacity  for which significant manufacturing  capacity needs to be 
implemented. In addition, the possibility of using nuclear to produce heat for industrial 
processes and district heating purposes should be taken into consideration.  
Support for maintaining this European value chain is essential to allow the nuclear 
industry to deliver what is needed to reach Net Zero by 2050 , and the ambitions set in the 
Nuclear Alliance statement2 in May 2023. 
In order for this to happen, the nuclear sector must be considered as a whole and not 
limited to only a number of technologies. 
2. Overall context 
In December 2019, EU leaders, meeting within the European Council, agreed that the EU 
should achieve climate-neutrality by 2050.Becoming climate-neutral means that, by 2050, EU 
countries will have to drastically reduce their greenhouse gas emissions and find ways of 
compensating for the remaining and unavoidable emissions to reach a  net-zero emissions 
balance. 
In its conclusions, the Europe an Council underlined that the transition to climate neutrality 
brings significant opportunities for: 
• economic growth 
• markets and jobs 
• technological development 
After this agreement, the work started on the European Green Deal and an ambitious plan to 
decarbonise EU’s economy was put forward3 by EC – see below the plans to achieve climate-
neutrality by 2050.

As can be seen,  a lot of pressure is on power sector, seen as one the first to be decarbonized 
by 2040. In this context, it’s clear that all the electricity sources will be needed to achieve such 
an ambitious target including nuclear technology. 
Nuclear technologies are key to significantly decarbonizing European economy, in 
particular hard-to-abate industries (e.g. mining, steel manufacturing, chemicals, aviation) as 
they provide large volumes of low -carbon electricity, and potentially also heat, steam and 
hydrogen. The EU masters all techno logies in the fuel cycle and ensuring sovereignty over  
all these applications also means scaling up European production of the nuclear fuel 
supply. 
In addition to  existing large reactors, Small and Advanced Modular Reactors (SMR and 
AMR) technologies are d eveloping rapidly, and all are expected to make a significant 
contribution to the European strategic objectives. 
The value brought by nuclear extends beyond energy. For example, the  European nuclear 
sector is an important player in the medical field providing essential diagnosis and treatment 
technologies to fight cancer. 
And as part of the energy trilemma, the future policy files would need to tackle also the 
affordability and security of supply matters, on top of the decarbonization one, and nuclear 
sector can have also an important contributions to lower the energy price and for achieving 
EU energy independence. 
 
3. Current status of EU nuclear industry 
Not only does nuclear account for the greatest share of the EU’s electricity mix (currently 
around 23% of all electricity produced and 50% of low carbon electricity), but the European 
nuclear sector is composed of a highly skilled manufacturing capacity based in the EU. The

3.1 The existing nuclear supply chain 
As mentioned above, a very strong EU based nuclear supply chain has been established 
starting with 70s. Many countries deploying that time nuclear reactors, strengthen the 
capabilities of the local supply chain,  even if sometimes we were talking about non -EU 
reactors designs. For example, Romanian nuclear supply chain was aiming at providing in 
the 90s of over 70% of the components of a reactor design coming from Canada (CANDU-6 
reactors type). Like in other cases, this capacity decreased mainly because of the idle times 
between the reactors deployment. Now, when the discussions are advancing for the 
construction of Cernavoda Units 3 and 4, the supply chain capabilities are less than 40%5. 
Other countries like Fr ance managed to maintain a strong nuclear supply chain but 
developments are needed to support both the massive lifetime extension of the existing

5. Nuclear in the NZIA proposal 
In the EC’s proposal nuclear technologies are only considered to a limited extent: 
• advanced technologies to produce energy from nuclear processes with minimal 
waste from the fuel cycle. 
This point makes reference to so called Generation IV reactors which are still under 
development and are expected to reach the commercial operativity in early 2040s. 
• small modular reactors (SMRs). 
Comparing with the Generation IV reactors, the SMR technology deployment is making 
significant progress, expected to have in operation the first -of-a kind of the different designs 
in the late 2020s Both the advanced reactors (AMR) and SMRs are not yet at Technology 
Readiness Level 8, also a condition to receive support as Net -Zero Technology. However, 
there are strong efforts made by the industry to accelerate research and development in that 
field, as underlined in the European SMR declaration signed on April 4th2 .Currently under 
deployment and also planning for future large reactors deployment is the Generation III/III+ 
reactors, with advanced nuclear safety system in place. These technologies, would be able to 
make immediate effect for the decarbonisation efforts in reaching the Net -Zero target. At the 
previous point were mentioned the challenges faced by the nuclear sector – the supply chain 
and the workforce. For the advanced nuclear technologies and SMRs to benefit from Net-Zero 
Industry Act measures on longer term, a bridge should be created by including these 
measure also for short and medium term, by applying it to the existing nuclear reactors 
design (Generations II and III/III+) . As wind and solar existing technologies are included in 
the scope of the NZIA, existing nuclear technologies should be regarding their contribution to 
climate neutrality and resilience. The EU cannot afford to exclude proven technology to 
reach net zero and this is indeed contrary the prior statements by the Commission. It should 
be also mentioned the reference made several times by the policymakers on the Sustainable 
Finance Taxonomy – an alignment to with this proposal which includes the existing nuclea r 
fleet as well as Generation III/III+ technologies would be needed. Even further, the taxonomy 
is referring to a much longer timeframe – 2040 for existing plants and 2045 for the Generation 
III/III+ , whilst the NZIA horizon is 2030. EU policy needs to be aligned and coherent. 
• related best-in-class fuels 
We should consider at this point the fuels for advanced reactors and SMRs/Advanced 
Modular Reactors (AMRs)  - see point 5.2, above - but also advanced fuels for the existing 
nuclear fleet.

6. Response of NZIA to US-IRA 
5.1 Presentation of the measures proposed by US-IRA on supporting the US nuclear 
sector 
• Support to existing nuclear generators 
$30 billion in production tax credits to existing nuclear power plants from 2024 through 
2032, i.e., approximately $4 bn/annum. 
The zero-emission nuclear power production credit provides up to $15 per megawatt-hour 
for the electricity produced by the plants assuming that labour and wage requirements are 
met. 
This support might be of interest for European nuclear services providers involved, for 
example, in maintenance activities in existing U.S. nuclear power plants. 
• Support to new nuclear energy projects 
The IRA will transition from the current technology-specific tax credits for renewable energy 
into technology-neutral credits that place new nuclear energy projects on a level playing field 
with other zero-carbon generation. 
Producers will be able to choose from a technology-neutral production tax credit of $25 
per megawatt-hour for the first ten years of plant operation or a up to 30% investment 
tax credit (on new zero-carbon power plants placed into operation in 2025 or after. 
These credits can be applied to a single facility but both do include a 10% bonus if the power 
plant is built at a brown field site or a fossil “energy community”—ensuring social and energy 
justice for all Americans. 
These tax credit may be increased by another 10% if a domestic content standard is met 
(construction with U.S steel, U.S. concrete for example). 
• Support to develop availability of fuel needed for advanced reactors 
The IRA provides $600 million to fund the High-assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU) 
program, for use in acquiring HALEU produced through enrichment or obtaining it by 
processing enriched uranium in Department of Energy stockpiles (which likely can supply 
only small quantities at best). It would also provi de $100 million to develop suitable 
transportation capabilities. 
This money must be applied by 2026 and the U.S. DOE plans to use these funds quickly and 
efficiently to address the industry’s most pressing needs. 
IRA also allocates $150 million to US DOE to improve the overall R&D infrastructure at 
DOE’s national labs that could also support these and other nuclear energy research 
capabilities.

7. Recommendations regarding the inclusion of nuclear in NZIA 
Support for maintaining this European value chain is essential to allow the nuclear 
industry to deliver what is needed to reach Net Zero by 2050 , and considering the ambition 
set in the Nuclear Alliance statement. 
In order this to happen, the nuclear sector to be considered as a whole and not only as 
a limited number of technologies. 
Whilst the inclusion of some nuclear technologies in the list of Net -Zero Technologies is a 
step in the right direction, much more could still be achieved by including the nuclear 
sector as a whole and treating nuclear in the same way as other strategic technologies. 
These technologies do not exist as separate and independent entities on their own and are 
interdependent. The whole nuclear sector must be supported in order to make sure that the 
investments are implemented in an efficient manner, similarly how was done in US, supported 
by IRA (see point 5.1).

Box 1. What is HALEU 
HALEU is uranium that has been enriched so that the concentration of the fissile isotope U-
235 is between 5 and 20 percent of the mass of the fuel. This is higher than the 3 to 5 percent 
U-235 concentration, or “assay,” of Low-Enriched Uranium that fuels the existing fleet of light 
water reactors. 
HALEU fue l has many advantages that improve reactor performance. The higher 
concentration of fissile U-235 means the fuel assemblies and reactors can be smaller, and 
reactors don’t need refueling as often, which is why many small modular reactor (SMR) 
and microreactor designs will run on HALEU . HALEU can also reduce the volume of 
waste generated.

THE Critical Raw Materials ACT:  ACTION MODE ON 
The European association for ferro-alloys and silicon industries, Euroalliages, welcomes the European 
Commission’s proposal on Critical Raw Materials. It is a key starting point to prevent shifting our 
industrial base outside Europe. The CRMA will have to constitute a solid legal reference not only to 
support European industry but also to stimulate its production in order to reduce the dependence of 
the European economy on third countries. Its implementation will be a major challenge but necessary 
to ensure a well-functioning Union market and ultimately preserve the processing industry in the EU. 
Euroalliages will primarily focus its comments and the below recommendations on the CRMA although 
the text should be viewed in conjunction with the Net Zero Industry Act. 
1- Adequate legal framework conditions preserving European competitiveness.  
For the past three years, the European industry has faced the hardest conditions jeopardizing their 
existence, from the Covid crisis to the Russian invasion of Ukraine and the related skyrocketing energy 
prices. Until this moment , 40% of the silicon and ferro -alloys furnaces have been idled with the 
corresponding decrease in sales and exports . This dramatic situation is being faced by other energy 
intensive industries. The CRM act, to fulfill its objectives, should focus on preserving the industrial base 
and provide all the support the industry needs to protect the jobs of millions of European citizens, 
their valuable know-how and expertise, and the welfare the industry creates.  
• Affordable energy prices and OPEX support of existing industrial structures 
Without short term measures to significantly mitigate the soaring energy prices , the objectives and 
measures considered by the CRM Act  and the NZI  Act run the risk of having no  effect in putting 
European industry back on track in the global race.   Materials listed under the CRM act should also 
benefit from incentives given under the Net-zero Industrial plan as they are essential elements in the 
deployment of sectors and technologies contributing to the twin transition.  
 
We regret that in response to the US IRA only investment in new projects is considered and not the 
essential need for support to the OPEX of existing industrial structures.  It is essentially these 
industrial structures that will be the basis for deploying the production necessary for decarbonization. 
It must be understood that factories which have curtailed their production or are shut down today 
due to the energy crisis cannot embark on new investments when they are no longer able to cover 
their basic invoices. The CRMA must be the start of a broader Industrial Plan, better recognizing that 
European industry is central to Europe’s future progress and prosperity. Therefore, easy access to 
financing for both CAPEX and OPEX is needed to preserve the existing EU industrial base and facilitate 
its expansion in light of strategic autonomy. 
 
• A level playing field where all actors compete under the same rules. 
If compared to third countries, European producers are not competing on an equal footing – and have 
not done so for a number of years. It is well known that in other regions producers do not sustain the 
same environmental, social and production standards as in the EU. We are very much concerned about 
the following statement from the Commission’s Communication1:” The Commission will follow closely 
the impact of tariffs on the ability to import CRMs and examine requests for duty suspensions, in light 
of the objectives of facilitating the diversification of critical raw materials and re ducing our 
dependencies.”

Any a ttempt to remove anti-dumping or customs duties is unacceptable. The imposition of anti-
dumping duties is a legal response over an illegal practice, fully compatible with WTO law. When anti-
dumping duties are imposed, the level playing field is restored, ensuring fair competition, which is key 
to preserve a European industrial base. However, in some cases, the level of duties imposed in the EU 
remains insufficient. Other countries, such as the US, better capture the reality faced by producers. 
 
Anti-dumping and customs duties are not  barriers to trade, but actions taken by WTO Members to 
trade practices that are not in accordance with GATT 1994 , the Anti -dumping Agreement  and 
ultimately the European Anti-dumping regulation which complies with the first two . The removal of 
anti-dumping and customs duties  will only worsen Europe's dependence on third countries, while 
offering windfall effects to those who denounce them as trade barriers. Trade statistics demonstrate 
that anti-dumping and customs duties do not prevent imports from entering the EU. 
 
• Harmonization and policy coherence across EU regulations  
Euroalliages calls for coherence between the current political goals and the existing regulations under 
revision, e.g., the REACH legislation which could impose barriers or even restrictions to the production 
of critical or strategic materials based on a disputable hazard assessment. All these uncertainties will 
not create business cases to invest or operate in Europe. 
 
2- Tailored and ambitious targets  
It is of paramount importance to support strong resilient value chains while they still exist in Europe, 
and not portions of them, hence creating dependency. Considering that the list of strategic raw 
materials is not exhaustive, tailored targets per strat egic raw materials should be discussed with 
concerned industry stakeholders along the stages of the value chain. A global target covering all raw 
materials can risk putting the focus on several potential candidates leaving the rest with their current 
capacities. A differentiation between raw materials with existing domestic production and others with 
no current production should lead the way for a constructive methodology for target setting.  
For instance, the E EA silicon industry supplied 6 5% of European d emand in 202 2. A realistic target 
would be to maintain this value and anticipate an increase of European production and capacities via 
strategic projects by X X% in 2030.  Silicon producers in the EU, Norway and Iceland have a current 
total capacity of 442 kt and can easily meet the extra demand growth of 158kt by 20302, presuming 
that the adequate legal framework conditions are in place.  
The differentiated target applies to all steps of the value chain. In terms of recycling silicon metal is not 
recycled in significant amounts in  semiconductors or solar applications, but it is recycled in the 
aluminium recycling streams . The  15% target for recycling  should reflect the reality of each raw 
material. Targets should be assessed every couple of years, giving the time for projects to be accepted 
and implemented.  
3- Prevent harmful joint purchasing  
We are extremely concerned about provisions related to joint purchasing. This cannot in any way be 
compared to gas joint purchasing which is related to a societal issue. From a downstream point of view,

the joint purchasing might be an attractive way forward; however it may increase price pressure on EU 
upstream supply chains, and in particular on Energy Intensive Industries like ours that already have to  
cope with the  increased sustainability, compliance, climate, and energy costs. This approach would 
circumvent competition and trade laws and hence would go against increasing Europe’s autonomy and 
resilience in critical raw materials. Joint purchasing to constitute Member States stocks should

jeopardize the goal of the entire regulation.  
4- Increase European content for sustainability 
Sustainability, in its environmental, social, and economic dimensions should be integrated in every 
aspect of the proposal. We support the recognition of certification schemes and call for a harmonized 
approach for raw materials ecolabelling by establishing the link with the ongoing Eco -design for 
sustainable products regulation (ESPR). Setting minimal green public procurement requirements can 
help create lead markets for sustainable products setting the competition not only on a price level but 
also on a footprint level. For instance, if produced in the EU, the CO2 footprint for silicon is more than 
three time less than the silicon  produced in the PR China . The European regulation and subsequent 
platforms (ERMA, ESIA, EBA...) must promote more European local content that fulfils sustainability 
standards. 
 
5- Strategic importance a subsequence of criticality  
Some raw materials are on the criticality list since 2011 , based on strict and transparent criteria and 
more than a decade of work. We are concerned that the proposal in several parts covers only strategic 
raw materials (strategic projects, investments…) as we are convinced that any critical raw material used 
in the twin transition should be deemed strategic and should benefit from the actions proposed. 
In addition, the methodology for defining strategic raw materials should be clear and transparent as 
additional raw materials like ferro-alloys, mainly used in steel, are key enablers for strategic and clean 
technologies like solar, wind, electricity grids and e-mobility. Chromium and its ferro-alloy form are 
key materials needed for aerospace and defense technologies. For this reason, we consider that the 
list should include the ferro-alloy form of the suggested raw materials in addition to chromium.  
Finally, t he European association for ferro -alloys and silicon industries, Euroalliages, urges the 
European legislators to consider our recommendations and ultimately put European competitiveness 
at the centre of the upcoming legislations.

Annex - Recycling of Silicon under the CRM Act 
In view of the Critical Raw Materials Act, the association for European ferro -alloys and silicon 
producers, Euroalliages, welcomes the commission’s proposal to set out targ ets for critical raw 
materials along the value chain. However, considering the specificities of each raw material, 
Euroalliages considers it crucial to differentiate materials with existing domestic production capacities 
and those with no current EU capacities. Individual Targets (per raw material) should further be 
considered to avoid that the capacity of some materials having already reached the current rate will 
not be supported to expand their capacity to meet the growing demand desired by the twin transition. 
recommends a case -by-case target for each raw material. This note reflects the recycling stage of 
silicon and the potential considerations.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Silicon applications  
Silicon is mainly used in Chemical applications, followed by Aluminium Industry to produce AlSi alloy, 
in terms of quantities. Silicon is also a key component of the Solar applications and electronic 
applications. In the chemical applications, Silicon is t ransformed into other components and in 
particular into Silicones. In the Aluminium Industry, Silicon becomes part of the AlSi alloy matrix. In 
both cases, Silicon cannot be any more isolated, either because it has been transformed into another 
component or because it is part of a metallurgical mixture.  
 
Figure 1: Silicon applications (source: EU CRM List 2023, Silicon factsheet) 
In brief, 
- The downstream applications of Silicon is mainly used in aluminum where Silicon is 
embedded in the alloys and in chemicals applications where Silicon is transformed into 
other substances, Silicones. Other well known applications are solar panels and microchips. 
- Silicon is not in large scale recovered to its initial state but is recycled within the recycling 
streams of aluminum. The “substance” recovery will gain in importance with the solar 
market and the first decommissioned panels. 
- Solar PV, electronic chips and EV batteries forecast demand growth is boosting research in 
silicon recycling streams. 
- Recycling of silicon should be considered including functional recycling in existing recycling 
streams like aluminium alloys. 
- Boosting research and innovation in the recycling and recovery of silicon under Horizon 
Europe and other European R&I funds is essential. 
 
5 
 
 
Currently, there are no pure silicon recycling streams in significant quantities. In the case of the AlSi 
alloy, nearly 75 % of all aluminium and aluminium alloys ever produced are still in use today and have 
therefore gone through a recycling or recovery process. End-of-life silicon is recycled at rates of ~45% 
in aluminium alloys but it is not recovered as pure silicon. The substance recovery of silicon is 
technically not possible in some application streams, and this should be considered when setting 
recovery targets. Functional recycling of metals is sustainable and is the reality of a series of metals 
embedded in other materials.   
 
Secondary material supply potential 
Material recovery of Silicon as such in large quantities is new as the quantities of Silicon scrap, coming 
mainly from the electronics or photovoltaic industry were until recently not enough to run sustainable 
recycling processes. Nowadays, within the industry which buys silicon, recycling streams using 
separate or specialised processes for utilisation of any side streams are being implemented.  
Silicon used in the electronics industry is of higher quality than for other applications. Most of the 
silicon scrap generated during crystal ingot and wafer production for electronics applications c an, 
therefore, be used in the photovoltaic industry. The huge annual growth in consumption of 
photovoltaics, where silicon is the dominant PV material, is boosting research activities to recover 
waste containing silicon, thus constituting a growing potential silicon feedstock.  
 
Figure 2: Global and European secondary supply potential (source: Metals for Clean Energy , KU Leuven ) 
  
In addition to the strong growth of solar panels, EV batteries (with silicon -based anodes) and their 
expected future waste streams can initiate a silicon recycling industry. The study ‘’Metals for Clean 
Energy ‘’done by KU Leuven and commissioned by Eurometaux, assumes an average recycling rate of 
90% for solar panels and 70% for batteries.  Globally, this would lead to 200 kt secondary silicon supply 
by 2030 and up to 650 kt by 2050. In Europe, it will lead to ~ 180kt of secondary supply (figure 2). 
 
Existing recycling streams  
The development and scale up of efficient recycling technologies to recover silicon metal is a major 
challenge, considering their economic viability.  Research and development in this area is encouraged 
and a focus on sustainability and circularity in raw materials should be prioritised in Horizon Europe 
projects and other R&I European funds.   For instance, the European Institute of Innovation and 
Technology (EIT - Raw materials) is financing several projects related to Silicon recover y from 
photovoltaic panels (ROSI; CarSiFer; ReSiELP).

European projects for recycling solar exist at early stages including: 
• Resitec in Norway, which recycles wafers waste generated in the production process of 
photovoltaic materials. 
• ROSI/Veolia in France, a recycling plant to treat end-of-life solar panels and recover both silicon 
and silver. 
• Circular Silicon in Germany is a new star t up that recycles silicon and aluminium powder 
coming from solar recycling streams and transforms it into silicon pallets that can be used as 
raw materials for the aluminium industry.

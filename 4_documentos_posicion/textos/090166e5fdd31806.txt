 
Critical Raw Materials Act: 
Metals industry improvement areas to Commission proposal 
Europe’s Critical Raw Materials Act is an important first step forward for Europe’s raw materials and industrial 
policy. It recognizes that the EU must act urgently to avoid unhealthy dependencies or supply bottlenecks for 
the metals and minerals required in its energy and digital transitions. 
 
The European Parliament and Member States have an important role in strengthening the Commission’s 
proposal and ensuring the Critical Raw Materials Act really does catalyse new domestic investment and 
competitiveness, in competition with even stronger industrial programmes from the US, China, and Japan. 
Europe’s metals industry recommends that the Commission’s proposal is amended in seven key areas.  
 
Summary of policy recommendations 
 
1. Strengthen the 2030 benchmarks, by requiring EU and Member State plans for delivering on them and 
making them a formal priority for referencing across all future EU legislation of relevance  
2. Refine the Strategic Raw Materials list, through adding back aluminium, in line with the Commission’s 
methodology (plus others), and referencing the primary commodity operations of byproduct materials  
 
3. Support the ambition for accelerating permitting, through endorsing maximum deadlines and one-stop 
shop, while still ensuring there is adequate time for public consultation and other important steps 
 
4. Link the Critical Raw Materials Act with future financing, through require that any future EU financing 
mechanisms – like the sovereignty fund – must be used to support the Critical Raw Materials Act goals 
 
5. Check added value of stockpiling and joint financing obligations, specifically considering to delete the 
proposal for joint purchasing due its potential for market distortions 
 
6. Maintain pragmatic proposals on certification & footprinting, especially the mechanism for the European 
Commission to validate which third party mining certification schemes meet i ts sustainability requirements. 
 
7. Strengthen Europe’s strategic partnerships strategy, through requiring that the European Critical Raw 
Materials Board prepares a formal strategy report for the European Commission to implement in its diplomacy 
 
1. Strengthen the 2030 production benchmarks (Article 1) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act proposal sets non-binding overall 2030 benchmarks for the EU to supply 10% of its 
extracted needs and 40% of its processed needs from domestic sources, with 15% of supply to come from recycled 
sources by 2030. The metals industry welcomes the principle of these benchmarks, but suggests their scope and 
ongoing use should be further clarified: 
• Ensure the benchmarks are relevant to each individual commodity before group ing. The list of 
strategic raw materials has major variance in market volume, from 4 million tonnes of annual EU copper 
demand to 64 tonnes of annual platinum demand. Measuring everything together would could lead to 
unintended consequences by favour focusing investments into the high tonnage materials first while 
overlooking specialist low-volume markets. When grouping commodities together, the Commission should 
transparently apply a volume-weighting to guarantee a proportionate approach. 
• The Commission should be required to follow its proposal with a detailed implementation report by 
end-2024. This should analyse Europe’s self-sufficiency and project pipeline for each strategic raw 
material, quantifying the level of investment needed to achieve the defined benchmark and current 
bottlenecks or challenges. This could be complemented by a requirement for Member States to develop 
Raw Materials plans, detailing their planned actions to contribute towards the EU -level benchmarks

• The 2030 benchmarks should be applied as Union priorities in all future EU policies. Achieving the 
benchmarks will require a greater alignment and rationalization of wider EU policy. To ensure regulatory 
coherence, the Critical Raw Materials Act benchmarks should be considered in all future EU policies of 
relevance, and not only the Ecodesign Regulation as the proposal currently  specifies in Article 1.4 
(including for example REACH/ CLP Regulations, Energy policy, Industrial Emissions/Water Directives).  
2. Refine the list of Strategic Raw Materials (Article 3) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials identifies a shortlist of strategic raw materials with a close link to the energy and digital 
transitions and an identified potential supply concern in the next decade. Shortlisted raw materials are eligible for 
accelerated permitting measures and extra financial support. We recommend some key improvements to this list. 
• Aluminium, zinc, and indium should be added to the list of strategic raw materials. The European 
Commission’s impact assessment identifies aluminium as fulfilling the definition of a strategic raw material 
(with its ore bauxite already on the critical raw materials list), due to strong rising transition demand, an 
eroding EU production base, and the growing role of China and Russia in European supply. It should be 
added back into the list strategic raw materials as was originally planned. Zinc and indium are also important 
strategic materials in a range of clean energy technologies. Zinc is the primary commodity for accessing 
byproduct critical raw materials like germanium – with an under-threat EU production base.  
 
• The main metallurgical process for accessing listed by-products should also be strategic. Several 
strategic raw materials are only mined or recycled as byproducts. This means their production is only viable 
from bigger operations focused on the primary commodity, such as zinc for germanium or lead in precious 
metals recycling. The Critical Raw Materials Act’s individual material focus does not reflect this necessary 
integration. The primary commodity operations necessary for accessing strategic byproduct raw materials 
should also automatically qualify as strategic, to send a clearer investment signal.  
 
• Substitution measures should be balanced. The Critical Raw Materials Act has a fundamental goal to 
improve conditions for domestic investment in EU strategic metals and minerals supply chains. New 
substitution measures should be added with care to avoid creating a contradictory market signal to investors, 
and should not target strategic raw materials without a current supply risk under the Critical Raw Materials 
Act methodology (i.e. those without a supply risk >1.0 according to the Commission’s methodology).  
 
3. Support the proposals for accelerating permitting (Articles 8-13) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act makes important proposals to speed up the permitting process for strategic mining, 
processing, and recycling projects. Slow permitting is a major bottleneck to European projects today, with new mines 
taking 10-15 years to come online. We support the Commission’s proposals for a one -stop shop, permitting deadlines, 
and other streamlining measures. These proposals are important to reduce overall bureaucracy and timelines, but must 
keep adequate time for necessary environmental checks and transparent community involvement.  
• Maintain the ambition of the Commission’s permitting proposals. We encourage Member States and 
MEPs to maintain the Commission’s ambition in it s permitting acceleration proposals, especially to maintain 
the one-stop shop proposal and deadlines for processing strategic projects. The practicality of the proposed 
deadlines should be tested further against Member State legislation and procedures, and  adequate time is 
required for transparently consulting all stakeholders. 
4. Link the Critical Raw Materials Act with future EU-level financing (Article 15) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act has no dedicated EU-level financial mechanism for supporting new projects, with 
Member States instead given new state aid flexibilities under the Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework. To 
support a level playing-field amongst differently resourced Member States, its important that the Critical Raw 
Materials Act is already formally linked to upcoming discussions on the EU -level sovereignty fund. 
 
• Require that the Critical Raw Materials Act is supported by any future EU-level funding. The Critical 
Raw Materials Act should commit that any future EU-level funding sources under the Green Deal Industrial 
Plan will be made available to strategic raw materials projects, and concede that this is an important gap in 
the EU’s plan compared with competing programmes from the US, China, and Japan.  
5. Check added value of stockpiling and joint purchasing proposals (Articles 21-24) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act includes clauses on centralized EU activities like stockpiling and joint purchasing. The 
metals industry is skeptical about the added value of these new clauses, and has a specific market concern on the 
proposal for a joint purchasing mechanism. 
• Delete the clause on Joint Purchasing (Article 24): The Critical Raw Materials Act proposal for an EU joint 
purchasing mechanism has potential to distort well-functioning global markets for raw materials trading, while 
increasing price pressure on European mining operations already facing higher operating and regulatory costs 
than global competitors. It would require that private companies would disclose internal information e.g. 
specifications, quantities, prices which would violate competition regulations. The cl ause should be deleted. 
6. Maintain pragmatic proposals on certification & footprinting (Articles 29-30) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act takes a pragmatic approach to ensuring the sustainability of Europe’s metals and 
minerals supply, which the metals industry overall supports. We recommend that the Parliament and Council 
maintains the Commission’s approach in this area, while ensuring coherence with related policy files.  
• Maintain recognition for certification schemes: The Critical Raw Materials Act proposes a system for 
the Commission to recognize third party certification schemes meeting its sustainability expectations. This 
is a pragmatic way for the EU to benchmark the range of different schemes that industry and other actors 
have developed to certify responsibility, and should be supported vs singular approaches.  
 
• Optimise Environmental Footprinting methodology: We support that the Critical Raw Materials Act 
proposal for environmental footprinting requirements is based on the Product Environmental Footprint 
methodology. This is the right basis for the Commission to work further on robust rules, coherently with 
parallel initiatives like the Battery Passport. We recommend that the selection of impact categories reflects 
only those sufficiently robust for benchmarking. 
 
• Strengthen regulatory text on recycling: The Critical Raw Materials Act sets requirements for Member 
States to adopt national circularity programmes. We recommend that further measures should be added 
into these plans, including requirements that Member States increase the uptake of quality standards for 
recycling processes of complex waste streams like electronics waste. 
7. Strengthen Europe’s strategic partnerships strategy (Article 33) 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act makes reference to the EU’s strategic partnerships as an integral tool to securing 
global supplies of scarce resources by 2030. However, there is a general lack of detail on the EU’s strategy and 
prioritization for negotiating these partnerships. Because of the 10-15 year timeline for bringing new mines online, 
we already know which regions will be able to deliver raw materials supply to Europe in 2030.  
• Define a central strategy for the EU’s raw materials diplomacy. The Critical Raw Materials Act asks the 
Critical Raw Materials Board to “periodically discuss” the EU’s strategic partnerships (Article 33). We 
consider that this requirement should be strengthened, with the Board asked to provide a formal strategy  
for the Commission to implement as one of its first priority tasks, ideally for completion by the end of 2024. 
This strategy should include a prioritization of the most important trade partners for meeting Europe’s short- 
term raw materials needs.

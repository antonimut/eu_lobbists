SMEunited Position paper on the  
Critical Raw Materials Act  
COM/2023/160 final 
Key messages  
• SMEunited welcomes the proposed measures consisting of p referential 
treatment and clear rule s for Member States  to facilitate and consequently 
increase the supply of raw materials. 
• SMEs are vital players across the whole value chain: from the extraction of 
critical raw materials to the development of  future-oriented technologies and 
the sale of the end product.  
• SMEunited call s for the full  application of the equal treatment principle 
regarding the different sizes of critical raw materials proj ects and their 
operators.  
• Alignment with related initiatives is imperative, particularly with regard to the 
definition of strategic technologies that are also essential for the Net -Zero 
Industry Act. Also, other interlinkages need to be deepened:  
o With the Single Market Emergency Instrument with regard to strategic 
reserves 
o With the Ecodesign Regulation with regard to product conformity  
o With the Chips Act with regard to the use of raw materials. 
• SMEunited supports the limited durations of the permit granting processes for 
Strategic Projects (Article 10). However, it is unclear, whether  the Regulation 
would take precedence over  existing rules , due to which the procedures are 
currently held up, and if not, how the time limits can be achieved. 
• We welcome the separation of critical and strategic raw materials,  which 
allows to account for both current and anticipated developments. Yet, more 
clarity on the relationship between the two categories is needed. 
• SMEunited is worried about the governance of the newly created European 
Critical Raw Materials Board. We fully believe SMEs must be represented in all 
meetings and discussions that concern them or their possible involvement in the 
raw materials value chain. This must not be left to the discretion of the Board.

Resume from SME perspective 
The Critical Raw Materials Act is an EU initiative trying to fight trade dependencies internally. 
This is simultaneously its weakness and its strength. Unless we find new trade partners 
through new approaches to trade policy, the EU will not achieve security of supply chains. Yet, 
the EU must also tackle existing bottlenecks within its own domain to achieve its political goals. 
The political goals the Critical Raw Materials Act pursues are resilience and autonomy as well 
as the twin transition for a sustain able future. To achieve  them, preferential treatment and 
streamlined processes to raw material projects are sensible actions to take. 
SMEunited therefore welcome s this initiative aiming to increase the supply of raw materials 
that are needed to enable work ing with future -oriented technologies.  SMEs are drivers of 
innovation and the deliverers of the twin transition. Thus, supply of these materials is crucial 
for SMEs. At the same time, SMEs are vital players across the whole value chain: from the 
extraction of critical raw materials to the sale of the end -product. For this reason, when 
implementing the Critical Raw Materials Act, all concerned bodies should pay particular 
attention to whether SMEs have been regarded fully and fairly. In the current proposal,  this is 
left to the goodwill of the newly created European Critical Raw Materials Board or based on 
the assumption that Member States have enough capacities to fulfil all of the newly gained 
responsibilities in the most diligent and timely manner. We fear  that this won’t always be the 
case and SMEs will be forgotten or knowingly excluded.  
Ultimately, we remain sceptical of the effectiveness of the proposed regulation. As a 
consequence of the many responsibilities given to the Member States, there is no harmonisation 
achieved with regard to the administrative processes . Already engaged Member States will 
find hardly any value added, while Member States whose authorities are already overladen 
and lagging behind receive no assistance to achieve the desirable outcomes of quick and 
efficient processes. Moreover, outcomes of permit granting process also won’t converge. By 
leaving Member States responsible for the speed of the procedures, the authorisation 
procedures in the EU remain vastly heterogen eous due to the fact that Member States take 
different conditions and a different number of conditions into account in the procedures. Finally, 
SMEs will require more than administrative support to assist them financing Strategic Projects.  
Trade and investment policy 
To gain greater access to critical raw materials  the EU will have to  use of a broad range of 
trade policy instruments . Consequently, it is a good sign that the Communication 
accompanying the proposed Regulation mentions various tools like a Cr itical Raw Materials 
Club, trade and investment agreements, new generation Sustainable Investment Facilitation 
Agreements, the Global Gateway strategy, bilateral Strategic Partnerships  and the EU Export 
Credit Facility . While trade agreements and strategic  partnerships will remain the key for 
access to critical raw materials, many other tools still need to prove to be effective. We are most 
hopeful with regard to the EU Export Credit Facility. Instruments such as guarantees and loans

that support companies in obtaining the necessary critical raw materials directly would present 
a quick  alternative when  political negotiations are stagnating.  Ultimately, the goals of any 
investment tool must be to provide sufficient investment certainty because this industrial sector 
is characterised by a long payback period. This is ever more important in a sector in which the 
EU is heavily dependent on third countries known for unfair trading practices. 
Therefore, the  Commission’s assessment to make better offers to partner countries when 
offering to assist in the material procession in the partner country, is definitely correct. We call 
for active consideration of what SMEs can provide in this regard such as technical know-how, 
support and joint projects. 
Consistency with other legislation 
The necessary consideration of a multitude of national and European regulations, which lead 
to long procedures, remains uncommented by the Commission.  We call for a  clarification of 
how this regulation stands in relation to the other proposed regulations, particularly on CBAM, 
Deforestation, and more explicitly on the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 
(CSDDD), which is at least mentioned in the explanatory memorandum. 
According to the proposal, “the calculation of the environmental footprint of each material 
under the CRMs Regulation, could contribute to the effective implementation of a due diligence 
policy”. In light of this, it is important that SMEs shall only be responsible for their own activity 
and cannot be dragged into responsibility by larger companies, which are accountable for the 
whole supply chain. 
Furthermore, there is a need for a lignment with other initiatives, such as  the SMEI regulation 
regarding the issue of strategic reserves , the Ecodesign Regulation regarding product 
conformity as well as both the Chips Act and the Net Zero Industry Act regarding the use of the 
raw materials. As the implications for SMEs are evidently manifold, it is disappointing that the 
SME test was conducted without considering how the measures of this proposal, i.e. provisions 
for national authorities, will be implemented. 
Specific remarks 
Article 1 - Scope 
Article 1 outlines the objectives of the future regulation defining target values for extraction, 
processing and recycling of strategic raw materials within in the European Union. The 
Regulation aims to enhance the efforts Member States will have to undertake to reach these 
targets. If the Union is likely not to achieve the objectives, the Commission will consider 
“proposing measures or exercising its powers at Union level”.  
 
 
 
 4 of 10 
In order to strengthen the accountability as well as the expertise of such a decision, we believe 
the European Critical Raw Materials Board  referred to in Article 34 must be consult ed 
specifically on the need for further action on European level. 
Article 2 – Definition and interrelation of raw materials, critical 
raw materials and strategic raw materials  
The proposal lacks a clear classification of raw materials, critical raw materials and strategic 
raw materials. The Impact Assessment would imply that the three categories are hierarchically 
structured with critical raw materials being a subset of all raw materials and strategic raw 
materials being a subset of critical raw materials. This has implications throughout the text of 
the proposal when provisions are made for “critical raw material projects”, which is frequently 
mentioned but – other th an “Strategic Projects” – not defined in Article 2.  In any case, a 
definition of “critical raw material projects” should be added.  
Articles 3 & 4 – Lists of strategic & critical raw materials 
We welco me the separation of critical and strategic raw materials, the former allowing to 
account for both current developments and the latter allowing for future developments that are 
already anticipated for the implementation of the twin transitions and defence and aerospace 
objectives.  
Furthermore, it is important that the proposal defines critical raw materials in a way that 
includes all raw materials that are essential for the EU industry and where the disruption 
between supply and demand has reached a critic al level. However, we are worried that the 
admission criteria, i.e. the threshold for supply risk indicated in Article 4.2, is too strict and scarce 
materials are omitted even thought their lack of supply is de facto already jeopardising the 
continuity of the economic activity. When only materials that are hard fought over are eligible 
for the list, SMEs – who most likely cannot compete for those materials – are systematically 
excluded from the benefits of this regulation. Therefore, we call for a close and  continuous 
evaluation of the listing criteria.  
Moreover, we are sceptical of the delegated acts for amending the list of strategic raw materials 
without a transparent methodology communicated in advance . In order to support the twin 
green and digital transition, the European Commission must remain open to developing 
technologies. In this regard, the selection of strategic raw materials must also be aligned with 
the strategic net -zero technologies that are determined in the Net Zero Industry Act. 
Nonetheless, we demand the Commission to consult the relevant stakeholders to receive a full 
picture of all materials necessary for future-oriented technologies. 
Regardless, a 4 year-interval between updating these lists is too long . Considering the speed 
of developments in the field , this interval will leave  the EU lacking behind other economic 
powers and thus, SMEunited proposes that the update of the list takes place every two years.  
 
 
 
 5 of 10 
To account for planning and investment security, we would welcome a transition period for raw 
materials which would no longer be considered a strategic raw material as the result of an 
update. As this could have particularly severe effects for SMEs, we propose that they shall still 
be considered to be a strategic raw material for two years following the publication of any such 
update. 
Furthermore, we believe that materials that replace strategic raw materials should be treated  
as strategic raw materials and also qualify for strategic raw materials projects at any step of 
the value chain in accordance to Article 5. 
Article 5, 6 & 7 – Benefits of strategic projects for downstream 
sectors 
Regarding Article 5 on criteria for the recognition of strategic projects, SMEunited welcomes 
the criteria set out in paragraph 1d, “the strategic project has to prove its positive impact on 
downstream sectors”. Furthermore, this article should explicitly mention SME s. It is important 
that downstream sectors, mostly SMEs, are taken into account across the whole Regulation, as 
SMEs fill important gaps in supply chains.  
Article 6 on Application and Recognition of Strategic projects foresees the requirement to 
provide relevant evidence of the fulfilment of the criteria laid down in Article 5.1. As far as Article 
5.1(d) is concerned, true evidence of an impact of strategic projects on downstream sectors will 
only be determinable once the strategic project is implemented. Therefore, we call for an 
obligation to include evidence of the benefits for downstream sectors in the report the project 
promoter already has to submit to the Board according to Article 7.5.  
Article 8 – Authorities in the permit granting process 
Business operators must know who their national competent authority is when the regulation 
enters into force – not three months later as currently proposed. The ongoing legislative process 
also provides Member States with sufficient time to designate one national competent 
authority. 
With regards to Article 8, which outlines streamlined permitting processes, SMEunited would 
welcome more clarity whether the possibility to delegate responsibilities to a regional authority 
are possible in accordance to Article 8.3. This would be important as most permits are currently 
granted at local and regional level. Member States should be able to choose the most efficient 
way that ensures no delays to the granting of permits. 
If Member States do not have the resources to establish or permanently operate a centralised 
One stop shop, the Commission shall provide technical assistance for the Member State to fulfil 
requirements set out in this article. Overall, we are sceptical of creating bottlenecks instead of 
a facilitation for entrepreneurs. The once-only principle and frictionless forwarding of

information between authorities must become a reality with regard to the delegation of tasks 
or responsibilities. 
Article 10 – Duration of permit granting processes 
SMEunited supports the limited durations of the permit granting processes for Strategic 
Projects (Article 10). The essential question with regard to the duration of the permit granting 
process, however, is how the Critical Raw Materials Regulation relates t o the substantive 
regulations (such as E nvironmental Impact Assessments, Industrial Emissions Directive  or 
existing national procedural regulations), due to which the procedures currently take longer 
than the stated time limit. In these cases, clarity is n eeded whether the time limitation take s 
precedence over substantive law. 
Environmental Impact Assessments need to weigh the negative and positive, direct and indirect 
impacts carefully. This requires time and thus, strategic raw material projects  must also be 
treated as a priority in these procedures. The speedy construction of a more sustainable raw 
materials value chain in Europe can create large environmental gains in the near future. At the 
same time, it is essential that Strategic Projects comply with the regulations to truly ensure the 
sustainability of such projects. Otherwise, they will be very unpopular even if they create jobs. 
Article 11 – Assistance for carrying out environmental impact 
assessments 
We welcome the efforts to streamline t he environmental impact assessments and to provide 
Member States with concrete guidance for assisting to that end.  However, we see that these 
provisions are one of many examples of a significant increase in administrative burden for 
national authorities, where existing resources are already stretched thin. Member States must 
make sure they are able to fulfil requirements of this Act in full without prejudice to the size of 
the project they are dealing with. Bigger projects must not be treated preferentially in case 
deadlines cannot be met.  
Article 16 – Off-take agreements 
If done right, a n off-take system can be an enabl ing and inclusive instrument that brings 
interested off -takers and promoters of Strategic Projects together and allow s for SMEs’ 
participation in the value chains of strategic raw materials.  Therefore, we welcome this 
initiative by the Commission and call for easy access to existing offers and bids and an 
unbureaucratic possibility for SMEs to place bids and offers. 
Articles 19-24 – Risk monitoring and mitigation 
We welcome the intention of the European Commission to identify strategic projects along the 
entire supply chain and to build up strategic reserves where supply is at risk – if there is no

mandatory quota for companies and the final decision about stockpiling remains with the 
companies. 
Monitoring is a central pillar of the proposal, but it risks turning into a paper tiger. Systematically 
collecting a wide pallet of data points from market participants could lead to a disproportionate 
administrative burden, as SMEunited has already pointed out for similar provisions in other EC 
proposals (e.g. on the Single Market Emergency Instrument). It would be more useful to create 
communication channels for market participants to identify (imminent) disruptions in supply 
chains at an early stage, allowing targeted and risk -based action. The holding of strategic 
stocks for the public interest should additionally be publicly funded. 
A potential stockpiling by the EU and / or Member States needs to address a range of still 
unsolved questions: Who will pay for the raw materials? Will there be an  obligation for EU / 
national companies to deliver raw materials to the stock, if needed? At which price will the 
companies be requested to deliver the raw materials? In case of a crisis, how will the available 
raw materials be distributed to the companies that need these raw materials? The different 
quality of raw materials is very important in different application s and processes. This should 
also be taken into account in the stockpiling decision.  
Article 23 – Supply chain audits 
Article 23 foresees an audit that large companies shall carry out 
 (a) a mapping of where the strategic raw materials they use are extracted, processed or 
recycled;  
(b) a stress test of their supply chain of strategic raw materials, consisting of an 
assessment of its vulnerability to supply disruptions by estimating the impact of different 
scenarios that may cause such disruptions and their potential effects. 
Given the extensivity of the stress test large companies would have to undertake, there is no 
doubt that this will create s ubstantive reporting burden for SMEs working with large 
companies. We believe these provisions are disproportionate and recommend deleting the 
article as a whole. 
Article 24 – Joint purchasing 
SMEunited rejects the minimum amounts of demanded material necessary to participate in the 
joint purchasing system outlined in Article 24.2(b) as they will systematically exclude SMEs and 
lead to the concentration of demand on a limited number of large buyers. 
The main purpose of joint purchasing initiatives is to ensure access to purchased materials on 
a broader and larger level. SMEs therefore must be given a fair possibility to access highly -
demanded materials to prevent the fragmentation of the Single Market caused by a free betting 
race.

Furthermore, measures should be taken to avoid autonomous initiatives by Member States, in 
order to ensure maximum coordination . This would bring coherence to the Single Market 
Emergency Instrument which in Article 39 foresees that “participating Member States shall not 
procure goods or services covered by such procurement by other means”. 
Article 25 - Administrative burden trough reporting on waste 
We are particularly critical of the obligations of the Member States described in paragraph 5 to 
report separately the quantities of components containing relevant amounts of critical raw 
materials removed from such waste equipment and the quantities of critical raw materials 
recovered from the waste electrical and electronic equipment, in addition to the reporting of  
data concerning the quantities of waste electrical and electronic equipment recycled to the 
Commission. This would create an immense administrative burden for the companies 
concerned, which would have to record the corresponding quantities of each device. It must be 
sufficient for the quantities of components recovered from the equipment to be reported once 
a year by the companies as a total quantity. 
Paragraph 3 stipulates that within 4 years, EU Member States must adapt and implement 
measures to promote the recovery of critical raw materials from extractive waste. It is unclear 
what measures are envisaged here. 
Article 26 – Unreasonable obligations targeted at exploration 
and extraction of critical raw materials 
The proposed regulation includes an obligation for a preliminary economic assessment study 
for all operators. For companies for which the exploration and extraction of critical raw 
materials is not part of their business activities, the obligation to carry  out a corresponding 
study at their own expense and risk is unreasonable. There must be an exception here for those 
operators whose mining waste facilities do not contain critical raw materials. 
We also take a critical view of the publication of company-related data regarding the publicly 
accessible database mentioned in paragraph 4. Company and business secrets must be 
preserved. Therefore, exceptions must be provided for here as well. Moreover, the Member 
States must not pass on their obligation to the companies. 
Regarding the reporting of quantities and concentrations, it should be specified that not all raw 
materials in the stockpiles must be named, but only the relevant critical raw materials. 
Article 27 & 28- Recycling permanent magnets 
We welcome the planned obligation for distributors of various products to attach data carriers 
to the products, which provide for example information on the weight, location and composition 
of the permanent magnet. In addition, we would also like to suggest that information on other 
 
 
 
 9 of 10 
"hazardous contents" such as capacitors, mercury switches, etc. should also be provided on 
the data carriers. 
Regarding the regulation on minimum recycled content for permanent magnets envisaged for 
2030, it must be ensured that the regulation is carried out based on an impact assessment and 
that the relevant stakeholders are involved in the decision -making process. Therefore, an 
obligation of the Commission for an impact assessment and prior consultation with the relevant 
stakeholders must be inserted. 
We therefore propose the following amendments to Article 28, paragraph 3, paragraph 1:  
“After 31 December 2030, the Commission may adopt delegated acts  shall make a proposal 
for a regulation to the Council and the European Parliament supplementing this Regulation by 
laying down minimum shares for neodymium, dysprosium, praseodymium, terbium, boron, 
samarium, nickel and cobalt recovered from post-consumer waste that must be present in the 
permanent magnet incorporated in the products referred to i n paragraph 1. Before doing so, 
the Commission must carry out an impact assessment and consult relevant stakeholders.” 
Article 34 & 35 – Governance of application procedures 
The European Critical Raw Materials Board itself, as outlined in articles 34 and 35, appears to 
be political in nature , while technical assistance is carried out on the level of the subgroups.  
Whether and which stakeholders will be heard for establishing the status quo for measuring it 
against benchmarks is left to the discretion of the Board.  We fully believe SMEs must be 
represented in all meetings and discussions that concern them or their involvement in the raw 
materials value chain. 
In order to gain insight into the specific European dependencies and future demand a nd to 
develop a targeted policy and more action perspective in case of disruptions, it is important to 
combine the knowledge of institutions, governments and business es. For this reason, it is 
worrying that business representatives do not have a permanent seat in the governance 
structure in the Commission's proposal. This is a recurring shortcoming in recent proposals (e.g. 
SMEI). SMEunited believes that besides the 27 Representatives of Member States, European 
social partners should also be included in the Board. 
To act as an effective  intermediary body, there must be a more institutionalised inflow out of 
information from social partners outlined in the text. This is vital to ensure a fair depiction of 
economic reality and to prevent the systematic exclusion or neglect of SMEs from raw material 
supply chains.  
In conclusion, we suggest establishing an additional  subgroup that brings together industry 
representatives from the strategic sectors, that ensures representation of SMEs across Member 
States and allows to  discuss possibilities to boost SME involvement  in raw materials value 
chains.

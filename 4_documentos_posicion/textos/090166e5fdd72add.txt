WHY ALUMINIUM IS A  
STRATEGIC RAW MATERIAL 
 
Brussels, June 2023 
 
The Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) presents  a forward-looking approach in identifying strategic raw materials . 
Aluminium is unequivocally recognised as a strategic material in both Canada and the United States mineral strategies, 
creating an investment signal making North America a more attractive destination compared to Europe for the aluminium 
sector. With this short paper, we aim to dem onstrate how aluminium clearly meets the criteria to be considered a 
“strategic raw material” under the European CRMA. 
Under the methodology included in Annex I – Section 2.1 of the CRMA proposal, it is specified the eligibility of a material 
to be included in the list as of “strategic importance shall be determined 
based on the relevance of a raw material for the green and digital 
transition as well as defence and space applic ations”. As such 
aluminium is indispensable for the production of all those technologies 
and therefore eligible to be considered as a Strategic Raw Material. 
The additional factors described in Section 2.2 and 2.3 should be 
considered as mathematical tools for future analytical trends to assess 
the progress and determine relevant needed policy actions (Annex 1).  
Aluminium was also explicitly referred to as a strategic raw material in the impact assessment accompanying the CRMA 
proposal. In particular, with reference to the strategic list (p.35), the impact assessment notes that “It would be shorter 
than the current list of critical raw materials and would likely overlap with it, but not fully be a subset as some raw materials 
that are strategically important and likely to face future supply challenges (e.g. copper, nickel and aluminium) are currently 
too diversified to pass the supply risk threshold of the criticality assessment”. 
Despite aluminium being considered strategically important, its global supply is not well diversified.  China holds a 
dominant position in the global primary aluminium production market, accounting for around 60% of production on 
average (figure 2) and rising (twenty years ago, China accounted for just 10% of global primary aluminium production, but 
this share has increased rapidly over the past two decades). As a result, any market or policy changes in China can lead to 
unexpected disruption in the global supply, whose impact could be significantly amplified if the supply was highly 
dependent on unreliable trade countries. Therefore, not including aluminium in the strategic list is in contradiction with 
the proposed methodology and would make the CRMA ineffective in capturing future supply risks. 
According to KU Leuven study on metals for clean energy, the EU energy transition will lead to a 30% increase in aluminium 
demand by 2040. Aluminium plays an essential  role in Europe’s transition to a sustainable future (figure 1),  being a key

ANNEX 1 
 
The CRM Act includes in Annex I – Section 2 a methodology for select 
Strategic Raw Materials. According to the methodology the strategic 
importance shall be determined based on the relevance of a raw 
material for the green and digital transition as well as defence and 
space applications and elaborated upon a set of methodological 
factors for developing in the future the statistical analysis and 
informing policy1.  
Section 2.1 combines robust qualitative and quantitative factors and it 
specifies that the “strategic importance shall be determined based on 
the relevance of a raw material for the green and digital transition as 
well as defence and space applications ”. As such aluminium is 
indispensable for the production of all those technologies and 
therefore eligible to be considered as a Strategic Raw Material.  
The additional factors described in Section 2.2 and 2.3 should be 
approached as mathematical tools and considered for future analytical 
trends to assess the progress and determine relevant needed policy 
actions.  
Section 2.1 of methodology for Strategic Raw Materials  
Based on the criteria defined on Section 2.1.a ( amount of strategic technologies using a raw material as an input ), 
aluminium is of strategic importance as it is relevant raw material for the green and digital transition as well as defence 
and space applications.

Section 2.3 difficulty of increasing production 
The Commission’s formula under Section 2.3 (a)  to determine the difficulty of 
increasing production, calculates the current production scale (PS) of a raw 
material for a reference period with log10 being the common logarith m and GS 
is the global annual production of a raw material for a reference period.  
 
The common logarithm is the logarithm with base 10 used in mathematics to calculate the 
exponentiality needed to increase production and plot the appropriate graphs for statistical analysis.  
 
If we assume that GS is 100 then 100 = 102 the log base 10 of 100 is 2, or log10 (100) = 2  
 
In the case of aluminium, we estimate global primary aluminum production to be 64.7 Mt in 2020, 
67.4 Mt in 2021 and 69.1 Mt in 2022.  
 
Therefore, using the formula the results produced will be 1.81, 1.82 and 1.83 
respectively for 2020, 2021 and 2022. These data then can be used to plot 
the graph for the relevant statistical analysis.   
 
We have to note for the purposes of analysing global aluminium production, 
reliable data for production of recycled aluminium would also need to be 
collected and integrated into the full amounts.  
 
 
 
The Commission’s formula under Section 2.3 (b)  calculates the r eserves-
production ratio R/P of a raw material for a reference period with R denoting the 
reserves of economically extractable geological resources of a raw material and 
GS the global annual production of a raw material.  
 
Given the unique production process of aluminium, this formula would need to be  elaborated further 
as to produce primary aluminium it is required to extract bauxite, refine it into alumina and then 
transform it into primary aluminium in a smelter, with a ratio roughly of 2:1 as 4 tonnes of bauxite 
needed to produce 2 tonnes of alumina and then again with a ratio of 2:1 the 2 tonnes of alumina are 
needed to produce 1 tonne of aluminium.

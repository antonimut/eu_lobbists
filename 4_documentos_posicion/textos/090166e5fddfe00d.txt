   
  
 
 
 
 
Feedback to European Commission on the European Critical Raw Materials 
Act from Germanwatch e.V. (June 2023) 
 
Raw Materials Use Reduction  
Our recommendations (Recitals 3 and 6; Article 1 and 25): 
- The Union’s consumption of critical raw materials must be mitigated in order to achieve an 
overall reduction of raw materials consumption as fast as possible.  
- The Union should phase out single-use products containing Critical Raw Materials (CRMs) 
(e.g. portable batteries and disposable single-use vapes). 
- We welcome the introduction of a DPP. It has a significant potential to pave the way towards 
a more circular economy as it can address the information deficit along a circular value chain 
that often impeded circularity. Information on CRMs and the supply chain included in their 
components and products should also be clearly indicated in the digital product passport 
requirements. 
- Each Member State shall adopt and implement national programmes containing measures 
designed to reduce the need for CRMs and devise measures where their use can be avoided 
without significant investments, economic losses or endangering the health of individuals. 
Such measures may include increasing material efficiency, developing sufficiency plans, and 
encouraging the development and use of alternative materials (i.e. substitution). 
 
Strategic Projects and permitting   
Our recommendations (Articles 7, 10, 13 and 34):  
- The CRMA should keep the balance between resilience of industrial production, 
environmental protection, public participation and streamlined permitting time. This means 
that every project should have an environmental, social and economic impact assessment. 
Provisions regarding tacit approval of permitting procedures and the overriding public 
interest clause shall be deleted. 
- Member states need to ensure that their licensing authorities count on sufficient financial 
resources and well-trained staff in order to carefully and in a timely manner revise project 
applications. Beyond hard deadlines, allocate specific financial resources to Member States 
may help to increase staff dealing with permits and make mandatory “pre-permitting” 
procedures with early involvement of all affected communities. 
- The complete Aarhus Convention instead of only Articles 6 and 7 must be referenced as well 
as corresponding international agreements such as the Escazú Agreement. 
- The provision concerning engagement with local communities should make explicit reference 
to established international frameworks, such as Article 27 of the International Convention of 
Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights 
(UNGPs), the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, the UN Declaration on the 
Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), and ILO Convention 169. 
- Public access to and transparency of documents submitted by project promoters and 
applicants for Strategic Projects, both to national authorities and the Critical Raw Materials 
Board, have to be guaranteed.

- Strategic Projects should not be recognised in cases where the available scientific evidence 
establishes a plausible risk of significant environmental harm or of disrupting the global 
carbon cycle, even if the evidence is inconclusive, including deep-sea mining. 
- To ensure the aforementioned criteria, establish a subgroup on sustainability and responsible 
mining within the Critical Raw Materials Board to include civil society and Indigenous 
Peoples representatives.  
 
Sustainability Requirements 
Our recommendations (Article 5 and Annex III): 
- The CRMA must uphold and add sustainability criteria and reference to international 
instruments. This includes good faith, meaningful, continuous, and transparent stakeholder 
consultation, Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC), and the Right to Say No. At a 
minimum, the following international agreements have to be referenced: UNDRIP, ILO 
Convention 169, ICCPR Article 27, the Escazú Agreement, Article 1 of the International 
Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, the International Convention on the 
Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, and the Convention on the Elimination of 
All Forms of Discrimination against Women. 
- The CRMA must require that all companies applying to be project promoters demonstrate a 
substantial track record of respect for human rights, Indigenous rights, the environment, and 
the rule of law. When establishing whether a project can be implemented sustainably, the 
Critical Materials Board should assess, alongside internationally recognized instruments, the 
company’s respect for due diligence standards set out in the UNGPs as well as existing 
legislation such as the Battery Regulation, and upcoming legislation such as the Corporate 
Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD). This has to apply regardless of whether the 
project promoter fall under the scope of such instruments. 
- The CRMA must prevent companies with a record of poor corporate conduct from becoming 
project promoters, which would jeopardise the integrity of public funds, by drawing on and 
expanding exclusion criteria set out in the Public Sector Directive and Award of Concessions 
Contracts Directive, particularly surrounding corruption, to also cover convictions in OECD 
countries or countries where Strategic Projects are located and crimes against the 
environment, human rights, and Indigenous rights. 
- The extraction, processing and importation of CRMs exploited from the deep sea must be 
prevented. Consistent with the precautionary principle, the CRMA must ensure the necessary 
environmental and social safeguards to prevent the exploitation of the deep sea for CRMs 
while scientific uncertainty persists. 
 
Certification Schemes 
Our recommendations (recitals 11, 49; Annex III (4)): 
- Adherence to or the commitment to obtain a certification scheme cannot suffice to fulfil the 
sustainability requirements for a Strategic Project. They cannot replace environmental and 
social due diligence. Certification schemes can only be one tool amongst others that 
companies and regulators may use to assess the sustainability aspects of a Strategic Project, 
   
  
 
 
 
 
and are not a replacement for a broader assessment of human rights and environmental 
performance. 
- The Act should equip the European Commission with the necessary mandate and resources to 
conduct its own independent analysis of whether a potential Strategic Project meets the 
sustainability and human rights standards set out in the law. 
- To make certification schemes effective as one amongst other tools a range of criteria would 
have to be met. These apply on the one side to the standard system itself and on the other side 
to the methodology and reports. 
Our recommendations for the standard system, methodology and reports (Article 29, Annex IV): 
- Governance through a multi-stakeholder system that gives an equal voice alongside industry 
to affected Indigenous and non-indigenous communities, workers, and civil society groups. 
- Companies have to adhere to comprehensive human rights and social and environmental 
standards according to the UNGPs, OECD Guidelines on Multinational Enterprises, UNDRIP 
and ILO Convention 169. 
- The standard system has rules governing disclosures to assess conflicts of interest between 
the auditing firm and the company that is audited. 
- The standard system offers an effective and accessible grievance mechanism according to the 
UNGPs to evaluate and adjudicate complaints related to audits and their outcomes and in 
between audits. 
- The standard system takes adequate measures to ensure that audited companies address 
environmental and human rights harm identified during audits (corrective action plans). 
- The audit methodology builds on adequate participation from and consultation with local 
Indigenous and non-indigenous communities, workers, and other key stakeholders; it shall 
particular take into account FPIC and the Right to Say No.  
- The full audit report and remediation plans publicly available and include sufficient detail 
that is comprehensible for all stakeholders, including language and technical knowledge 
limitations. 
- The audit company must be selected independently from the audited company. The costs for 
audits should be carried by the certification scheme in order to avoid conflicts of interest 
between mining companies and auditing services. 
 
Strategic Partnerships 
Our recommendations (Article 33): 
- Criteria and actions for Strategic Partnerships must be extended and strengthened. They 
should ensure that the EU and the third countries' regulatory frameworks are aligned with 
international agreements such as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and 
on Economic and Social Rights, UNDRIP, including the right to FPIC, the ILO Declaration 
on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work and ILO Convention 169. They should also be 
aligned with multilateral environmental agreements, such as the Convention on Biodiversity 
(CBD), the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Frameworks and the Minamata 
Convention on Mercury. 
   
  
 
 
 
 
- The EU needs to ensure that the actual implementation of the regulatory framework ensures 
the monitoring, prevention, remediation and mitigation of environmental and social impacts. 
Furthermore, the EU has to guarantee the use of socially responsible practices including 
respect for human and labour rights, the Right to Say No for local communities and 
meaningful engagement with and active participation of affected and local communities – 
particularly Indigenous Peoples (including a grievance mechanism).  
- Moreover, the principle of non-regression and the principle of progressive realization should 
be applied; the focus has to be on working together to improve raw materials governance. 
Consequences in case of non-compliance have to be established. Compliance policies must be 
structural and robust. 
- Term value addition has to be further defined while taking into account the interests of the 
respective partner countries and rightsholders there. The EU should focus on supporting 
domestic industrialization, sharing knowledge, technology, patents and capital, and being 
willing to import finished goods. 
- Participation of civil society organisations and Indigenous peoples from the EU and the third 
countries should be guaranteed when negotiating, implementing and monitoring the 
partnership.  
- Transparency of Strategic Partnerships and access to information must be guaranteed. 
Therefore, Memoranda of Understanding between the EU and third countries on Strategic 
Partnerships, roadmaps as well as regular reporting of the actions and advancements have to 
be publicly available in order to enable constant monitoring by all stakeholders. 
- Mechanisms in other regulations and trade agreements may not be used to undermine what is 
agreed on in a Strategic Partnership. 
 
Financial instruments 
Our recommendations (Article 15): 
- Export credit agencies (ECAs) must urgently address their notorious lack of transparency and 
accountability. It is imperative that they promptly share important information, such as 
environmental and social impact assessments (ESIAs), with the public, including the 
communities directly affected by projects. ECAs must ensure the provision of basic data, 
including transaction volumes, sector-specific transactions, and recipient countries, to address 
concerns regarding accountability. 
- ECAs need to realign with the development, climate, environmental and human rights policy 
objectives of the EU before being mobilized for Strategic Projects. 
 
Circular Economy  
Our recommendations (Article 1, 25, 26 and 27): 
- The CRMA has to increase coherence with the waste hierarchy, favouring prevention, repair 
and reuse over recycling. Collection of waste containing CRMs should be directed towards 
prioritising reuse and repair. For that, the regulation should devise circular design and 
lifetime extension requirements (or refer to another law devising so). It is important that the 
CRMA at least establishes a reusability assessment for equipment and components collected 
containing CRMs, as well as reuse targets at the national level. Moreover, information on 
   
  
 
 
 
 
disassembly for permanent magnets should be available to independent repairers and 
refurbishers in addition to recyclers. 
- Targets for EU consumption coming from EU recycling capacity shall be increased from 
15% to 30% (aggregated target for Strategic Raw Materials). 
- Improvements are necessary regarding collection and separation of components or products 
containing CRMs. EU level collection targets related to the quantities of CRMs placed on the 
market must be defined to ensure proper reporting and avoid fraud. 
- The European Commission should propose a plan within the CRMA to define recycled 
contents targets for each CRM in all the products containing CRMs that goes beyond 
batteries and permanent magnets and which could articulate with the propsed Ecodesign 
Regulation. Furthermore, collection and EOL (end-of-life) recycling rates (not recovery) 
targets for each CRM shall be proposed. This plan will ensure that the recycling capacity 
target will not be fulfilled only by increasing the EU’s recycling capacity for industrial metals 
such as copper. 
- Criteria for public procurement, such as product longevity and ability to disassemble, should 
be established. 
- Recovery of mining waste may not replace recycling. Recovery of mining waste should be 
part of the target for 10% of supply coming from mining capacity within the EU. The 
recovery of CRMs from extractive waste remains an extractive activity producing residual 
mining waste, it should therefore be specified that it must be subject to the whole Directive 
on the Management of Waste from Extractive Industries (2006/21/EC), and not just Articles 3 
and 5 cited in the text. Moreover, when an extractive waste recovery project is launched, the 
waste mobilized for re-extraction must be subject to a soil pollution diagnosis that can 
contribute to the database of all closed waste facilities. Finally, the CRM extraction process 
in extractive waste should be coupled with a plan for the remediation of historical pollution.

Materials for the European Green Deal  
 
The European Green Deal has been an important compass of European political leadership since 2019. 
Europe's commitment to climate neutrality by 2050 has made it imperative for the EU to realign its 
legislation and propose more ambitious targets to reduce g reenhouse gas emissions and improve 
energy efficiency. In parallel to the stricter standards for new buildings under the revision of the 
Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, the European Commission rightly identified the 
refurbishment of existing buildings in Europe as an important priority for action, with the ambition 
to double refurbishment rates over the decade.1  
Russia's aggression against Ukraine has been a powerful reminder of the vital need to secure Europe's 
energy supply. It is of utmost importance to avoid energy losses, especially through the renovation of 
buildings, as they still account for approximately  35% of Europe's energy -related greenhouse gas 
emissions in 2020. 
Speeding up building rehabilitation and minimizing the carbon footprint of existing and new buildings 
requires low -carbon embodied solutions for new and existing buildings . Raw materials such as 
gypsum, which are mainly sourced in Spain, account for 50% of Europe's needs, are an important 
component of lightweight, low-carbon and energy-efficient building solutions.  
ATEDY, the Technical and Business Association of Gypsum, a national organization representing 
suppliers and manufacturers of plasters, plasters and their derivatives, is committed to the objective 
of supplying essential raw materials to European societies in the most sustainable way and 
contributing substantially to the objectives of the European Green Deal.  
 
Measures considered  in the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) is a clear impetus for the EU to consider 
the importance of raw material supply to secure its most strategic objectives. The concept of 
"strategic" raw materials, added to the existing category of "critical" raw materials, shows the growing 
importance of strategic concerns when it comes to security of supply of raw materials.

The measures considered in the proposed Law on Critical Raw Materials, such as  support for access 
to financ e or the permitting timeframes for strategic projects, have long been demanded by the 
extractive sector and should be extremely useful to reinforce agility to face the challenges caused by 
climate change and the geopolitical situation.  The issue of public acceptance for raw materials supply 
should in our view also feature high in the CRMA.   
However, we regret that the definition of strategic materials and the scope assigned to strategic 
projects remain too narrow. As indicated above, the refurbishment of European buildings has rightly 
been identified as an important priority for the EU to ac hieve its climate-neutrality objective. In this 
regard, we strongly believe that this objective should feature high in the strategic considerations. it 
comes to defining raw materials for which the supply should be facilitated. 
We duly recognise that raw materials needed for batteries are critical for Europe to accompany the 
electrification of the energy mix. However, the current list of critical raw materials and the definition 
of strategic raw materials fail to consider minerals which will be decisive to perform the energy 
renovation of the building stock. 
Furthermore, we believe that focussing all efforts on critical and strategic raw materials as defined in 
the proposal will result in detrimental consequences as regards the processing of permits and the 
overall supply conditions of other materials, which are nevertheless essential to achieve the European 
Union’s policy goals.  
We therefore call on the EU institutions to amend the CRMA proposal so that such materials are 
considered strategic, or to introduce a new category of "essential" raw materials  that also benefit 
from simplified permits and access to finance, as required by the Essential Raw Materials Coalition2.  
Gypsum is the first raw material exported by Spain, mainly to the EU and accounts for 50% of the 
natural gypsum needs demanded by EU countries . The gypsum ore mined by our companies 
contributes to Europe's strategic autonomy.  The sector also considers it a crucial duty to use natural 
resources in the most efficient and environmentally sustainable way, and to minimize all externalities 
of our extractive, process and manufacturing activities. Our industry has also pioneered circular 
practices in the construc tion sector, with closed -loop recycling systems for plasterboard in several 
European countries, and recycling figures have gradually increased in recent years3.  
However, increasing difficulties in accessing mining permits, combined with the expected reduction 
of alternative sources of gypsum from flue gas desulphurisation from coal-fired power plants that are 
being shut down due to the energy transition, are raising serious concerns about the future supply of 
this essential mineral for building construction and renovation.  
Therefore, recognition of the strategic nature of minerals needed for the rehabilitation of the 
European building stock, including gypsum, as well as appropriate deadlines for permitting, would be 
particularly useful to ensure the long -term supply of national gypsum resources, locally and

Materiales para el Pacto Verde Europeo  
 
El Pacto Verde Europeo ha sido una brújula importante del liderazgo político europeo desde 2019. El 
compromiso de Europa con la neutralidad climática para 2050 ha hecho indispensable que la UE 
reajuste su legislación y proponga objetivos más ambiciosos par a reducir las emisiones de gases de 
efecto invernadero y mejorar la eficiencia energética. Paralelamente a las normas más estrictas para 
los edificios nuevos en virtud a la revisión de la Directiva de eficiencia energética de los edificios, la 
Comisión Europea identificó acertadamente la rehabilitación de los edificios existentes en Europa  
como una prioridad importante para la acción, con la ambición de duplicar las tasas de rehabilitación 
a lo largo de la década.1  
La agresión rusa a Ucrania ha sido un poderos o recordatorio de la necesidad vital de asegurar el 
suministro de energía de Europa. Es de suma importancia evitar las pérdidas  de energía, 
especialmente mediante la renovación de edificios, ya que todavía representan aproximadamente el 
35 % de las emisiones de gases de efecto invernadero relacionadas con la energía de Europa en 2020. 
Acelerar la rehabilitación de edificios y minimizar la huella de carbono de los edificios existentes y 
nuevos requiere soluciones de bajo contenido de carbono incorporado para edificios nuevos y 
existentes. Las materias primas como el yeso, que se obtienen principalmente en España, representa 
el 50% de las neces idades de  Europa, son un componente importante de las soluciones de 
construcción ligeras, bajas en carbono y energéticamente eficientes.  
ATEDY, la Asociación Técnica y Empresarial del Yeso, organización de ámbito nacional que representa 
a los suministradores y fabricantes de yesos, escayolas  y sus derivados, está comprometida con el 
objetivo de suministrar materias primas esenciales a las sociedades europeas de la manera más 
sostenible y contribuir sustancialmente a los objetivos del Pacto Verde Europeo.  
 
La presentación de una Ley de Materias Primas Críticas (CRMA) es un claro impulso para que la UE 
considere la importancia del suministro de materias primas para asegurar sus objetivos más 
estratégicos. El concepto de materias primas " estratégicas", añadido a la categoría existente de 
materias primas "críticas", muestra la creciente importancia de las preocupaciones estratégicas 
cuando se trata de la seguridad del suministro de materias primas.

Las medidas consideradas en la propuesta de Ley de Materias Primas Críticas, como el apoyo para el 
acceso a la financiación o la autorización de plazos para proyectos estratégicos, han sido demandadas 
durante mucho tiempo por el sector extractivo y deberían ser extremadamente útiles para reforzar la 
agilidad para enfrentar los desafíos causados por el cambio climático y la situación geopolítica.  En 
nuestra opinión , la cuestión de la aceptación pública del suministro de materias primas también 
debería ocupar un lugar destacado en la Ley de Materias Primas Críticas.  
Sin embargo, lamentamos q ue la definición de materiales estratégicos y el alcance asignado a los 
proyectos estratégicos sigan siendo demasiado estrechos. Como se ha indicado anteriormente, la 
rehabilitación de los edificios europeos se ha definido acertadamente como una prioridad importante 
para que la UE alcance su objetivo de neutralidad climática. En este sentido, creemos firmemente que 
este objetivo debe ocupar un lugar destacado en las consideraciones estratégicas cuando se trata de 
definir las materias primas para las que debe facilitarse el suministro.  
Reconocemos que las materias primas necesarias para las baterías son fundamentales para que 
Europa acompañe la electrificación de la combinación energ ética. Sin embargo, la lista actual de 
materias primas críticas y la definición de materias primas estratégicas no contemplan minerales que 
serán determinantes para realizar la rehabilitación energética del parque edificatorio.  
Además, creemos que centrar todos los esfuerzos  en las materias primas críticas y estratégicas, tal 
como se definen  en la propuesta, tendrá consecuencias perjudiciales en lo que respecta  a la 
tramitación de los permisos y a las condiciones generales de suministro de otros materiales, que, sin 
embargo, son esenciales para alcanzarlos objetivos políticos de la Unión Europea.    
Por lo tanto, pedimos a las instituciones de la UE que modifiquen la propuesta de CRMA para que 
dichos materiales se consideren estratégicos, o que introduzcan una nueva categoría de materias 
primas "esenciales" que también se beneficien de permisos simplificados y acceso a la financiación , 
como exige la Coalición de Materias Primas Esenciales2.  
El yeso es la primera materia prima que exporta España , principalmente a la UE y supone el 50% de 
las necesidades de yeso natural de demandan los países de la UE . El mineral de yeso extraído por 
nuestras empresas contribuye a la autonomía estratégica de Europa .  El sector también considera 
como un deber crucial utilizar los recursos naturales de la manera más eficiente y ambientalmente 
sostenible, y reduciendo al mínimo todas las externalidades de nuestras actividades extractivas, de 
proceso y fabricación. Nuestra industria también ha sido pionera en prácticas circulares en el sector 
de la construcción, con sistemas de reciclaje de circuito cerrado para placas de yeso en varios países 
europeos, y se incrementan paulatinamente las cifras de reciclaje en los últimos años3.  
Sin embargo, las crecientes dificultades en el acceso a los permisos de extracción, combinadas con la 
reducción prevista de fuentes alternativas de yeso a partir de la desulfuración de gases de combustión 
de las centrales térmicas de carbón que se están cerrando debido a la transición energética, están

 PRIMIGEA COMPLEMENTARY AMEDMENT SUGGESTIONS Preliminary comment The text of the regulation incorporates a series of measures that the sector fully supports, insofar as they will have positive effects on the raw materials industry.  Thus, the measures to speed up deadlines and procedures (Articles 8, 9, 10 and 11); planning and zoning (some) (12); online accessibility to administrative information (17); the implementation of national exploration programs (18); measures to avoid supply crises (19-22); measures to promote circularity (25-28); recognized certification systems (29); measures to ensure free circulation, conformity and market surveillance (section 3), are considered to be very positive with some exceptions that are analysed in this report and with the main point that some of them should be extended, with the necessary adaptations or exceptions, to all MPMs, including all their value chains.  That is why it is essential to broaden the scope of strategic raw materials or to define a third category (which would include all other RMs), for example, essential MPMs, to which most of the articles would not apply, but which would include some measures to improve access to resources and a less ambitious time limit for the processing processes. In addition to solving this serious problem that invalidates the proposal for 99% of the European RMs industry, the following needs for improvement should be mentioned: § The CRM Regulation is more a declaration of intent towards Member States than a Regulation with concrete and effective measures, with a very high-level wording and not very concrete. It is clearly a soft regulation, more akin to a Directive than a Regulation. Thus, the approach of the Regulation makes it weaker and less robust than the Net Zero Industrial Act, which makes it appear to be a subsidiary regulation. § The proposal seems to forget that it must be applied in 27 member states and more than 325 regions, with different legislations in force, different distribution of competences between states, regions and municipalities. In addition, it is very important to note that the territories are not virgin and that there is a multitude of pre-existing extractive rights in force, of rights in process, of limiting land use planning, etc.  § The administrative structures that would have to comply with Regulation are already saturated and overwhelmed by interminable permitting procedures. The prioritization of some projects over the rest would cause further delays for those excluded. This point is particularly important, since for this reason and the one described below, the proposal ceases to be neutral for 99% of the industry and becomes detrimental to it, which makes it unacceptable. There is a precedent derived from the prioritization of renewable energy projects. § Another critical problem derives from the lack of mechanisms to equitably resolve overlaps between pre-existing rights or with the permitting processes already initiated and new projects derived from the Regulation. § Both issues could be a source of conflicts in the territory that would be detrimental to the very objectives pursued by the Regulation due to the conflict judicialization that could be undertaken by the companies harmed in the exercise of their legitimate rights. § The proposal omits private or public-private research, does not offer any clear framework for it, and does not include measures against speculation, nor to adapt to the different legal frameworks of ownership of RMs resources in the different Member States, or to their internal organization and distribution of competences.  § Nor does it include mechanisms for compatibility with regulations that prevent access to resources (nature protection, ecological restoration, soil health, etc.).

Proposal on article 3 (3)  Regarding article 3 paragraph 3 “The Commission shall review and, if necessary, update the list of  strategic raw materials by [OP please insert: four years after the date of entry into force of this  Regulation], and every four years thereafter”  Proposal  “The Commission shall review and, if necessary, update the list of strategic raw materials by [OP please insert: four years after the date of entry into force of this Regulation], and every 4 four years thereafter the second year after entry into force of this Regulation and every two years thereafter.  Para 4 [new] By derogation from Paragraph 3 of this Article, the Commission shall add additional raw materials on the list upon request of the Board if the Board or the Commission detects while exercising the monitoring, stress testing and information obligations under Articles 19.1, 19.2 and 20 of this regulation changes in supply and access to specific raw materials that risk endangering the targets set out in Article 1.2.”  Justification:  If a supply risk arises, there is a need to be able to react quickly.

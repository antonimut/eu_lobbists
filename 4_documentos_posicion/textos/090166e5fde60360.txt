The BDE Federation of the German Waste, Water and Circular Economy Management Industry was founded in 1961 and is the Industrial and Employers' Association of the circular and resource economy. The member companies of the BDE represent 75 percent of the turnover generated by the private sector in the fields of “wastewater disposal”, “collection, treatment, disposal and recycling of waste”, as well as “elimination of environmental pollution and other waste management” in Germany. The approximately 750 members of the BDE represent the entire value chain of the circular economy and resource management sector. This ranges from the collection, sorting, and recycling of waste to the use of the raw materials and products obtained from the recycling process.  The BDE welcomes the proposal for a Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) presented by the European Commission on 16 March 2023 and supports its objectives to ensure the long-term supply of raw materials urgently needed for the green transition and the digital transformation, and to overcome the EU's dependencies on imports of such raw materials from politically difficult third countries. This is crucial in the wake of ever-increasing demand for critical and strategic raw materials needed primarily for the expansion of renewable energies, the digital industry, aerospace, defence, and the healthcare sector. These raw materials include, inter alia, lithium, cobalt and copper. Europe is currently heavily dependent on imports of critical raw materials from a few third countries, which often hold monopoly positions. In order to reduce such dependencies and make the European economy more sustainable while at the same time protecting Europe as an industrial location, it is essential to strengthen and diversify supply chains as well as the recycling of critical raw materials.  The circular economy management sector and especially the member companies of the BDE are ready to contribute to achieving these goals and increase the efforts for – as well as the investments in – the recycling and the recovery of strategic raw materials.   However, this requires the appropriate legal and economic framework. As one of the three pillars of the Green Deal Industrial Plan to strengthen the EU's resilience, the CRMA, together with the reform of the EU’s electricity market design and the Net Zero Industry Act (NZIA), is intended to ensure that a favourable regulatory environment is provided for climate-neutral industry and the competitiveness of the EU’s economy. In this sense, the Commission's proposal for a CRMA points into the right direction. Nevertheless, the BDE still sees some shortcomings and potential for improvement.   In the following, the BDE therefore takes the liberty of commenting on some points of the proposed Regulation and evaluating them from the point of view of companies in the private waste management, raw materials and circular economy sector.

1. Benchmarks to reach by 2030 with regard to strategic raw materials The Commission’s proposal distinguishes between so-called "critical" and "strategic" raw materials. "Critical" are the economically most important raw materials for which there is a supply risk. These are listed in Annex II, Section 1 of the proposal and include, for example, bauxite, copper, helium and magnesium. "Strategic raw materials" (Annex I, Section 1) are critical raw materials that have a particular strategic importance. This importance arises from their use in strategically important technologies for the green and digital transformation and for defence and space applications, from a projected significant imbalance between supply and demand, and from the difficulty of increasing their production. Strategic raw materials include, for example, lithium, cobalt, or copper. This distinction by the Commission between “critical” and “strategic” raw materials is significant, as it recognises the need to prioritise certain raw materials based on their particular importance, such as for digital transformation.  It is envisaged by the Commission that 10% of the EU's demand for such strategic raw materials will be produced from domestic mining in 2030. In addition, the further processing of strategic raw materials within the EU is to be increased to 40% of demand by 2030. Also in 2030, 15% of the annual demand for strategic raw materials is to come from secondary raw materials recycled within the EU. However, these targets are not binding quotas, but benchmarks. Nevertheless, their achievement should be strictly pursued in order to strengthen the resilience of the EU’s raw materials industry.   These benchmarks set by the Commission are ambitious, but to be welcomed. It should be positively emphasised that these quotas are, at this stage, only benchmarks. The fact that it is expressly desired to exceed these benchmarks ensures the necessary flexibility. However, the BDE points out that it will be difficult to achieve the targeted recycling rates, as the necessary recycling capacities are not yet available. Without special government incentives and subsidies (see points 3 and 4), the necessary investments will probably not be made. The market and the demand for recyclates will not develop so quickly that the market itself would provide sufficient incentives for investments and for the expansion of capacities to the extent necessary to achieve the targets.  2. Acceleration of permit-granting processes Complicated and, in particular, lengthy approval procedures represent a major obstacle to the rapid expansion and development of the necessary recycling capacities.  Therefore, the BDE very much welcomes, in principle, the simplification of procedures provided for in the Commission's proposal, in particular the limitation to a one-time extension of the permitting procedures by one month for strategic projects in the field of recycling, as well as the fictitious approval after the expiry of the deadline. The extension of the permit-granting processes by one month is only intended for exceptional cases that require special justification, for example due to the extraordinary size of a particular project. The fictitious approval creates planning security for companies in the waste management industry and exerts pressure upon the responsible authorities to rapidly carry out the permit-granting processes. The companies are thus given the perspective

that a final decision will have been made after 13 months at the latest, which provides for incentives to invest in strategic projects for the recycling of critical raw materials.  However, due to the urgency of expanding the necessary recycling infrastructures, a 13-month approval procedure still proves to be too long in the end. In Germany, for example, shorter processing periods of 7 months or 3 months (simplified procedure) already apply under the Federal Immission Control Act (Bundesimmissionsschutzgesetz – BImSchG), Article 10(6a) BImSchG.   In this context, however, it is problematic that the processing periods generally do not begin to run until all the documents required by the national competent authority have been submitted. In practice, the delays in the approval procedures usually result from the fact that the authorities successively request additional documents (e.g. noise protection reports, etc.), the provision of which is sometimes very time-consuming.   Therefore, the deadlines mentioned in Article 10 of the Commission's proposal should be shortened significantly, i.e. to 7 months for projects in the field of recycling of strategic raw materials, and an addition should be included which obliges the national competent authority to list all documents and information it considers necessary, and which states that this request constitutes the only possibility for the authority to request further information.  3. Lack of funding for strategic projects The Commission’s proposal is insufficient with regard to the financing of strategic projects. Much more concrete rules on the financing of strategic projects are needed. The proposed provisions of Article 15 lack any binding force, which is, however, urgently needed to achieve the ambitious and at the same time welcome targets of Article 1. The Member States alone will not be able to provide the necessary financial resources. Therefore, EU funds must also be made available if the CRMA is to serve as a response to the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), which itself provides funding of $369 billion for energy and climate protection projects. In addition, the EU Commission must quickly create the legal basis for state aid to enable the Member States to grant financial aid in a legally secure and generous manner. At present, the Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework (TCTF) constitutes the most suitable option for granting state aid. However, this framework generally only allows subsidies of 15% of eligible costs and requires that the subsidy must be granted no later than 31 December 2025.  The BDE therefore proposes to introduce a provision requiring the Commission  - to enable, in the short term, the financing of strategic projects through funds from the relevant Union funding and financing programmes, such as Horizon Europe or the Innovation Fund, and to adapt these programs for this purpose if necessary;  - to provide, in the short term, the legal framework that allows Member States to finance strategic projects through State aid within the meaning of Article 107 TFEU to a degree that

creates real incentives for investments that contribute to achieving the objectives of this Regulation.  4. Sustainable public procurement Sustainable public procurement should be fostered more consistently because it can be a highly effective tool for promoting the circular economy and green innovation. As noted in the Commission Communication of 15 May 2023 on a revised monitoring framework for the circular economy (COM(2023) 306 final), the EU spends around 14% of its GDP (around EUR 2 trillion per year) on the purchase of services and goods through public procurement.1 Against this background, the role of sustainable public procurement should be strengthened in the context of national circular economy policies (Article 25(1)(c)), for example by making it mandatory to take into account, in the award criteria, the share of recycled critical or strategic raw materials in goods to be procured. This would promote the market for secondary raw materials and stimulate investment in recycling capacities.  5. Provisions on the minimum recycled content in permanent magnets The proposal provides for the Commission’s right to make specifications on the recycled content in permanent magnets by means of delegated acts (Art. 28(3)). However, provisions on the recycled content of permanent magnets are essential to promote the recycling of critical or strategic raw materials. They are thus an essential provision within the structure of the CRMA and should therefore be part of the Regulation itself, in line with Article 290(1) TFEU. For this reason, the Commission should not be empowered to adopt delegated acts in this regard.   In addition, since it is not clearly foreseeable when the technologies and infrastructure for recycling strategic and critical raw materials will be sufficiently available, no specific date should be set for establishing targets for the recycled content of permanent magnets (Art. 28(3)).  6. Representation of the circular economy within the European Critical Raw Materials Board Moreover, the BDE proposes to introduce an additional permanent subgroup in the European Critical Raw Materials Board (Art. 34 and 35) dealing with the circularity of critical raw materials. This subgroup should bring together the waste management sector and relevant stakeholders. It should primarily deal with the development of recycling capacities, recycling targets, markets for secondary raw materials, provisions on minimum recycled content, but also with all other aspects related to the circularity of critical raw materials. Strengthening the circular economy as one of the pillars of the CRMA must necessarily be one of the core topics within the European Critical Raw Materials Board.

7. Improved monitoring of recycling capacities and capabilities Article 25 contains very positive and decisive provisions on mandatory national programmes to strengthen the circular economy, not least in paragraph 1, letter c) with regard to measures promoting the use of critical secondary raw materials in manufacturing. To ensure a continuous development of recycling infrastructures for critical raw materials, Art. 25 should be supplemented to the effect that national programmes on circularity also contain measures to improve the monitoring of existing capacities and capabilities for the recycling of critical raw materials in the respective Member State.  8. Phosphorus as a strategic raw material Finally, the BDE calls for phosphorus to be included in the list of strategic raw materials. As a raw material for fertilisers, phosphorus is essential for agriculture and food security. While global demand for phosphorus is steadily increasing, its availability is decreasing. Technologies for the recycling of phosphorus already exist, but there is an urgent need to increase recycling capacities in order to reduce the extraction of phosphorus as primary raw material, and thereby reduce the negative environmental impacts this extraction causes. The Commission's study on critical raw materials for the EU 2023 shows that the processing of phosphorus depends to 100% on imports.2  9. Conclusion Overall, the urgently needed and much awaited proposal for a Regulation on Critical Raw Materials correctly highlights that the only way to achieve EU independence regarding the supply of critical raw materials is to consistently improve the infrastructure for a European circular economy. Many of the proposed provisions point into the right direction.  It is particularly welcome that the EU-wide problem of too long permit-granting processes has been recognised. Nevertheless, a further shortening of the procedures as described is essential in order to advance the recycling of strategic raw materials as quickly as possible.   From the BDE’s point of view, the biggest shortcoming of the proposal is the lack of binding rules on financing. With respect to this, there is an urgent need for concretisation and sharpening of the corresponding provisions. Otherwise, there will not be sufficient incentives for investments in the expansion of recycling capacities, and the ambitious goals of Article 1 will not be achieved.   Looking ahead, a constant monitoring of the raw materials market will be necessary to be able to quickly react to changes in demand for certain critical raw materials. In addition, the main barriers to a stronger secondary raw materials market should be analysed, as well as the reasons why secondary raw materials are not already being used to a greater extent.

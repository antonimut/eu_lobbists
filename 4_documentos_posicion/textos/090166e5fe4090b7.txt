 
 
BASF welcomes the publication of the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) as it introduces a necessary 
set of measures to secure the sustainable implementation of the Twin Transition, as well as the 
resilience of many European frontrunning industries, including the chemical industry.  
We support in particular the ambitious 2030 production goals proposed in the Act, as well as the bold 
schemes for accelerating project permitting, the introduction of an EU-level framework to monitor 
critical materials’ value chains, and the ESG provisions. 
At BASF, we are committed to strengthen our supply chains and recycling processes of key raw 
materials.  
We aim at processing 250,000 metric tons of recycled and waste-based raw materials annually by 
2025, and we are particularly addressing battery recycling as an additional growth market, so as to 
ensure that valuable metals remain in the production cycle for as long as possible. At our 
Schwarzheide site in Germany, for instance, where a cathode active materials plant is already under 
construction, we have recently launched a new prototype plant for battery recycling so as to 
significantly increase recovery rates of lithium, nickel, cobalt, and manganese from end -of-life lithium-
ion batteries. 
This considered, we welcome the Commission’s proposals to streamline the permitting process so as 
to prevent avoidable delays for strategic sourcing, processing, and recycling projects. 
We however believe that such measures should have been accompanied by dedicated provisions on 
facilitated financing from EU and national tools, and on public funding opportunities, including via 
debt/equity investments in foreign projects with offtake of European industry players (e.g. Korea/Japan 
model). 
Such approach would have: 
- Prevented implementation issues/inconsistencies due to different funding capacities at 
Member State level (e.g. securing a speedy roll-out of all national one stop shops); 
- Secured direct R&D financing to support reformulation initiatives, and to optimize strategic raw 
materials’ use;  
- Partly balanced the international level playing field, now affected by increasingly subsidized 
competition (e.g. U.S. Inflation Reduction Act). 
 
Measures to secure horizontal regulatory alignment and coherence are unfortunately missing in the 
CRMA proposal.  
The current and upcoming (i.e. REACH revision) EU chemical legislative framework , for instance, 
severely impacts the investments’ certainty for critical raw materials’ recycling. Several strategic 
metals, such as lithium and cobalt, have to comply to oftentimes unpredictable changes i n 
classification and/or risk management procedures – which finally hinders investments’ flows into the 
sector. Failing to integrate the current EU’s legislative framework could thus potentially nullify the 
CRMA’s ambitions of creating sustainable value chains for CRMs in the EU.   
Looking ahead, we would welcome to see broader and horizontal impact assessments taking place 
when new legislative measures are introduced - especially when these are potentially impacting 
supply availability and EU products' competitiveness on global markets. 
In the aftermath of recent supply chain disruptions, high price volatilities and shortages, we’d like to 
see  more regular updates (e.g. every 2 years) of the current methodology used to define a “Critical 
Raw Material”. This would be important in order to better reflect current and expected dependencies, 
future demand increases, supply risks, and investment needs.  
Increasing the CRM list update timeline from 3 to 4 years, as proposed in the CRMA, will further limit 
the EU’s capacity to flexibly adapt to market trends, technological developments, and geopolitical 
changes affecting CRM supply. It additionally extends the exclusion of several crucial metals to the 
energy transition – like aluminium, silver, and zinc – from the current Strategic Raw Materials’ list, 
notwithstanding EU companies’ feedbacks to the 2022 consultation.

with increased reporting requirements. Whilst we understand the value in monitoring EU -level CRMs

supply, the proposal is set to impose on companies regular and extensive auditing, which is also not 
aligned with other already existing supply regulations (e.g. Due Diligence, Deforestation Free Supply 
Chain etc.).  
BASF, like many other companies, has a Supplier Financial Risk Monitoring system in place so as to 
quarterly assess critical raw materials’ value chains and mitigate potential supply risks/disruptions. 
The auditing exercise foreseen by the CRMA proposal therefore represents an unjustified duplicat ion 
of work, putting additional burden on companies in the EU which those in other international markets 
don’t have. 
The relevant CRMA provisions should thus be amended so to allow EU businesses to voluntarily 
provide all already available information to the European Commission. Such initiative should be 
carried out in alignment with national authorities, so as to centralize data collection and avoid the 
duplication of CRMs monitoring schemes within the EU.

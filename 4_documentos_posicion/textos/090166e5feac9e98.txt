The Foreign Direct Investment Screening Regulation (EU/2019/452) 
AEGIS Europe position  
 
Introduction  
AEGIS Europe appreciates the opportunity to give input in the context of the revision of the 
Foreign Direct Investment Screening Regulation. The European Union needs to ramp up its 
foreign direct investment screening mechanisms in order  to preserve Europe’s strategic 
autonomy and ensure a global level playing field. Hereinafter you may find the AEGIS Europe 
observations and suggestions to improve the existing legislative framework. 
The EU industrial sectors grouped within AEGIS Europe represent the entire value chain 
ranging from commodities such as ferro -alloys, non -ferrous metals or ceramics  to end 
products like railway equipment, bicycles or solar panels. Our members account for an annual 
turnover of €530 – €550 billion and 1.65 – 1.7 million of jobs across the EU.  
We believe t hat the FDI Screening Regulation should be revised bearing in mind  today’s 
geopolitical reality and in a context of reducing the EU’s dependency vis -à-vis certain 
countries in industries critical for the functioning of the internal market. 
AEGIS Europe has  always supported openness towards FDI since the EU industry relies on 
straightforward access to capital in order to innovate and grow. However, there was a lack of 
transparency as regards inward FDI into EU and the FDI Screening Regulation was adopted to 
tackle such issues – nevertheless there is room for further improvement, and we hope to 
contribute to it via the suggestions you may find below.   
1. Streamlining FDI mechanisms across all Member States  
According to the List of Screening Mechanisms notified by Member States (last updated on 
28 June 2023), only 19 have adopted and implemented a FDI screening mechanism. 
Furthermore, there are major discrepancies among the various mechanisms  since in some 
Member States such as France, Italy and Spain , FDI screening is fully mandatory while in 
Germany, Finland and Lithuania are only partially mandatory. In addition, there are also 
significant differences in terms of obligations, thresholds and sectors covered . This lack of 
unity makes the EU FDI screening system less effective and less efficient.  
Our first suggestion is that all Member States should adopt and implement an FDI screening 
mechanism that assesses whether foreign investments pose a threat to securi ty and public 
order.

Additionally, in view of increasing its effectiveness and efficiency, there should be more 
convergence and a screening obligation of FDIs taking over  “critical infrastructure” and 
“critical technologies” relevant for the transportation sector1, critical raw materials and net 
zero industries.  
Since several neighbouring countries  (Norway, Turkey and the UK) as well as Australia, 
Canada, China, India, Japan and the United States have also implemented FDI regimes , it 
appears rather obvious that a trend of screening FDIs in critical sectors,  which goes beyond 
the EU jurisdiction has been established.  
When streamlining national FDI screening mechanisms it is important to ensure that intra-EU 
investments are not made more cumbersome since some national FDI screening mechanisms 
also encompass FDI from within the EU  leading to unwelcome additional administrative 
burdens for European investors. 
2. Enhancing the role of the European Commission  
The fact that Member States and the European Commission are able to share information via 
the cooperation mechanism is very much appreciated. However, it appears essential to 
enhance the role of the European Commission and the latter should be entitled to issue 
opinions and assessments addressed to Member States competent authorities prior to their 
decisions when the FDI concerns “critical infrastructure” and “critical technologies” that could 
affect security and public order. This cooperation mechanism should be further developed so 
that it includes criteria and parameters facilitating the evaluation of the impact of a foreign 
direct investment. 
3. Assessing the origin of the FD investor  
In what concerns the origin of the entity or natural person making the investment, AEGIS 
Europe would like to draw the attention to two aspects. First, when assessing whether a FDI 
is likely to affect security or public order it is of utmost importance to look into situations 
where a foreign investor is directly or indirectly controlled by the government, including state 
bodies or armed forces, of a third country, including through ownership structure or 
significant funding. We believe that this FDI Regulation provision should be taken over by all 
FDI mechanisms set out at national level. Furthermore, FDIs made by foreign investors that 
are owned or controlled by a third country can unlevel the playing field within the internal 
market as they will not be subject to the same rigorous competition and especially State aid 
rules as their European counterparts.  
Second, AEGIS Europe considers that investments from within the Union by means of artificial 
arrangements that do not reflect economic reality and circumvent the screening mechanisms 
and screening decisions, where the investor is ultimately owned or controlled by a natural 
person, or an undertaking of a third country  should also make the object of FDI screening . 
Thus, an assessment should take place not only when the direct investor is either a natural 
person or an undertaking from a third country, but also when the direct investor is established

4. Screening of FDIs dealing with projects and programmes of Union interest  
According to the current Regulation, the European Commission has the possibility to screen 
FDIs in the context of projects or programmes of the Union interest, as it is the current c ase 
of the Trans -European Networks for Transport (TEN -T) and Horizon 2020 (now Horizon 
Europe) framework programme for research and innovation.  
However, the screening of direct investments should go beyond TEN -T and Horizon Europe. 
In fact, according to t he recent European Commission’s Communication on “European 
Economic Security Strategy”, is it essential to prevent EU-funded technology leakage and the 
Commission, in justified cases, may exclude certain third country entities or EU entities 
controlled by certain third countries from participating in research and innovation. 
Therefore, AEGIS Europe wishes to extend the scope of projects and programmes of Union 
interest to be subject to FDI screening, in particular in the context of the upcoming 
Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) 2028 -2034 covering all the EU programmes that 
could potentially support critical infrastructure or critical technologies 2. Additionally, the 
European Structural and Investment Funds (ESIF) where, thanks to the Cohesion Fund, the 
European Regional and Development Fund and the Just Transition Fund, Member States also 
allocate EU funding to boost investments in such critical infrastructure or technologies.  
Given the strategic importance of the newly created IPCEIs (Important Project s of Common 
European Interest), the possibility of screening FDI s across them should also be granted. 
Furthermore, given past experience of EU programmes that have been created with a 
temporary nature off -the-MFF ( i.e., NextGenerationEU), applying FDI scre ening in critical 
infrastructure projects financed under these potential new programmes or instruments is 
very pertinent. Finally, the European Investment Bank should also play an active role in the 
context of these projects and programmes of Union interest and support EU businesses. 
5. Reciprocity and ensuring a level playing field  
The FDI Regulation should be consistent with the EU’s trade toolbox i.e. the recently adopted 
International Procurement Instrument (IPI) and Foreign Subsidies Regulation. Whereas the IPI 
Regulation takes a strong stance on reciprocity in the field of public procurement in order to 
support the opening of international procurement markets and creating a robust leverage, 
the Foreign Subsidies Regulation intends to tackle challenges re lated to unfair competition 
(i.e. subsidies coming from foreign State-Owned Enterprises that operate in the EU market on 
a distortive basis). Therefore, it would be critical to have a reciprocity check to assess 
openness towards investment with in the third country from which the FDI originates 
targeting the EU market.

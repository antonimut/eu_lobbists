Introduction  
The European Rail Supply Industry Association (UNIFE) welcomes the opportunity to express its view on the EU’s Foreign 
Direct Investment Screening Regulation (EU/2019/452).  
 
UNIFE represents more than 115 of Europe’s leading rail supply companies – from SMEs to major industrial champions – 
active in the design, manufacture, maintenance and refurbishment of rail transport systems, subsystems and related 
equipment. UNIFE also brings together na tional rail supply industry associations from  12 European countries.  Our 
members account for 84% of the European, and 46% of the global, market for rail equipment and services.  
 
UNIFE supported the European Commission’s initiative to establish a FDI Screening Regulation as a way to foster 
countries to further evaluate foreign investments/non -EU related to security and public order, in particular when it comes 
to critical infrastructure. Moreover, in the past years, foreign direct investment has significantly increased and the rail sector 
has been identified by several countries as a strategic sector (for instance, rail equipment is one of the ten manufacturing 
sectors identified in the ‘Made in China 2025’ strategy). At the same time, the recent OECD study on measuring distortions 
in the rolling stock value chain also demonstrates clearly the several policies and non -EU government intervention that 
affects competition. 
 
The FDI Screening Regulation should be evaluated and revised taking into account its alignment with today’s geopolitical 
reality and in a context of reducing the EU’s dependency vis-à-vis certain countries in  certain industries. Furthermore, it is 
important to remember that the transportation sector is already part of the category of “critical infrastructure”. Considering 
the role that efficient transportation networks play for our societies and our economies, transportation industries shall be 
considered as “critical technologies” and thus properly analyzed taking into account the impact on security and public order 
at national and European levels. 
 
While it is of primary importance to maintain the EU as an attractive destination for foreign direct investment, UNIFE c alls 
on the European Commission to increase the effectiveness of the current FDI Screening Regulation by introducing a 
number of changes in view of a possible upcoming revision, as follows. In doing so, the EU should focus on those 
investments that pose a security or public order risk and prevent to generally make FDI more cumbersome through longer 
procedures or higher bureaucratic burdens. 
 
I – Implement and promote convergence of FDI mechanisms across all 
Member States, including screening for “critical i nfrastructure” and 
“critical technologies” / transportation sector: 
For the time being, it appears that only 19 Member States have implemented a screening regime, although some additional 
FDI regimes are expected during 2023. The national screening mechanisms differ substantially from one Member State to 
another no only in terms of obligations, thresholds and sectors covered, but also in terms of whether or not these screening 
mechanisms are fully mandatory (e.g. France, Italy and Spain) or partly mandatory (e.g. Finland, Germany and Lithuania). 
 
In order to properly assess foreign direct investments that poses a threat to security and public order, it is imperative that 
all 27 Member States implement a screening mechanism as soon as possible. The European Commission should support 
Member States in this regard.  
 
Additionally, in view of increasing its effectiveness and efficiency, there should be more convergence and a screening 
obligation as regards “critical infrastructure” and “critical technologies” rela ted to the transportation sector, such as the 
acquisition of companies manufacturing rolling stock and components, rail infrastructure and signaling and control 
management technologies (i.e. ERTMS). Currently, some Member States that have established a scr eening mechanism 
do not include in their scope the transportation sector (i.e. Bulgaria, Croatia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland and Latvia).  
 
Moreover, special attention should be given to specific situations of the acquisition by foreign investors of nationa l 
accreditation bodies : these companies have access to critical homologation and certification information, as well as to

important information about the design of critical rail technologies and products they certify. Thus, they should be in the 
scope of screening mechanisms. Their acquisition can be a way for foreign/non -EU investors to access know-how linked 
to in particular sectors that are at the cutting edge of EU technological developments, which ultimately can pose security 
and public order challenges.   
 
Finally, it is also worth noting that several neighbouring countries also have implemented FDI regimes which demonstrates 
the trend that has been established beyond the EU jurisdiction in order to screening foreign direct investments in critical 
sectors and industries, as it is the case in Norway, Turkey and the UK. Other countries have also established screening 
mechanisms, for instance Australia, Canada, China, India, Japan and the United States. 
 
In strengthening national FDI screening mechanisms it is important to ensure that intra-EU investments are not made more 
cumbersome. Many national FDI screening mechanisms also encompass FDI from within the EU. Such practices lead to 
additional administrative burdens for European investors.    
 
II – Strengthen the role of the European Commission  
The cooperation mechanism establishes a good basis for information sharing across the Member States and with the 
European Commission and foster the parties to evaluate its investments. However, it would be essentia l to increase its 
effectiveness and strengthen the role of the European Commission by having opinions/assessments addressed to all 
Member States when it comes to “critical infrastructure” and “critical technologies” that affects security and public order. It 
would be also essential that Member States react to the European Commission’s opinions/assessments prior to any 
decision on whether or not accepting foreign investments related to “critical infrastructure” and “critical technologies”. 
 
Moreover, when foreign investments related to “critical infrastructure” and “critical technologies” is deemed to affect either 
one single Member State or more than one Member State, the assessments of such foreign investments should be made 
by the Member States concerned and by the European Commission through the cooperation mechanism and possibly with 
defined criteria/parameters to facilitate the evaluation on the impact of a foreign direct investment (for example part of the 
information to be communicated could be the lis t of the main customers and suppliers of the company being acquired in 
each Member State).  
 
 
III – Look at the ultimate origin of the investor  
When determining whether a foreign direct investment is likely to affect security or public order, it is of upmost importance 
to look into situations where a foreign investor is directly or indirectly controlled by the government, including state bodies 
or armed forces, of a third country, including through ownership structure or significant funding. UNIFE believes that this 
current provision that is included in the FDI Regulation should be applied in all FDIs at the national level. 
 
The acquisition of European rail supply companies by foreign investors that are owned or controlled by a State can create 
two classes of competitors on the European market and put European companies at a disadvantage as they are bound by 
market and EU competition policy rules (especially on State Aid), while their foreign competitors are not. Moreover, foreign 
investors that are owned or controlled by the State of a third country may seek to acquire control of or influence in European 
undertakings and use these assets to the detriment not only of the EU’s technological edge but also its security and public 
order.  
 
This is particularly important for the rail sector as the largest global railway manufacturer CRRC (China Railway Rolling 
Stock Corporation) is the only large rolling-stock manufacturer that is a state-owned and -controlled, according to a recent 
study of the OECD ‘Measuring Distortions in the Rolling Stock Value Chain’.1

IV –  Increase the scope of projects and programmes of Union interest  
In the current Regulation, the European Commission has the possibility to screen direct investments i n the context of 
projects or programmes of the Union interest, as it is the current case of the Trans-European Networks for Transport (TEN-
T) and the Horizon 2020 (now the Horizon Europe) framework programme for research and innovation.  
 
However, the screening of direct investments should go beyond TEN-T and Horizon Europe. In fact, according to the recent 
European Commission’s Communication on “European Economic Security Strategy”2, is it essential to prevent EU-funded 
technology leakage and the Commissi on, in justified cases, may exclude certain third country entities or EU entities 
controlled by certain third countries from participating in research and innovation. 
 
Therefore, UNIFE wishes to extend the scope of projects and programmes of Union interest to be screening against foreign 
investments, in particular in the context of the  upcoming Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) 2028 -2034 covering all 
the EU programmes that could potentially support rail. Notably, the new Connecting Europe Facility (CEF)  III, which will 
mostly support rail infrastructure for the completion of the TEN -T and other interoperable technologies such as the 
European Rail Traffic Management System (ERTMS). Additionally, the European Structural and Investment Funds (ESIF) 
where, thanks to the Cohesion Fund, the European Regional and Development Fund and the Just Transition Fund, Member 
States also allocate EU funding to boost investments in rail infrastructure, including at the urban level, rolling stock, 
signalling systems and alternative fuels solutions.  
 
Given the strategic importance of the newly created IPCEIs (Important Projects of Common European Interest), the 
possibility of screening FDI across them should also be granted. Furthermore, given past experience of EU programme s 
that have been created with a temporary nature off -the-MFF (i.e. NextGenerationEU), applying FDI screening in key 
transport infrastructure projects financed under these potential new programmes or instruments is very pertinent.  Finally, 
the European Investment Bank should also play an active role in the context of these projects and programmes of Union 
interest and support EU businesses.  
 
 
V –  Introduce reciprocity and address level playing field 
The FDI Regulation should be consistent with EU trade pol icies, in particular the recent adopted EU’s trade autonomous 
tools, such as the International Procurement Instrument and the Foreign Subsidies Regulation. Whereas the IPI Regulation 
takes a strong stance on reciprocity in the field of public procurement i n order to support the opening of international 
procurement markets and creating a robust leverage, the Foreign Subsidies Regulation intends to tackle challenges related 
to unfair competition (i.e. subsidies coming from foreign State -Owned Enterprises that  operate in the EU market on a 
distortive basis). Therefore, it would be fundamental to have a reciprocity check to assess investment openness with the 
country from which the investment is originated in the EU.  
 
In third countries, EU rail suppliers face many barriers when trying to do investments and many requirements going beyond 
the regulatory framework (i.e. de facto requirements) are applied: joint venture requirements, transfer of technology, among 
others. Overall, investments in the EU shall be asse ssed based on the reciprocal basis and contribute to a level playing 
field.

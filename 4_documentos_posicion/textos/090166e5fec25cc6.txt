evaluation and possible revision of the current EU

Consultation response from the In-House Competition Lawyers’ Association 
 
ICLA welcomes the opportunity to comment before the European Commission (EC) will present a 
report to the European Parliament and Council evaluating the functioning and effectiveness of 
Regulation (EU) 2019/452 (“EU FDI Regulation”). ICLA members have first-hand experience in 
coordinating investment screenings and advising their respective internal teams on legal, reputational 
and policy matters in relation to investment screening laws and regulations. 
 
ICLA is an informal association of inhouse competition lawyers with more than 500 members across 
the globe. The Association does not represent companies but is made up of individuals who are 
inhouse experts in competition law. This paper represents the position of ICLA and does not necessarily 
represent the views of all its individual members.

1. Introduction 
1. We recognize that national security and public order are of utmost importance to EU Member 
States. We also believe that cross-border investments can make a significant contribution to 
national security and public order. Investments ensure sufficient availability of capital for 
innovation and growth and foster global cooperation. Investments can also open new markets for 
European companies. It is for these reasons that investments into the EU need to be encouraged, 
not discouraged. 
2. The evaluation of the EU FDI Regulation comes at a crucial moment: 
 Regulatory red tape associated with operating in the EU and associated costs and impact 
on competitiveness increasingly concern businesses of all sizes. 
 The period where private capital was abundant due to low interest rates has come to an 
end, with early-stage and growth companies increasingly in global competition over a 
smaller and more risk-averse pool of capital. 
 Companies also need access to foreign capital to grow, in particular companies in the EU 
where equity markets are historically smaller than in the United States, for example.1 
 Political alliances have changed, and certain events show that national security, in 
particular in relation to critical infrastructure, can be instrumentalized to demonstrate 
political power. 
3. To promote investments in the EU, the Commission should evaluate how a revised and updated 
FDI screening regime can minimize the costs arising from investment restrictions, while preserving 
its policy goals. We believe that an updated FDI screening regime should target the most 
formidable threats to national security in areas of critical technology, critical infrastructure, and 
other sensitive areas, while ensuring openness and predictability in all other areas of foreign 
investment. 
4. Against this backdrop, we identify three core areas for improving the FDI screening regime in the 
EU: 
 Alignment of Member State laws 
 Transparency and collaboration 
 Administrative efficiency and best practices 
2. Alignment of Member State laws 
5. We acknowledge that it is the sole responsibility of Member States to safeguard their national 
security, as laid down in the EU Treaties. That said, we believe that closer alignment between 
Member States on the design of their screening regimes could materially simplify the foreign 
investment screening process. This could make investing in the EU more attractive without 
compromising the Member States’ ability to screen for national security risks. 
6. We encourage the European Commission to evaluate alignment specifically in the following areas:

2.1. “Foreign Investor” definition 
Background 
7. The concept of “foreign investor” is the starting point for any FDI filing analysis. Unlike in the UK, 
where the screening regime applies equally to UK and foreign investors alike, in the EU almost 
exclusively cross-border investments trigger an approval requirement (the only exceptions we are 
aware of being The Netherlands, Italy, and Sweden). Article 2(2) of the EU FDI regulation stipulates 
that an undertaking of any third country is a foreign investor. However, EU Member States are not 
bound by this definition and have developed their own legal tests and, in certain cases, have 
extended the FDI screening process even to investors of EU origin: 
Examples: In Austria, a foreign investor is defined as a non-EU, non-EEA, or non-Swiss investor. 
In the Czech Republic, a foreign investor is generally defined as any investor with non-EU 
ultimate beneficial ownership. In Poland, a foreign investor is defined as a non-EEA/non-OECD 
entity or natural person. In France, every non-French investor is generally caught, but lower 
shareholding thresholds apply in case of a non-EU/EEA investor. In Italy, the Golden Power Law 
and relevant approval requirements may also apply to Italian and EU persons depending on 
the relevant sector and type of transaction. 
Challenge 
8. Even an EU-incorporated and controlled investor might need to seek approval and suspend a 
transaction if the target has a relevant presence in jurisdictions such as Italy or France. Requiring 
FDI approvals for a broad range of intra-EU investments can greatly hinder capital movement 
within the EU. 
9. Different definitions of “foreign investor” can also lead to arbitrary outcomes regarding which 
regulator has to be notified of a transaction. The filing position may not reflect where the 
substantive concern lies. 
Example: Consider a situation where a Swiss investor makes an investment into a pan-
European business. This may technically trigger a filing in the Czech Republic due to its wider 
concept of “foreign investor”, even though the sensitive parts of the business may 
predominantly be in Austria, for example. The transaction would not be notifiable in Austria – 
where the sensitive parts of the business reside –, but in the Czech Republic, where only 
ancillary operations exist. 
Proposed solution 
10. The EC and the Member States should evaluate whether the “foreign investor” definition can be 
aligned. For instance, Member States might wish to maintain flexibility solely for investments into 
highly sensitive sectors, such as the narrowly defined defence sector. For all other investments 
subject to review, a common concept of 'foreign investor' excluding all investors with EU ultimate 
beneficial ownership and as suggested later in this paper, a unified definition of control should 
apply.

2.2. Aligning review timelines 
Background 
11. The EC notes substantial variations in actual timelines across Member States.2 Our members, too, 
have observed considerable discrepancies in the duration of both Phase 1 and Phase 2 reviews. 
Furthermore, delays can arise even during pre-notification and before the official 
acknowledgement of a filing as complete. 
Challenge 
12. Transactions, such as mergers, acquisitions, or venture capital funding rounds, are almost always 
negotiated and completed under time pressure due to the following reasons:  
 The benefits of a transaction often depend on favourable market conditions which can 
change rapidly.  
 Transactions are often financed by debt or mezzanine capital, and these financing 
arrangements usually have time-bound terms.  
 Pending M&A transaction can disrupt management and affect employee morale. 
 The longer a deal takes to close, it is more difficult, e.g., for the seller to maintain the target 
as it was when the transaction was signed. It is also more likely that the valuation of the 
companies involved will change and that the positive effects of deals may be reduced 
(through uncertainty and delay giving rise to management challenges, staff turnover, etc.). 
This can result in renegotiations and potentially derail the transactions. 
13. Unpredictable timeframes naturally deter transactions where time is of essence, including project-
specific funding, venture capital rounds, or companies in distress. Particularly harmful are those 
reviews that extend beyond the agreed long-stop date, which often means that the transaction 
needs to be renegotiated to rescue it.  
14. Importantly, our members have observed that even when investors cooperate fully during the 
review process and provide the requested information in a timely fashion, significant delays can 
still arise. These delays might be due to difficulties in obtaining a Minister's signature, infrequent 
committee meetings, or slow responses from Ministry specialists. Opaque decision-making 
processes often do not allow the parties to identify the precise cause for the delay. 
Proposed solution 
15. The EU should encourage Member States to implement statutory “waiting periods” for both Phase 
1 reviews (preliminary reviews) and Phase 2 reviews (in-depth investigations). Like merger control 
reviews under the EU Merger Regulation, once such waiting period has expired without the 
authority adopting a negative decision, the transaction should be deemed cleared and be allowed 
to proceed. Such periods should also be proportionate (and EU Merger Regulation timelines are a 
good benchmark here), since even for national regimes that have Phase 1 and Phase 2 reviews, 
currently, basic Phase 1 review periods can be as long as 90 days, which is a manifestly 
disproportionate period for most filings to be delayed pending review.  
16. Since national security assessments can be complex and not all relevant information may be readily 
available, authorities should have the power to “stop the clock”, in exceptional cases only. 
However, the overall duration of an investment review process should then be capped. To ensure

2.3. Substantive assessment 
Background 
17. There is no common methodology as to how to screen transactions for national security risks.  
Article 4(1) of the EU FDI Regulation lists proposed areas of increased sensitivity and Article 4(2) 
lists proposed threat factors. However, these lists are non-binding on Member States and even 
where national laws resemble those lists, interpretation by the national authorities (and ultimately 
the courts) can diverge over time. 
18. In addition, under Article 6 of the EU FDI Regulation, the substantive test for the assessment of an 
investment is whether the investment is “likely to affect security or public order” in one or more 
Member States. This standard appears to be significantly lower than the test under the EU 
jurisprudence relating to the freedom of establishment which refers to a “genuine and sufficiently 
serious threat” to “one of the fundamental interests of society”.   
Challenge 
19. Diverse perspectives on national security threats can result in regulatory fragmentation across the 
EU, impacting transaction certainty and potentially leading to divergent economic outcomes for 
Member States. Countries with ‘more lenient’ investment screening policies may attract more 
foreign investment, creating incentives for opportunistic use of FDI screening rules. In addition, a 
loose substantive test can result in unjustified scrutiny, disproportionate information gathering 
and over-enforcement. 
Proposed solution 
20. A transparent and precise EU-wide understanding of national security and public order interests 
could improve legal certainty for investors and benefit the whole EU by increasing its attractiveness 
as a destination for foreign investment. Such alignment would also prevent new forms of hidden 
protectionism and minimize opportunities for regulatory arbitrage. 
21. As already discussed in relation to trigger events, we believe the EU should seriously explore with 
Member States whether a revised EU FDI Regulation should provide for a list of areas where 
security screening is recommended. These would be areas that represent common core security 
interests. Outsides those common core security interests, a revised EU FDI Regulation should 
provide for a list of “opt-in” sectors where Member States may, but do not have to, identify their 
own specific national security risks and filing requirements. National security screening beyond 
these two categories of sectors would be discouraged. In addition, Member States should be 
required to narrowly define those sector lists and report on the interpretation of the notion of 
security and public order as applied to these areas in the context of their annual reporting under 
Article 5 of the EU FDI regulation. 
2.4. Triggering events 
Background  
22. The EU FDI Regulation does not prescribe what types of transactions, and investments into which 
sectors or areas of activities, trigger an FDI review. The EU FDI Regulation provides an indicative 
list of sectors (Article 4) that are deemed sensitive and where investments resulting in foreign 
control could result in a threat to national security. However, this list is not binding on the Member

States and many Member States have their own lists of sensitive sectors that only loosely align 
with the EU position. 
23. The EU FDI Regulation also makes no stipulations, nor does it give any recommendations, as to the 
size of the transaction or the acquired rights or influence that warrant a review. This is left to the 
determination of the Member States. 
Challenge 
24. Differences in the definition of triggering events can result in filing positions that do not reflect 
where the substantive concern lies. 
Example: An investment into a pan-European business which operates a subsidiary in Spain 
valued above EUR 1 million can attract a mandatory FDI filing in Spain. This filing requirement 
would arise irrespective of the activities of the Spanish business if the non-EU/EFTA acquirer 
fulfils certain criteria (e.g., state-ownership, pre-existing investments in sensitive sectors in the 
EU). This means that a sales and marketing subsidiary in Spain could trigger a mandatory filing 
there, even though the critical manufacturing capabilities are in Denmark, for example, where 
a filing may not be triggered due to the lack of sensitivity of the target’s operations in general. 
25. Counter-intuitive jurisdictional positions can also occur in the following situations: 
 When the triggering event is the acquisition of a certain shareholding (e.g., 10%, 20%, 25%), 
and the required shareholding level in the Member State where the target is 
headquartered is higher than in the other Member States where the target operates; 
 When the triggering event is the acquisition of control and the control definition follows a 
domestic standard (e.g., France); or 
 When filing thresholds in one Member State include transaction size or asset value tests 
(e.g., Spain, Belgium), whereas in other EU Member States they do not. 
26. In the case of investments into pan-European businesses, such divergence of rules may also result 
in the broadest regime determining whether a transaction’s closing is suspended. This can 
inadvertently result in effectively tighter rules than originally intended by each individual Member 
State. 
Example: In its 2021 reform of FDI screening rules, Germany consciously chose to exclude 
typical VC investment structures from screening by setting a relatively high shareholding 
threshold (20%) for investments into most covered sectors.3 However, if the German company 
established a presence in another EU country with a lower triggering threshold, this policy could 
effectively be undermined, as an approval requirement and ban on closing would arise 
regardless. 
Proposed solution 
27. The EC and EU Member States should evaluate whether the triggering events and thresholds can 
be aligned. While Member States may wish to maintain flexibility for investments into highly 
sensitive sectors, such as a narrowly defined defence sector, for all other investments subject to 
review we believe the EC should seriously explore with Member States whether alignment can be 
achieved in the following ways:

 A revised EU FDI Regulation should create a list of sectors that need minimum security 
screening. These would be sectors that represent common core security interests and/or 
where there are significant cross-border (intra-EU) activities so that a harmonised 
approach in terms of triggering events lead to the Member States most affected by (the 
security aspects of) a transaction being all able to review it. Otherwise, a revised EU FDI 
Regulation should provide for a list of “opt-in” sectors where Member States can, but do 
not have to, identify their own specific national security risks and filing requirements. 
National security screening beyond these two categories of sectors would be discouraged. 
 Shareholding thresholds should ideally be consistent across the EU (e.g., 20% of voting 
rights) and potentially could be set differently for different sectors (e.g., 15% for narrowly 
defined defence sector). References to domestic corporate law, e.g., “blocking minorities”, 
would lead to further fragmentation and hence should be avoided. 
 Other triggering events should be consistently defined, e.g., based on the concept of 
“control” in the EU Merger Regulation (EUMR) which benefits from a body of significant 
jurisprudence and although requiring a qualitative assessment would still assist in terms 
of legal certainty. 
 Mere sales without a local presence should not trigger jurisdiction. 
 Intra-group reorganisations or restructurings should generally not trigger a review if there 
is no change in ultimate control. 
 Member States should in any event be required as part of their annual reporting under 
Article 5 of the EU FDI regulation to include guidance regarding the interpretation and 
application of their national legislation (e.g., what does or does not constitute critical 
infrastructure in connection with energy; any notion falling short of EUMR control which 
is not entirely based on a specific level of shareholding or voting rights such as a ‘qualifying 
holding’ under the draft Irish FDI legislation).  
3. Predictability of FDI screening outcomes 
28. Experienced global investors consider the risks associated with FDI screening when making 
decisions. If the FDI screening outcome is negative (e.g., disapproval, clearance with unacceptable 
conditions, significant delays), it can harm their reputation and consequently their ability to find 
new customers, suppliers, investment targets or partners. Therefore, it is the responsibility of the 
investor’s/acquirer’s management to weigh these risks. If the management is unable to anticipate 
these risks well, they might choose not to invest. While external advisors may be able to provide 
context and advice, because of their fiduciary duties, managers/board members ultimately need 
to balance benefits and risks of a transaction themselves and take a decision based on the best 
available information. We believe that the EU FDI screening system could be more transparent and 
collaborative, to promote investments into the EU. 
3.1. Transparency in decision-making 
Background  
29. We understand that only a few Member States (e.g., Finland) make non-confidential versions of 
decisions available to the public or interested third parties. That does not mean that no 
information becomes available in other Member States. There have been numerous instances 
where at least some information has become available, either because a party involved had to 
make disclosures on stock exchanges or because information was deliberately or inadvertently 
leaked to the press.

Challenge 
30. Investors have difficulties assessing both the likelihood and the probable outcome of FDI 
interventions risk due to a lack of data on Member States’ practice and situations of particular 
sensitivity. Sporadic availability of some data hardly allows for a systematically sound assessment 
of FDI risks. This can particularly discourage sophisticated and prudent investors with strong 
governance and risk systems. Controlled disclosure of FDI screening outcomes to the public could 
be highly beneficial without meaningfully compromising national security or public order.  
31. Furthermore, without access to past decisions legal counsels’ ability to effectively advise their 
clients is impaired. Legal advisors rely on reports of previous practice to predict future ones. Hence, 
a certain degree of transparency in authorities’ decision-making appears crucial for the area FDI 
law to develop and mature. 
32. Article 5 of the EU FDI Regulation requires Member States to submit annual reports on foreign 
direct investments and the application of their screening mechanisms. While the report is a 
valuable resource for understanding investment flows into the EU, the availability of data only in 
an aggregated format does not allow for an assessment of the practices of specific Member States. 
However, investors need to assess FDI screening risks at the level of individual Member States, not 
at the EU level, as the Member States are the ultimate decision-makers. This necessitates access 
to disaggregated information at the country level. 
Proposed solution  
33. Member States should be required to report at least annually on the types of cases they have 
screened, scenarios in which in-depth investigations were initiated, and what the outcomes were. 
Transparency would also be helpful with respect to countries of origin of applicants to understand 
which countries might trigger more scrutiny than others. Where the authority imposes remedies, 
these should be described with a sufficient level of detail. Parties’ names as well as information on 
the specific transaction proposal do not have to be disclosed if there are justifiable grounds for not 
disclosing them. Such reports would not in any way prejudice the authority’s ability to treat 
different cases differently and to take into account the specific (often confidential) fact-pattern in 
the specific case at hand.  
34. Such limited disclosures could avoid inconsistencies in decision-making, by providing benchmarks, 
aid in maintaining consistent standards, and foster the development of FDI law by offering useful 
references for external counsel, companies, and authorities. 
3.2. Communication between the authorities and the parties 
Background 
35. Our members have noticed significant variations in how authorities engage with the parties during 
the review process. While some authorities have actively engaged with the parties, including 
through face-to-face meetings at different stages of the review, there have been instances where 
the review process has been prolonged without any explanation for the delay. Our members have 
experienced that in-person meetings, e.g., in Austria and Germany were only offered during in-
depth investigation phases, which made it difficult for the applicants to understand where the 
concerns have been and where information was needed to create a better understanding of the 
nature of the business subject to the transaction.  
Challenge 
36. Lack of clear communication from the authorities to the parties can lead to unnecessary delays or 
even potential derailment of transactions. It also undermines the Parties’ rights of due process and

participation to defend their application and to mitigate concerns Member States might have. 
Parties who are under pressure to close, require optionality and where there is no end of the 
review in sight and insufficient information is being disclosed about its status, parties may have to 
engage in the planning and execution of alternative structures, or abandon the transaction 
altogether. In our members’ experience, communication from national authorities on the 
substantive direction of investigations is virtually non-existent until almost immediately before (or 
after) a decision is made (e.g., whether to extend the investigation or clear the transaction), which 
makes planning for outcomes and suitable remedies difficult. 
Proposed solution 
37. A reform of the EU FDI Regulation should include a guarantee that parties are being adequately 
heard on potential national security concerns as early as possible and regularly throughout the 
process. Since there is no “all knowing State”, hearing out the parties on potential national security 
concerns, in particular the company closely involved with the technology and its applications, 
provides a more comprehensive understanding of potential security risks, assisting the 
government's fact-finding process and enabling more informed decision-making. 
38. This should particularly be the case where concerns are raised as a result of the cooperation 
mechanism set up by the EU FDI Regulation by a Member State where the transaction is not being 
reviewed. Member States where the transaction is being reviewed should be required to disclose 
the origin of any request for information or question raised and provide parties the possibility to 
be heard or provide comments in response. In some jurisdictions, comments from Member States 
or an opinion by the EC also trigger the extension of the review period – the parties should be 
aware of events that trigger such extensions. 
39. Where an authority needs to withhold information due to its classified nature or for other national 
security or confidentiality reasons, the authority should be obliged to substantiate its 
confidentiality claims and share that assessment with the parties.  
3.3. Other aspects of collaboration 
Cooperation between Member States 
40. We consider the cooperation mechanism under the EU FDI Screening Regulation generally works 
well, provided Member States and the EU Commission submit opinions and comments to the 
Member State swiftly and in any case within the statutory timeframe of 35 calendar days. Two 
aspects to potentially improve are the following: (i) there is no fixed deadline for Member States 
to notify other Member States and the Commission of a foreign investment which is being 
reviewed in their jurisdiction under the cooperation mechanism, and that (ii) in multi-jurisdictional 
deals, the same transaction will be fed through the cooperation mechanism multiple times, 
providing renewed opportunities and more time for other Member States and the Commission to 
opine on the transaction. The timelines should be only triggered once per transaction, and the first 
notification should already trigger the applicable timelines. 
41. We believe parties should be kept better informed of the status of the filing and whether any non-
notified Member State has taken an interest in the transaction. Parties should be provided with 
the option (which should not be an obligation) to explain their investment case to all authorities 
reviewing the transaction, and not only the authority in the jurisdiction where the filing is triggered. 
Cooperation with third countries 
42. According to Article 13 of the FDI regulation, Member States and the Commission may cooperate 
with the responsible authorities of third countries on issues relating to the screening of foreign 
direct investments on grounds of security and public order.

43. While a certain level of coordination with third countries may facilitate the decision-making 
process, parties must be informed about such cooperation and potential implications on their

4. Administrative efficiency and best practices 
44. The time and cost associated with preparing for, and undergoing, FDI screening reviews can be a 
serious concern for parties. Unlike merger control, FDI screening laws frequently capture small to 
very small transactions, for example in early-stage high-tech companies. This means that the 
administrative effort and timelines associated with FDI screenings may impact the deal rationale 
and potentially even outweigh the benefits of the transaction in certain cases. 
Common filing forms and other administrative synergies 
45. We believe that the current EU FDI regulation process could greatly benefit from the 
standardization and streamlining of data collection. For example, consideration should be given to 
implementing a single, standardized filing form that can be used across the EU. This common filing 
form could collect all common data fields, e.g., information about the parties and the overall 
transaction. Member-state specific information, such as details about the company’s specific 
operations in the country or reputational information (e.g., criminal records), could be collected in 
a modular fashion using the same, straight-forward form. These sections could be left blank where 
they are not applicable.  
46. Where legally permissible under Member State laws, English should be adopted as the common 
filing language. This would eliminate the costs associated with translating filing materials into 
multiple European languages. 
Coherence with other regimes  
47. Global corporations and investors face increasing bureaucratic requirements when operating in 
the EU. The cumulative effect of multiple filing requirements – such as merger control, sector-
specific change-of-control notifications, FDI regulation, and potentially filings under the new 
European Foreign Subsidies Regulation – significantly adds to transaction timelines, transactions 
costs and uncertainty.   
48. Aligning FDI timelines with those of merger control could reduce delays and transaction costs. 
Unique aspects of the FDI review process, such as the necessary coordination with various subject 
matter expert ministries, might lead to extra time being needed in specific cases. However, we 
believe that FDI screening reviews should not per se take longer than merger control reviews.  
Trusted investor status 
49. The EC should evaluate introducing an option for investors to undergo a pre-screening outside of 
a specific transaction. Investors who have undergone such pre-screening successfully would only 
have to notify the competent authorities of their investment proposal but would not have to hold 
off closing the transaction subject to prior approval (this right to close immediately could possibly 
be made subject to certain conditions). Such system could be implemented by way of EU-wide 
exemption certificates that are valid for a certain period of time and that require the foreign 
investor to keep information about its ownership and governance structure up to date.

International cooperation on best practices 
50. We would encourage Member State authorities to share views on technical aspects, such as 
procedural best practices and the design of mitigation measures, at international fora or working 
groups. We are aware that certain fora for exchange already exist, such as the Trade and 
Technology Council (TTC) Investment Screening Working Group Meeting between the United 
States and the EU and certain working groups that operate at a national level.  
51. We believe that cooperation could be intensified, geographically widened, and made accessible 
for broader participation, including by companies principally affected by FDI regulation. This will 
help establish best practices and generally contribute to the development of FDI screening as an 
increasingly important policy area globally. 
Outbound investment review 
52. The EC has proposed a new European Economic Strategy, which includes strengthening screening 
processes for inbound investments but also emphasizes the control of outbound investments to 
prevent potential security risks, such as the compromise of technology and knowledge.4 We 
understand that this initiative is currently being reviewed together with an expert team and in 
collaboration with Member States, with the aim of finalising a more detailed proposal by the end 
of 2023. 
53. We encourage the EC as well as the Member States to gain a solid understanding of the issues first 
before engaging in rulemaking on aspects of outbound investments, given the potentially severe 
impact on cross-border investment and trade, the prospect of retaliatory measures, and the 
additional administrative burden for European companies. We are generally concerned about the 
rapid adoption of numerous trade and investment related policies without a comprehensive 
understanding of their individual impacts.  
54. Key questions that we believe need to be addressed prior to implementing a new screening regime: 
 What exactly is the national security concern arising from outbound investments?  
 If it is technology and know-how leakage to a foreign military complex, would a more 
dynamic and generally enhanced export control regime be able to address these concerns 
in a more targeted manner? 
 Is such screening mechanism a viable path to effectively monitor investment flows? How 
significant is the risk that such approach would lead to capital being redirected through 
funds in third countries and, consequently, decreased (not increased) influence over how 
capital “originating” from the EU is being deployed abroad?  What are the lessons from 
the monitoring of compliance with EU sanctions? 
 Rather than making outbound investments subject to government approval, could higher

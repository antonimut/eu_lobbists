Annex to EFPIA’s Response on the Compulsory Licensing Framework Proposal 
Author: EFPIA  Date: 28/07/2023  Version: FINAL  In its proposed Compulsory Licensing (“CL”) framework, the European Commission (“EC”) has taken a dramatic step in seeking to provide the basis for a new CL instrument, to be used as a “last-resort” in case of an EU-wide crisis.  Not only would such a Union-wide license significantly weaken innovator IP rights, but the framework itself represents a significant expansion of the role of the EC on a mandate that has long been firmly within the Member States’ remit, and where there already exist sufficient compulsory license provisions at both the international and Member State level.    Throughout the development of the proposal, EFPIA has actively worked to share our lessons from the COVID-19 crisis, so as to try to better inform the setting of any policy that would affect not only the pharmaceutical industry, but any industry whose goods would be identified as critical to resolving an EU crisis.  As an industry, we are committed to ensuring there is an appropriate framework in such a crisis and are committed to working with legislators to ensure such a framework exists.  Unfortunately, however, EFPIA believes there is considerable cause for concern not only with the substantive legislative provisions themselves, including their compliance with both EU Law and the EU’s international obligations under the TRIPS (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights) Agreement, but also with the absence of any compelling evidence which would justify the introduction of such a significant mechanism.   Baseline Considerations – Raison d’être  As a preliminary matter, the grant of a CL should be regarded as an absolute last resort, when all attempts at voluntary licensing have failed. This is for good reason: a CL significantly undermines the valid rights of an innovator and prevents innovators (who most fully understand the technology) from choosing the best-positioned, trusted partners to bring a given product to market within the shortest possible time frame.  In general, any unjustifiable willingness by authorities to employ compulsory licensing will undermine investors’ confidence in the future stability of the IP system and will harm the overall innovation pipeline. At the same time, it also impedes the speed of voluntary licensing measures to bring goods – in the case of the pharmaceutical industry, innovative medicines – to patients and the public in a time of crisis.  EFPIA expresses its concern, however, that outside of passing reference in the recitals to “exceptional circumstances,” there is little basis in the legislation proper to firmly contain CLs to measures of last resort.  Furthermore, the recent past experience from the COVID crisis is clear. Despite being arguably the most serious crisis faced by Europe in decades, there was no need for CLs. Instead, voluntary partnerships and licensing models permitted launch of COVID vaccines within a year and rapid scale-up of manufacturing capacity such that supply quickly exceeded demand. Challenges in delivery of vaccines were related to trade measures, e.g. India’s lengthy export ban on vaccines, or issues of logistics, uptake, and sufficient healthcare workers to enable efficient distribution of vaccines.  A CL would not have improved the outcome and, in fact, diversion of scarce company resources to support compulsory licences at that time could well have hampered, rather than helped, vaccine supply.    Given this context, it is disappointing to note that the Impact Assessment fails to provide any compelling evidence for the introduction of an EU-wide CL mechanism. Regulatory Scrutiny Board both for concrete examples of crisis situations which would have benefitted from an EU-wide CL, and a consideration of the possible impact on future R&D investment in a time of crisis.   Even more worryingly, the Explanatory Memorandum clearly expresses a disregard for the results of the related public consultation. EFPIA’s view is that the introduction of an EU-wide CL mechanism is simply not required, but that to the extent there is perceived to be a need for better harmonization, then this is better achieved through an EU-wide co-ordination mechanism. There appears to be no clear rationale as to why this approach has been rejected.  Overall, EFPIA is concerned about the global message this proposal sends, whilst already generating the wrong signals.  Despite the success of voluntary partnerships and licensing during the COVID pandemic, and despite providing almost no real-world evidence justifying the need for such a measure, this proposal from the EC posits another layer of compulsory licensing – this time at the Union level – in response.  Implicit in this policy choice is a tacit implication that IP was a barrier during the pandemic – which it was not – and that more must be done to overcome it.  At the global political level, the messaging inherent in this framework is already having negative, knock-on effects in the context of ongoing TRIPS Waiver discussions, pandemic preparedness negotiations, and on the overall international conversation around IP rights in general.  Incorrectly treating IP as a scapegoat, directly or indirectly, only impinges upon the global community’s ability to develop a thorough and innovative approach to address the actual, multifaceted issues which impede crisis response.   Legality of the Proposal  In addition to the lack of a need for an EU-wide CL, there are serious question-marks over the compatibility of the current proposals with both TRIPS and with well-established principles of EU law. These are set out in more detail below, but in summary:  TRIPS: The ability to ignore, with relative ease, (i) the identification of the rights-holder, (ii) the identification of relevant patents, (iii) the assessment of alternative solutions (such as voluntary licensing), and (iv) the need to negotiate with the rights-holder, is incompatible with Article 31(a) and (b) TRIPS. Furthermore, the ability to impose, without clear safeguards, open-ended “additional measures” on rights-holders, which could extend to the forced transfer of know-how, is incompatible with Article 39 TRIPS. Also, the extension of the framework to patent applications is incompatible with Article 31 TRIPS, which applies to the “subject matter of a patent,” and not applications.  Additionally, capping remuneration at 4%, and thereby divorcing this from a true assessment of economic value, is incompatible with Article 31(h) TRIPS. Finally, the absence of a clearly defined and thorough avenue of legal redress for such complex economic cases is incompatible with Article 31(i) and (j) TRIPS.  Principles of EU Law: The imposition of an EU-wide CL is a clear interference with the Right to Property under Article 17 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU. As such, any implementing legislation must include minimum safeguards designed to limit that interference to what is proportionate and strictly necessary. By failing to include such safeguards (for example by allowing CLs to be issued without the identification or negotiation with rights-holders, without the identification of the relevant patents, without the provision of adequate remuneration or rights of appeal, or by extending the CL/complementary measures to include know-how), this proposal breaches the principle of proportionality and is incompatible with Article 17, Article 41 and Article 52 of the Charter.

Therefore, it is EFPIA’s view that, as it currently stands, the EC’s proposed CL framework fails to comply not only with the EU’s obligations under TRIPS, but also the tenets of its own domestic EU law.  Rejection of a Coordination Mechanism  In the Executive Summary of the Impact Assessment Report, the EC acknowledges that the introduction of a coordinating role for the EC, as opposed to a decision-making role, was the preferred option among respondents. However, this preference appears to have been ignored on the basis that business and industry representatives “were the dominant group of respondents to the consultation.”   This is unfortunate, as in EFPIA’s view the use of a coordinating approach is much more appropriate than the current proposals for an EU-wide CL. Yet there appears no clear rationale as to why this approach was rejected, nor any compelling evidence or concrete examples showing that the CL provisions currently available to Member States at the national level are unworkable or insufficient.   In essence, an EU-wide coordination mechanism would utilize existing Member State CL practice – in whose remit CLs have traditionally been – and have the EC in a coordinating role, channeling communication and, where appropriate, evidence regarding the need for CL among Member States where a crisis may exist.  Such an approach would not only avoid the legal pitfalls facing the current proposal, but would also enable an agile response to crises that affect only a limited number of countries.  With the aid of the EC, Member State processes could be synchronized and channeled constructively, without the need to take the drastic step of imposing an EU-wide CL.  The Impact Assessment appears to reject this option without providing any clear rationale.  Substantive Framework Considerations  In looking at the proposed CL framework provisions broadly, there are some baseline expectations which are met in the proposal, such as: (1) the scope of a CL would be limited to “crisis-relevant products,” defined as products or processes that are indispensable for responding to a crisis or emergency or for addressing the impacts of a crisis or emergency in the Union; (2) certain safeguards re: labelling/packaging, quantities, territorial scope, etc. are provided for; and (3) the CL would be limited in time, and only applied following an EU-level decision activating a crisis mode or declaring an emergency under an existing EU crisis instrument.    However, these measures contain considerable limitations that prevent them from being sufficient safeguards; and these limitations raise significant concerns regarding the intention and direction of the legislation.  Particularly problematic substantive aspects of the framework include, at a glance:   (1) Lack of a truly clear definition of crisis, with limited references to other EU legislation. Given the likely significant impact of the grant of a CL on a rights-holder’s valid IP rights, it is critical that the circumstances which may lead to the imposition of a CL are clearly defined. The absence of such a clear definition inevitably leads to uncertainty for innovators, and brings the risk of extending implementation beyond genuine, pan-European crises requiring such a dramatic response;  (2) Ambiguity around when a rights-holder would be notified of a potential CL. The proposal appears to contain no, or at best minimum, safeguards which would ensure that a rights-holder is notified of the potential for a CL at an early stage. Yet these are the individuals/companies likely to be most seriously affected by such a decision, and are most likely to be able to positively contribute to a solution to the crisis. It is therefore crucially important that the rights-holder is engaged as early as practically possible;

(3) The decision to trigger the CL procedure appears to rest with the EC alone, and the role of any licensee is unclear. This contrasts with national laws, where the decision to impose a CL is made by national courts at the request of a licensee seeking the CL. In the absence of any willing licensee, it is difficult to envisage how the CL process could be made to work in practice. The presence of a willing licensee should therefore be a minimum requirement before any CL procedure can commence.  (4) Absence of any clear process allowing rights-holders to first negotiate voluntary licences with preferred partners, fundamentally undermining the best likely solution for any crisis situation. Voluntary agreements are the most suitable way of dealing with patented products or processes in a time of crisis. These take advantage of long-standing relationships with existing and trusted partners, and will inevitably lead to a more efficient and effective solution to the crisis, as highlighted during the Covid pandemic. Voluntary agreements must therefore be strongly encouraged, as without this, there is almost no incentive for a third party seeking a compulsory license to contemplate a voluntary license, which will inevitably hamper efforts to resolve the crisis. Yet the proposal is almost silent on this;  (5) The advisory body providing recommendations for CL issuance is intended to consult the rights-holder at some stage, but how its final recommendation is made, its effect, and the very composition of the body – including the competency and expertise of its members – is unclear. At the very least, the advisory body needs to have expertise in the field of patent law, but at present this is not proposed. Furthermore, there are very limited requirements on the need for the advisory body to engage with rights-holders, yet failure to engage early will inevitably delay the crisis response, given the expertise the rights-holder will be able to bring;  (6) Issued CLs can be overbroad and can automatically apply to all rights attached to a named product without needing to identify those rights, and even covering pending, and not granted rights. Given the potential economic impact of an EU-wide CL on a rights-holder, there need to be clear safeguards limiting the scope of such an imposition. As it currently stands, the breadth of these provisions merely increase business uncertainty;  (7) The Commission has been granted overly broad, vaguely defined powers for “complementary measures” to support a CL, which can extend to forced disclosure of highly confidential and commercially sensitive know-how and/or trade secrets. Allowing such apparently open-ended access to rights-holder’s confidential information is a wholesale disregard of fundamental IP rights. The very nature of trade secrets is such that their value is extinguished immediately on disclosure, and once a trade secret is out it is impossible to put the genie back into the bottle. Yet there are no apparent safeguards proposed in this legislation, leaving rights-holders exposed to the unacceptable risk of losing control of critical proprietary information. Additionally, one of the lessons of the Covid pandemic is how all-encompassing any efforts to respond to a crisis situation can be. If a rights-holder is required to devote significant resources to transferring know-how to an unknown third party, then this will have a hugely detrimental effect on the timeliness and effectiveness of the response, and on the ability of the rights-holder to continue to carry out day-to-day activities.  Finally, the selection of a qualified licensee to whom a CL is granted – one who can be expected to successfully produce goods to resolve a given crisis – should mean that any such knowledge transfer is unnecessary;  (8) Limited judicial review of a given CL is provided for in the text and under EU law, though precise avenues for redress, as well as its scope, are unclear. The provision of clear and substantive rights of appeal is critical to any fair process, yet beyond a reference to an appeal to the CJEU in relation to financial penalties, there is very little of substance in the proposal. This is unreasonable and the

rights-holder must be given express rights of appeal to the General Court and/or the CJEU including a full review of the facts on which the decision to issue a CL is based, not limited to points of law, together with adequate rights of injunctive relief and compensation, throughout the CL procedure;  (9) The posited adequate remuneration seems capped at a level too low to truly be considered “adequate,” while penalties for a rights-holder failing to comply/cooperate are capped at a level far, far higher than any possible remuneration – exceeding even the level of penalties provided for breaches of the General Pharmaceutical Legislation.  Given this context, where a third-party is faced with the prospect of (i) a very low maximum level of payment to a rights-holder, (ii) the possibility of being able to demand mandatory access to highly confidential and sensitive know-how, and (iii) knowledge that the rights-holder faces the uncertainty of ill-defined financial penalties, there is simply no incentive to seek a voluntary license. This will have a hugely damaging impact on the response to any crisis.  Overall, the EC’s proposed CL framework contains many unclarities which raise a multitude of concerns regarding the ultimate implementation of its provisions and the purposes to which it could be turned. This lack of clarity renders these provisions totally impractical, and more likely to harm, rather than help, any response to a serious crisis.  In addition, as also briefly discussed above, the CL framework proposals as currently presented are incompatible with both TRIPS and with general principles of EU Law. Dealing with each in turn:  1 Non-Compliance with TRIPS  The EU CL framework, as proposed, is inconsistent with TRIPS obligations with respect to protection of patents, trade secrets, and RDP: it limits the protection of these IP rights without complying with the relevant requirements of Articles 31 and 39 of TRIPS.  Article 31(a) TRIPS – “Individual Merits” Assessment Article 31(a) TRIPS requires an assessment of the need for a CL “on its individual merits”.  The proposed CL framework fails this requirement: it does not require the identification of a given IP right in the interest of expedience (Art. 8.1(a)), and it does not require notification of the affected rights-holder if they cannot be “identified with reasonable efforts” (Art. 8.1(b)).  This fails to meet the efforts required by TRIPS to thoroughly analyze each issue case-by-case to ensure use of a CL only as a remedy of last resort.  Article 31(b) TRIPS – Requirement of Prior Voluntary Negotiations Under Article 31(b) TRIPS, the “proposed user must make efforts to obtain authorization from the rights holder on reasonable commercial terms and conditions.”  The requirement to negotiate may be waived only “in the case of a national emergency or other circumstances of extreme urgency.”  In that case, the rights holder shall be notified “as soon as practicable.”   The proposed EU CL framework does not align with this system.    First, there is no “proposed user” or any requirement for any user to apply for a CL.  Instead, the Commission takes the role of initiator. Second, and most critically, the EU CL contains no default obligation for any proposed user to negotiate a voluntary licence before applying for a CL.  This is fundamentally incompatible with Article 31(b) TRIPS, and cannot be remedied by the provision of a chance to “comment on the possibility to reach a voluntary licensing agreement” which, moreover, comes close to the end of the EU CL procedure.  Third, the EU CL framework envisages the involvement of the rights-holder only when discussions in the advisory body have reached an advanced stage. This is clearly too late. By contrast, in case of an emergency waiver of the obligation to negotiate, Article 31(b) 
   
 www.efpia.eu          6  
TRIPS still requires the right holder to be notified “as soon as reasonably practicable”.  There is no corresponding obligation in the EU CL.  Articles 31(h) and 31(i) TRIPS – Judicial Review Articles 31(h) read in conjunction with 31(i) TRIPS requires Members to provide judicial review for decisions relating to the issuance of CLs and remuneration to the patent holder.  Under the EU CL framework, the ‘advisory body’ will take several procedural steps, unlikely to be reviewable by the EU courts.  And when the Commission issues a CL, it would do so in the form of an “implementing decision” – in the same way that a marketing authorisation, or an orphan designation, is granted by the Commission.  Although a Commission decision granting an EU CL will be reviewable by the General Court, it is not clear that there would be a full review of the factors leading to that decision. To the extent that the review fails to deal with such substantive issues, the Court’s practice would deny right holders’ effective legal remedies.  In addition, the EU CL framework does not assess or even mention the need for protection in the form of injunctive relief against CL decisions that are incompatible with EU law.   The right to request such relief appears all the more necessary if the EU CL permits the forced transfer of secret know-how and trade secrets, which are extinguished the moment they are disclosed or transferred (see below).  Article 31 TRIPS – Application to Patents, not Patent Applications Article 31 TRIPS applies to the “subject matter of a patent,” and does not apply to patent applications. Given that patent applications contain claims which are likely to be revised during the patent prosecution process, then it will not be clear at the time any crisis is declared whether a license to a patent application will be required. And given that any CL must be time-limited, it is difficult to foresee any situation where a license to such patent applications is genuinely needed. In the absence of any clear contrary evidence, the decision to extend the reach of a CL beyond granted patents to patent applications is disproportionate.   Article 39 TRIPS – Protection of Trade Secrets and RDP Pursuant to Article 39.1 of TRIPS, Members must ensure effective protection for trade secrets (“undisclosed information”) and for data submitted to obtain a marketing authorisation (RDP).  Members must respect minimum standards of protection preventing disclosure, acquisition, and use of trade secrets without the consent of persons in lawful control in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices (TRIPS Art 39.2). Members must also protect data submitted for the marketing authorisation of pharmaceutical products from “unfair commercial use” and “disclosure“ (TRIPS Art. 39.3).    As currently phrased in Article 14(2) of the EU CL framework, “additional measures complementing the Union compulsory license” could encompass the mandatory transfer of confidential information, know-how or other trade secrets. Indeed, the Impact Assessment explicitly states on page 13 that “for complex products such as vaccines, access to trade secrets or secret know-how would be needed on top of patents”, and Footnote 89 requires “…in addition to the permission to use the invention, the transfer of know-how including skills, abilities and knowledge”.   Such compulsory transfer of know-how/trade secrets is incompatible with Article 39 TRIPS, which recognises trade secrets as IP rights in their own category. The importance of safeguards for trade secrets is particularly important given that, by their very nature, and in contrast to other IP rights, their value is extinguished immediately on disclosure. In failing to include any such safeguards, the EU CL framework clearly fails to comply with TRIPS.  With respect to RDP, the proposal as currently drafted envisages the automatic suspension of RDP without any apparent safeguards. Thus, there is no requirement in the framework to seek the consent of

the marketing authorisation holder (a less onerous and more proportionate solution), nor to negotiate or impose fair compensation for the RDP.  Indeed, there is no provision to even contact the MA holder, or give them the right to be heard before a CL is imposed with the automatic effect of suspending RDP.  The mechanism lacks nuance and fails to guard against unfair commercial use contrary to Article 39.3.     Article 31(h) TRIPS – Adequate Remuneration Finally, Article 31(h) TRIPS requires Members to ensure that, upon granting a CL, adequate remuneration is paid to the patent owner, “in the circumstance of each case, taking into account the economic value of the authorization.”  The proposed CL framework does not fulfill these requirements, as the amount payable to a licensee is capped at 4% - a level set for developing countries - and does not consider the actual value of the patent within the EU under ordinary circumstances.  Furthermore, no compensation is foreseen for forcibly transferred technology, or for the use of data still protected by RDP.  The EU CL also lacks the counterbalancing obligation required by TRIPS - i.e., the obligation of the proposed licensee to negotiate in good faith first. The inclusion of a capped remuneration will hinder this and risks pushing the remuneration downwards.  This is only exacerbated by Article 8(2) of the proposal, which permits remuneration to be determined unilaterally by the Commission after the granting of a CL "where that determination requires further investigation and consultation.”    2.  Non-compliance with the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (“Charter”)  Any limitations on the exercise of the rights and freedoms set out within the Charter are only permitted if they comply with “the principle of proportionality” and are “necessary and genuinely meet objectives of general interest” (Article 52(1)).  It is clear from the most recent case law on personal data that, in order to satisfy the requirement of proportionality, an EU act which permits an interference with such fundamental rights must itself define the scope of that interference, must lay down clear and precise rules governing the scope, and must define minimum safeguards in clear and precise terms (see Case C-311/18).  Since the EU CL will precipitate serious interference with the right to property (including under Article 17 of the Charter), the EU CL framework must itself contain all the restrictions and safeguards necessary to ensure that the interference is limited to what is strictly necessary.  However, as already outlined, many aspects of the CL procedure are left open, without detailed process, and without clear safeguards, in clear breach of the principle of proportionality.  The proposed CL framework simply fails to limit the interference with fundamental rights to that which is proportionate and strictly necessary.  Article 17 – Proportionality, Protection of Intellectual Property and Fair Compensation  Article 17(1) of the Charter confirms that “[n]o one may be deprived of his or her possessions, except in the public interest and in the cases and under the conditions provided for by law, subject to fair compensation being paid in good time for their loss…”, and Article 17(2) mandates that “Intellectual property shall be protected”.  RDP – erroneously characterised as “rules” rather than ‘rights’ in the EU CL impact assessment – is a distinct form of IP and is recognized as such in TRIPS.  Trade secrets and know-how are acknowledged within the Impact Assessment itself as “intangible assets” and together with inventions are the “cornerstone of the EU economy and competitiveness.”  There is no doubt, then, that the proposed framework constitutes an “interference” with the rights granted by Article 17 of the Charter.    Similarly, to the extent the CL includes “measures complementing the compulsory license” and requirements to act in “good faith” – to be understood as forced transfer of confidential know-how and trade secrets, those secrets are effectively extinguished once they are disclosed.  Unlike patents, which serve as compensation for disclosure of an invention, the impact of the CL is even more draconian for holders of these rights, and yet they are not consulted or compensated.  Overall, the curtailment of RDP, know-how, and trade secrets rights, combined with the absence of suitable safeguards (see also the discussion on Article 52 of the Charter above) constitutes a breach of the principle of proportionality   
   
 www.efpia.eu          8  
 Article 17 of the Charter permits the deprivation of possessions in the public interest, but only under conditions provided for by law, and subject to “fair compensation.”  Article 9(2) of the proposed CL framework infringes the obligation to provide “fair compensation.”  For RDP and trade secrets, no compensation is foreseen at all.  For patents, as previously described, the 4% cap means that the requirement to provide fair compensation is not met.  Finally, it should be noted that the penalties for non-compliance with a CL implementing decision (6% of annual turnover of the rights holder) are much larger than the royalties to be gained by the rights holder (4% of the licensee’s turnover, where the licensee could be operating at much lower cost).  The 4% cap therefore breaches the principles of proportionality and equal treatment (different situations should be treated differently in this instance).  Article 41 – The Right to be Heard Article 41 of the Charter provides the right of every person to be heard, and the proposed CL framework falls woefully short of this standard.  First, the rights holder is merely provided with the “opportunity to comment” with no obligation for the Commission to take these comments into consideration.  There should be an express right to be heard on every aspect of the CL procedure.  Second, the rights holder is only involved (and therefore only obtains an opportunity to comment) “once there are ongoing advanced discussions” possibly a short time before the granting of the CL. The rights-holder would therefore be deprived of the opportunity to intervene in at an earlier stage, before deliberation and before an opinion is formed on the need for a CL. The rights-holder should be consulted first and foremost.    Third, the rights holder is not expressly granted a right of access to the file, thereby depriving the rights-holder with the opportunity to effectively defend their rights before a CL is granted.  Finally, the right to be heard is drastically infringed where the advisory body and/or the Commission proceeds without identifying the rights-holder.    Article 47 – Effective Remedy before a Tribunal Article 47 of the Charter provides the right to an effective remedy before a tribunal where that person’s rights and freedoms are violated, and the entitlement to a fair and public hearing within a reasonable time by an independent and impartial tribunal previously established by law.  The review of the General Court is “restricted” and does not cover a full analysis comparable to, say, the re-examination of a CHMP Opinion assessing the safety and efficacy of a medicinal product, thereby depriving the aggrieved party of its right to a fair trial.  Article 49 – Clear Definition of Infringements Article 49 of the Charter is violated because the CL framework does not clearly define the infringements attracting penalties. The principle of legality requires a strict, unambiguous definition of infringements allowing individuals to know with a reasonable degree of foreseeability the acts and omissions which will give rise to liability.  The definition of some of the infringements in Article 15 of the framework manifestly fails to meet such a requirement. On the basis of the provision’s wording, it is not clear what acts or omissions would make a rights-holder liable.  The principle of legality also requires that provisions that create infringements and penalties be adopted by the legislator, i.e. the European Parliament and the Council. Any argument that the infringements contemplated by the proposed regulation are merely administrative is unfounded in light of the massive fines that may be imposed: 6% of the total turnover in the preceding business year.  Article 290 TFEU – Clear Delineation of Powers Finally, the EU CL is inconsistent with Article 290 TFEU because the powers granted to the Commission are not precisely delineated.  Regulatory powers that are made available to the Commission by EU legislative acts must, in order to be compatible with the TFEU, be “precisely delineated and amenable to 
   
 www.efpia.eu          9  
judicial review in light of the objectives established by the delegating authority.”  The proposed regulation would empower the Commission to include in a CL undefined “measures complementing the compulsory licence, which are necessary to achieve the objective of the compulsory licence”( Art. 8(1)(h)), without defining the nature and the scope of such measures.  This article would clearly not withstand the test and would be incompatible with EU law.  Moreover, the proposed regulation would empower the Commission to impose on the licensee or the rights-holder fines for failure “to comply with the principle of good faith and cooperation referred to in Article 13”, which article also requires the rights-holder and the licensee to “make their best efforts to fulfil the objective of the Union compulsory licence.”  Such general and vague descriptions in Article 15 of the infringements for which fining powers are given to the Commission also run afoul of the principles of EU law here delineated.  Summary on the legality of the proposal: In short, the EU CL framework as currently proposed is incompatible with both TRIPS and with the EU Charter. And these are not minor breaches, which are readily rectifiable, but instead go to the very heart of the proposals themselves.  A Final Reflection  As this proposal is discussed further throughout the legislative process, EFPIA urges the EC, Parliament, and Council to engage in meaningful discussion with all stakeholders – including industry, who are the rights-holders that will inevitably be best placed to respond to any crisis, and yet most affected by such legislation.  The enacting of such a framework, which will have the serious effects of significantly weakening IP rights in the EU, calls for serious reflection: not only on the ultimate purpose of and need for the legislation – in light of the lessons learned from the COVID crisis and the success of the voluntary licensing model – but also the framework’s compliance with the EU’s existing international obligations, its compliance with TRIPS, and with fundamental EU law.  Instead, an EU-wide coordination mechanism, as preferred by the public consultation, would not only avoid the serious legal challenges facing the current proposal but also respect the remit of the Member States in issuing CLs, providing an efficient, carefully-delimited solution that utilizes existing legal mechanisms to address arising crises.  EFPIA welcomes the opportunity to work with legislators to minimize vagueness and risk of extension beyond its purported intent, ensuring that such measures would only be taken as an absolute last resort, in the context of a clearly defined crisis, and in the very unlikely event that voluntary measures would not yield a better outcome.

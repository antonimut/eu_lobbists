On behalf of the Japan Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association (JPMA) Intellectual 
Property Committee, we hereby provide our public comments on the proposal for a 
REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN P ARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL on 
compulsory licensing for crisis management and amending Regulation (EC) 816/2006 -
COM(2023)224 (“Proposed Regulation”). 
 
1. Introduction 
 
JPMA is a voluntary association comprising 72 research and development oriented 
pharmaceutical companies (as of May 1, 2023). With a mission of “realization of patient -
oriented healthcare”, JPMA established in 1968 has been contributing to global healthcare 
through the development of innovative new drugs for medical pharmaceuticals. JPMA is 
engaged with various initiatives such as activities for solving common issues to the 
pharmaceutical industry and deepening the idea of pharmaceuticals, and international 
cooperation. Especially, JPMA has been engaged in sound development of the pharmaceutical 
industry, by strengthening policy formulation and recommendation activities, responding to 
globalization, and strengthening public relations. JPMA's Intellectual Property Committee 
has been engaged in policy formulation for the systemization and improvement of intellectual 
property systems and policy recommendation to the related government agencies and 
organizations etc. in order to achieve sound development of the pharmaceutical industry. 
 
JPMA is concerned that the Proposed Regulation sends incorrect messages to people around 
the world that intellectual property rights are obstacle to provide new medical products and

2. Granting compulsory licen ces adversely affects to middle and long -term innovations and 
new medical developments 
 
The intellectual property system is designed to foster innovation s and incentivize research 
and development. Granting compulsory licen ces allows others to use a patented invention 
without the consent of the inventor or fair compensation, significantly d iminishing the 
motivation for inventors and companies to invest their tim e, resources, and expertise in 
developing new products and processes. This reduction in incentives could disrupt the 
pharmaceutical ecosystem that has generated numerous groundbreaking new medicines and 
significantly hinder ability to address future crises and emergencies. 
 
3. Granting compulsory licences is not an effective solution on the crises or emergencies 
 
1) Voluntary licences or other cooperation should be pursed instead of compulsory licences 
 
Voluntary cooperation, negotiations, and licensing agreements between patent holders and 
relevant parties often yield mutually beneficial outcomes. Governments and international 
organizations should focus on facilitating such negotiations and promoting voluntary licenses, 
cooperative partnerships, and support for technology transfer as more effective alternatives, 
rather than relying on compulsory licen ces. We appreciate that the Proposed Regulation 
recognizes “voluntary agreements are the most adequate, qu ick, and efficient solution”  
(Preamble (1)). 
 
During COVID -19 pandemic, intellectual property rights support development and 
production increase of safe and effective vaccines at an unprecedented high speed. COVID-
19 vaccine production by pharmaceutical c ompanies in developing and developed countries

reached to about a billion doses per month in May 2021, less than six months after the first 
vaccine was approved 1. The intellectual property frameworks have provided the foundation 
for establishing more than 380 voluntary partnerships for COVID-19 vaccines (88% of which 
involve technology transfer) in a record short time. Making the granting of compulsory 
licences easier leads to disassembly of the intellectual property frameworks, which have 
provided solutions to cope with COVID-19 and have promoted an unprecedented number of 
partnerships, voluntary licensing, and knowledge sharing during the pandemic, and has a 
negative spreading effect on future similar frameworks.  Once again, it should be reaffirmed 
that the incentives provided by the intellectual property system have supported technological 
development for many years, that the accumulation of such basic technologies have led to the 
success of these mRNA vaccines and other products, and that the intellectual property system 
has a role in contributing to innovations by allowing superior technologies to be made public 
and accumulating another technical improvement on them. T hese activities should not be 
impeded in order to prepare for the next pandemic. 
 
2) Issues of access to medicine is not a matter of intellectual property rights 
 
In order to  significantly improve access to medicine on the crises or emergencies , it is 
important to focus on its true problems. For instance, during COVID-19 pandemic, the issue 
of vaccines was not vaccine production shortages but how to deliver vaccines to people who 
need them. Its immediate and essential issues were elimination of trade/regulatory barriers, 
enhancement of medical personnel in l ow/lower-middle income countries, improvement of 
public awareness to healthcare  including the prevention of vaccine hesitancy and 
dissemination of misinformation on vaccines due to lack of medical information or healthcare 
education, and improvement of distribution processes. In other words,  intellectual property 
rights did not inhibit access to vaccines at all, and compulsory licence s d id not provide 
solutions to these underlying issues. In sum, compulsory licensing will not help for access to 
medicines when there are causes other than patents. Therefore, compulsory licensing should 
be considered as a last resort. 
 
4. Compulsory licensing should be limited to the minimum necessary 
 
Compulsory licensing should be a last resort and should only be applied in extremely limited

and necessary circumstances. This includes cases where alternative means or approaches are 
insufficient and when it is essential to protect urgent and significant public interests. We 
welcome the statement in the Preamble (16), (40) of the Proposed Regulation that Union 
compulsory licen ces must only be granted exceptional circumstances.  Under crisis or 
emergency, however, Union compulsory licences should be granted only if voluntary licenses 
are not achievable and if it can be demonstrated that Union compulsory licences are effective 
in addressing the crisis or emergency. In other words, even if compulsory licensing is 
necessary to enable timely supply of essential products to address th e crisis and the licensee 
can demonstrate sufficient capacity to supply such products in the required quantities and 
quality, it should be noted that the compulsory licensing is not necessarily able to protect 
urgent and significant public interests. As di scussed above, the issue of access to medicines 
often involves many factors beyond intellectual property rights. Therefore, Union compulsory 
licences should not be granted hastily when it cannot provide an immediate solution. 
 
We welcome that the Proposed Regulation makes clear the purpose, duration, and territorial 
limitations of Union compulsory licences (Preamble (16), Art. 5), the prohibition of exports 
(Preamble (29), Art. 11), the deliberation on the necessity of Union compulsory licences (Art. 
7(1), (2)), and the obligations of the licensee (Art. 10). However, it is unclear under what 
circumstances Union compulsory licences would be applied although there is a definition for 
"crisis and emergency" . Therefore,  we request that decisions regarding the granting and 
termination of Union compulsory licences are made based on fair and objective standards (Art. 
4). Additionally, consideration should be given to whether Union compulsory licences can 
effectively and promptly resolve crises or emergencies (Art. 7(1), (2)).  
 
5. The rights-holder should not be required to disclose trade secrets 
 
JPMA has serious concerns about the trade secret misappropriations potentially caused by  
Union compulsory licen ces. According to the proposed language of Art. 14(2), “the 
Commission shall decide upon reasoned request by the rights-holder or the licensee or on its 
own initiative on additional measures complementing the Union compulsory licence to ensure 
it achieves its objective as well as to facilitate and ensure the good collaboration between the 
rights-holder and the licensee .” (emphasis added). Preamble (32) also explains that “ such 
additional measures may include requesting further information which is deemed 
indispensable to achieve the objective of the compulsory licence .” (emphasis added ). 
Furthermore, upon a breach of such obligation, the rights -holder would be subject to “fines

not exceeding 6 % of their respective total turnover in the preceding business year”  (Art. 
15(1)(c)), or “periodic penalty payments not exceeding 5 % of their respective average daily 
turnover in the preceding business year per day” (Art. 16(1)(c)). 
Despite the proposed language of  Art. 2(1) which clearly limits the scope of intellectual 
property rights subject to the Union compulsory licensing to patents (including published 
patent applications), utility models and supplementary protection certificates, the ambiguous 
and overly broad requirements for “further information” potentially extend to the mandatory 
disclosure of trade secrets including know -how for manufacturing the innovate medicinal 
products. Since trade secrets cannot be recovered once they  have been misappropriated, it 
should b e clarified that the rights-holder will not be required to disclose or publi sh trade 
secrets or any other intellectual property rights beyond the scope as explicitly required by Art. 
2(1). It is also clear from this that voluntary licensing that allows for the selection of partners 
with the technical capabilities to produce effective and safe products  and can protect trade 
secrets by taking appropriate measures, is far preferable to compulsory licensing. 
 
6. The protection of rights-holders and the transparency and fairness of legal procedures for 
compulsory licensing should be ensured 
 
The procedures regarding the granting of compulsory licence s should uphold transparency 
and fairness. It is crucial to properly incorporate the rights-holder's opinions and procedures 
for filing objections. 
 
We welcome that the Proposed Regulation states mandating that products manufactured 
under Union compulsory licences should be distinguished from products of rights-holders or 
voluntary licenses (Preamble (26), Art. 10(1)), the opportunity for rights-holders to express 
their opinions (Preamble (20), (22), Art. 7(3)) and notifying right s-holders the potential 
grant of Union compulsory licen ces (Preamble (21), Art. 7(4) ). However, it is unclear 
regarding the sufficient duration for rights-holders to submit their opinions and the 
availability of remedies for challenging decisions. Therefore, we request clarification on these 
matters. 
 
In addition, even if Union compulsory licences are granted, intellectual property rights for 
which protection is provided by the TRIPS Agreement and other agreements should be duly 
protected, and the rights granted to a rights-holder of the patent for which Union compulsory 
licences are granted should also be properly protected.

7. Conclusion 
 
As discussed above, the intellectual property system is a crucial element that promotes 
innovations and supports advancements in medical technology, and it does not hinder access 
to medicines during cri ses or emergencies. Moreover, compulsory licensing could suppress 
incentives for long-term investments and new medical development; hence, we are concerned 
that compulsory licensing does not aid in future crises or emergencies and also has negative 
effects on it.
